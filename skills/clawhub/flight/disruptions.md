# When It Breaks — Delays, Cancellations, Bumping

Scope: the two tracks that must be run in parallel and never confused — **getting there**, which is urgent, and **getting paid**, which is not. Money back for an unflown ticket is `refunds.md`; a missing bag is `baggage.md`.

**Before advising**, read `~/Clawic/data/bookings/<year>.md` for the affected ticket's locator, carrier and fare rules, and `## Claims` in `memory.md` in case there is an open claim against the same carrier.

## The First Twenty Minutes

Order matters, because the good seats on the alternative go first.

1. **Find the alternatives yourself** before speaking to anyone: same carrier, partners, other airports in the same city, and surface transport where it is genuinely competitive. Arriving with a specific request ("put me on the 19:40 via Lisbon, there are four seats in economy") is what gets it done.
2. **Work two channels at once**: the app or website rebooking screen, and the queue or phone. Whichever completes first wins; abandon the other.
3. **Phone a different country's office** of the same airline if the home line is saturated. It is the same reservation system.
4. **Keep the original ticket alive.** Do not cancel to buy something new until the replacement is confirmed — cancelling voluntarily converts an involuntary disruption, where the airline owes rerouting, into a voluntary change where it owes nothing.
5. **Collect evidence now**: photograph the departure board, save the airline's own SMS or email stating the delay and its reason, and note the actual arrival time later. Compensation turns on arrival time, and boards are edited retroactively.
6. **Do not accept a voucher as a settlement.** Accepting compensation "in goodwill" can extinguish a statutory claim worth several times more. Meals and hotel are duty of care and are accepted freely; anything described as compensation or final settlement is not.

## Rerouting

- Under most regimes the passenger chooses between **rerouting at the earliest opportunity** and **a refund of the unused portion**, and the airline does not get to pick for them.
- "Earliest opportunity" includes other carriers. Airlines resist this; it is a matter of policy for them and, in several jurisdictions, of law. Ask explicitly for endorsement to another carrier.
- Rerouting to a nearby airport is normally allowed, with the airline covering the surface transfer to the original destination.
- If the airline refuses to reroute at all and you buy your own replacement, keep the receipt and claim it — but say clearly that self-help is the fallback, not the first move, and that recovery is not guaranteed for an unreasonable purchase (a last-minute business-class ticket when economy existed).
- On two tickets there is no rerouting obligation at all for the second leg (`connections.md`).

## Duty Of Care

Owed under EU/UK rules **regardless of the cause**, including weather and strikes, once the delay crosses defined waiting thresholds: meals and refreshments proportionate to the wait, two communications, and hotel accommodation with transfers when an overnight is required.

- Airlines rarely volunteer it. Ask, and if nothing is provided, buy reasonable equivalents and keep the receipts — reimbursement of reasonable care costs is the most reliably recovered category in the whole domain.
- "Reasonable" means a normal hotel and normal meals, not a suite and a tasting menu.
- The US has no federal duty-of-care right; what exists is each airline's own commitment, which several now publish and which regulators track. Check the specific carrier's published commitment rather than assuming.

## Compensation Regimes

Verify amounts and thresholds before promising money: these rules are amended, and the EU regime has been under active revision with proposals to change both the hour thresholds and the amounts.

| Regime | Applies to | Trigger | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| EU261 | Departures from an EU airport on any carrier; arrivals into the EU on an EU carrier | Arrival 3+ hours late, cancellation notified under 14 days ahead, denied boarding | €250 up to 1,500 km · €400 for intra-EU over 1,500 km and other 1,500-3,500 km · €600 over 3,500 km; halved when the rerouting arrives within a defined margin |
| UK261 | UK-anchored, same structure | Same | £220 / £350 / £520 |
| US DOT | Flights to, from and within the US | No compensation for delay. Cancellations and significant changes give an automatic cash refund if you do not accept the alternative | Refund; denied boarding pays a multiple of the one-way fare, capped |
| Canada APPR | Flights to, from and within Canada | Tiered by delay length, with different amounts for large and small carriers, and a cause test | Set by regulation |
| Brazil, Turkey, India, Israel and others | Their own domestic regimes | Varies | Varies |
| Everywhere else | The carrier's conditions of carriage | Varies | Often only a goodwill gesture |

Two mechanics that decide most cases: compensation is measured by **arrival delay at the final destination on the ticket**, not departure delay, so a late departure that makes up time pays nothing and a missed connection on one ticket pays on the whole journey. And distance is measured **great-circle from origin to final destination**, not per segment.

## Extraordinary Circumstances

The carrier's defence, and where most refusals live. Weather, air-traffic control restrictions, security incidents, political instability and third-party strikes generally qualify. **Technical faults generally do not** — routine maintenance and unexpected component failure have repeatedly been held to be inherent to running an airline. Crew shortage and the airline's own staff strike generally do not qualify either.

Knock-on effects are contested: an aircraft delayed by genuine weather earlier in the day may excuse a later rotation only if the airline shows it took all reasonable measures. Ask the airline to state the cause in writing; a vague "operational reasons" is not a defence and is worth quoting back.

## Denied Boarding

- **Volunteering is a negotiation.** The airline's first offer is never its last, and there is no obligation to accept the first alternative flight offered either. Ask for: the amount in cash rather than a voucher, confirmed seats on a specific flight, a lounge pass, meals, and a hotel if overnight. Get it in writing before surrendering the boarding pass.
- **Involuntary denied boarding** triggers statutory compensation in the EU/UK regimes and a fare-multiple payment in the US, plus rerouting and care. It is not a voucher unless you agree to a voucher.
- Being denied boarding for missing documents, arriving after the cut-off, or being unfit to fly is not compensable — it is the passenger's obligation (`documents.md`).
- Overbooking is legal nearly everywhere; the compensation is the mechanism that prices it.

## Downgrade

Involuntary downgrade to a lower cabin gives a **reimbursement of a proportion of the ticket price for the affected segment** under the EU/UK regimes — a percentage that steps with distance — and it is calculated on the ticket price, not the fare difference the airline would like to refund. It is separate from delay compensation, and can be claimed alongside it. Elsewhere, it is a fare-difference refund at best; ask for the cabin's cash difference and escalate with the ticket receipt.

## Schedule Changes

Made by the airline in advance, and much more generous than people assume:

- A change beyond the carrier's own significance threshold — commonly in the 60 to 120 minute range, and defined in law for US-anchored tickets at 3 hours domestic and 6 hours international — unlocks a free change or a full refund **even on a non-refundable fare**.
- The refund right survives a fare that says "non-refundable", because the airline changed the contract.
- Free rebooking on a schedule change often extends to different dates and nearby airports. Ask for what you want; the first offer is the algorithm's.
- A change of aircraft type invalidates seat assignments and can invalidate the reason you chose that flight (`seats.md`).
- Watch for silent changes: airlines reshuffle schedules months out and notify by email that looks like marketing. Re-check any booking older than three months against the current timetable.

## Making The Claim

1. Establish the facts: flight number, date, scheduled and actual arrival times, cause as stated by the carrier, and distance band.
2. Claim directly with the airline first, in writing, in one page: what happened, which regulation, which amount, bank details for payment, and the evidence attached. Bank details go to the airline, never into `~/Clawic/data/`.
3. Expect a first refusal citing extraordinary circumstances. Ask for the specific cause and the evidence for it.
4. Claim-management companies take 25-40% for work that is mostly this letter. They are worth it only when the airline has already refused twice or the jurisdiction is hostile.
5. **Open a row in `## Claims` in `memory.md`** the day the disruption happens: date, flight, what happened, basis, amount with currency, reference, status, and the deadline. Put the deadline in `## Due` too — claims are time-barred by national limitation periods ranging from about one year to six, and the limitation runs from the flight, not from the refusal.

## Escalation

- **National enforcement body** in the country of departure (and of arrival, for EU-carrier inbound flights). Free, slow, and effective in aggregate.
- **Alternative dispute resolution schemes** where the airline subscribes; binding on the airline, usually free to the passenger.
- **Small claims court** for amounts within its limit: cheap, no lawyer needed, and airlines frequently settle before the hearing.
- **Chargeback** is not the tool for compensation; it is the tool for services not delivered and for insolvency (`refunds.md`).
- **When a claim is finally paid, and the letter or argument that did it was non-obvious**, save it as `~/Clawic/data/flight/artifacts/claim-letter-<regime>.md` with its `## Boxes` line — the same airline refuses the same way twice, and the second claim then takes ten minutes.
