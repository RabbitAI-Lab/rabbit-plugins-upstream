## Whose Meeting It Is

**The report owns the agenda; the manager brings exactly one thing.** This single rule fixes most 1-on-1 pathologies, because the default gravity of the slot is toward the manager's status questions, and status is the one thing that could have been written.

- **Status goes async, before the meeting.** If the manager needs project state, they read it beforehand; the slot is for what writing cannot carry — blockers, judgement calls, friction, ambition, and the things people only say out loud.
- **A shared running doc, owned by the report**, is the mechanism: they add topics during the week, the manager adds theirs at the bottom, both see it before. It also means the meeting starts without the ninety seconds of "so… what's up?"
- **The manager's one thing** is feedback, context the report is missing, or a decision they need to weigh in on — not three things, because three things is the manager's agenda wearing the report's name.
- **Silence is the report's to fill.** A manager who fills the first pause has converted the meeting into a broadcast; hold it (`facilitation.md` on wait time).
- **Never cancel two in a row.** The report reads a cancellation as a ranking, and the honest topics stop arriving well before the complaints do. Shorten to 15 minutes instead of moving it.

## Cadence And Length

| Situation | Cadence | Length |
|---|---|---|
| New hire, first 90 days | Weekly, plus an unscheduled check in week one | 30-45 min |
| Steady state, experienced report | Weekly or biweekly | 30 min |
| Someone in a new or stretched role | Weekly, non-negotiable | 30-45 min |
| Remote report, no hallway contact | Weekly, camera on | 30 min |
| Peer or cross-functional partner | Monthly | 25 min |
| Skip-level | Quarterly per person | 30 min |
| Performance concern in progress | Weekly, documented | 30 min |

- **Frequency tracks how new the work is to the person, not seniority.** Grove's argument in *High Output Management* is that the interval should shorten when the person's task-relevant maturity is low; a senior engineer on a first management assignment needs the new-hire cadence.
- **Grove also argued for at least an hour**, on the grounds that short slots surface only easy topics — people raise the hard thing at minute 22, not minute 4. The practical compromise: 30 minutes weekly beats 60 monthly, but never let 30 become 15 by starting late.
- **More than 8 direct reports and weekly 1-on-1s do not fit** alongside the rest of the job. That is a span-of-control problem the calendar cannot solve; biweekly for the steady ones is the honest answer.

## The Shape

30 minutes, roughly: their topics 15 · your one thing 7 · open loops and next steps 8. The proportions matter more than the numbers — the moment the manager's segment is the largest, the meeting has flipped.

1. **Open with a real question, then stop talking.** "What's on your list?" beats "how's the project going", which invites status.
2. **Their topics, in their order.** Resist re-prioritising them; the order is information about what is actually bothering them.
3. **Your one thing**, framed as what it is: feedback, context, or a decision you need their view on.
4. **Close the loops.** Anything you owe them, anything they owe you, both with dates — into the same ledger as every other action item (`follow-through.md`).
5. **End with what changed.** "So this week you're unblocking X with Priya, and I'm getting you the headcount answer by Thursday."

## The Question Bank

Rotate; asking the same three every week trains a scripted answer. The good ones are specific and hard to answer with "fine".

- **Unblocking**: "What is slowing you down that I could remove?" · "What decision are you waiting on, and from whom?" · "What have you been avoiding this week?"
- **Signal**: "What do you know that I probably do not?" · "What is the team saying that is not being said to me?" · "If you ran the team, what is the first thing you would change?"
- **Calibration**: "What are you proudest of since we last spoke?" · "Where do you think you are behind, and do you want help or space?"
- **Risk**: "What is most likely to go wrong with your project in the next month?" · "Is there anything you have committed to that you now think is not achievable?"
- **Person**: "How is the workload actually feeling, honestly?" · "What would make next month better than this one?"
- **Never**: "Any updates?" — it is a status question wearing a 1-on-1 costume, and it gets a status answer every time.

## Feedback Inside A 1-on-1

- **Same-week or not at all.** Feedback held for a review cycle is a grievance with a date on it, and the behaviour has been reinforced for eight weeks.
- **Situation, behaviour, impact** (the SBI structure from the Center for Creative Leadership): where and when, what was observably done, what followed. It works because it removes intent from the sentence — the argument about "you didn't mean it that way" never starts.
- **One piece per meeting, positive or corrective.** A list reads as an ambush and the report remembers only the last item.
- **Ask for feedback upward first, and wait through the silence.** "What could I do differently?" answered with "nothing" means the answer is not yet safe to give — ask again next week, and ask about a specific thing you did.
- **Corrective feedback in a 1-on-1, never in a group, and never in writing first.** Written-first corrective feedback reads as documentation, and the person defends instead of listening.
- **Praise is the opposite**: public, specific, and repeated in writing where others see it.

## Career And Growth Conversations

- **Separate from the weekly slot.** Career talk squeezed into ten minutes after a blocker discussion gets the leftovers. Book it quarterly, 50 minutes, with its own prep on both sides.
- **Three questions to prepare, not to improvise**: what do they want to be doing in two years, what is the gap, and what is the next single project that closes some of it.
- **Growth happens in assignments, not in plans.** A development conversation that ends without a named piece of work has produced a document.
- **Never promise a promotion you do not control.** State what you will advocate for and what the decision actually depends on — a promise that fails costs more trust than the honest "here is what is in my hands".
- **Compensation, ratings and personnel changes are off the record**: discuss them, write nothing to disk (`memory-template.md`).

## Your Own Manager

The 1-on-1 with your own manager is the one most people waste by treating it as a report.

- **Bring an agenda, even if they do not ask for one.** The manager's default is their topics; the report's agenda is the only thing that changes that.
- **Lead with decisions you need, not with what you did.** "I need a call on X by Friday, here are the two options and my recommendation" is the highest-value use of the slot.
- **Surface bad news early and with a plan.** A manager who learns about a slip from someone else stops trusting the channel — and the slip was never the problem.
- **Ask for the context you are missing**: what is happening a level up, what changed in priorities, what they are being asked about that you should already know.
- **Ask what would make you easier to manage.** It gets a real answer far more often than "do you have any feedback".

## Skip-Levels

- **Purpose is signal, not supervision.** You are checking whether the picture your managers give you matches what the people doing the work see.
- **Quarterly per person, 30 minutes, rotating**, so nobody reads an invitation as a warning. Announce the rotation to the managers in between, so it is a norm rather than an event.
- **Never take an action that bypasses the manager.** Anything that needs fixing goes back through them, with the source protected. One bypass and every future skip-level is theatre.
- **Best questions**: "what would you change if you could change one thing?", "what is going well that I probably do not know about?", "is there anything your manager is doing that helps a lot?" — the last one gives the manager something usable, which keeps the ritual survivable.
- **Say what happens to what they tell you**, at the start. Ambiguity about confidentiality gets you nothing worth hearing.

## When It Rots

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| It has become a status report | The manager asks project questions first | Status async before; open with "what's on your list?" |
| Always cancelled when the week is busy | It is treated as the flexible slot | Shorten to 15, never move; two cancellations in a row is a signal to the report |
| Ten minutes and nothing to say | No shared doc, no prep on either side | Running doc owned by the report; the manager adds their one thing beforehand |
| Only bad news arrives | The slot has become the complaints channel | Add a "what went well" question and a growth topic once a month |
| The report agrees with everything | They do not think dissent is safe here | Ask for feedback upward and act visibly on one piece of it |
| Same topic every week, no movement | It is a decision nobody has made | Take it out of the 1-on-1 and give it a decision meeting with a method (`decision-rights.md`) |
| Anything else that repeats twice | The shape is wrong for this person | Ask them directly what would make the meeting worth their time, and change one thing |

**Write in the same turn as the 1-on-1**: a record block in `~/Clawic/data/meetings/records/<year>-<mm>.md` with the topics and what changed, each commitment in `## Follow-Ups` with owner and date, anything durable about how the person works, decides or wants to grow into their `Context` in `~/Clawic/data/contacts/contacts.md` (or their own file at `~/Clawic/data/contacts/<kebab-name>.md` once it no longer fits the row), the series and its cadence in `## Series`, and the skip-level rotation in `## Due` (`memory-template.md`). Compensation figures, performance ratings, health details and unannounced personnel changes are never written to disk — record the decision that resulted and nothing else.
