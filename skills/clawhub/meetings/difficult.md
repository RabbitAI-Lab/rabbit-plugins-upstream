# Difficult Meetings — Bad News, Conflict, Escalation And Hostile Rooms

**Before a hard conversation**, read the person's `Context` in `~/Clawic/data/contacts/contacts.md` (how they take news, whether they argue live or afterwards), `~/Clawic/data/meetings/decisions.md` for what was actually decided and when — a disputed memory is usually a missing log entry — the open items in `## Follow-Ups` that involve them, and `## Pain Points` for how this went last time. Everything here is a script for the human in the room, not an action to take on their behalf. Compensation, ratings, health and personnel content is discussed and **never written to disk** (`memory-template.md`).

## Delivering Bad News

**Headline first, always.** The instinct to build context before the news is the single most damaging habit in this domain: the listener stops processing at the first hint that something is wrong and hears none of the careful preamble. Say the thing in the first sentence, then explain.

Order, and it does not vary:

1. **The headline**, one sentence, no hedging. "The launch is slipping to 15 March."
2. **The cause**, briefly and without a search for blame. Two sentences at most; detail invites a debate about the cause instead of the plan.
3. **What you are doing about it**, already decided. Bad news without a plan hands the plan to the listener, and theirs will be more expensive.
4. **What you need from them**, specifically — a decision, cover, resources, or nothing.
5. **Silence.** Then take the reaction without defending. The reaction is not the conversation; it precedes it.

- **Never open with "how are you?"** before bad news. It reads as manipulation the moment the news lands, and it is remembered.
- **Own what is yours in one clause, then move on.** Extended contrition makes the listener manage your feelings, which is not their job in this meeting.
- **Never deliver serious news by group email or in a group meeting.** The people most affected hear it individually and first, or you will spend a month repairing that instead of the actual problem.
- **Give the accurate version, not the survivable one.** A slip announced as two weeks and delivered as six costs the credibility of every future estimate — and the second announcement is always harder than the honest first one would have been.
- **Bad news travels up before it travels sideways.** A manager who learns of a problem from someone else stops trusting the channel, and the channel matters more than the problem.
- **Same day.** Delay is what converts a bad number into a concealment.

## Conflict In The Room

- **Separate the positions from the interests.** Two people arguing about *what* to do usually want different things underneath — "we both want this shipped; you need it predictable and you need it fast. Which do we buy?" This move resolves more deadlocks than any amount of further debate.
- **Convert the disagreement into a testable question.** "We disagree about whether latency is origin-side. What measurement settles it, who runs it, by when?" Half of all stuck arguments are unresolved facts, not conflicting values, and facts have a procedure.
- **Name the process, not the person.** "We are going in circles" is usable; "you keep interrupting" starts a second argument on top of the first.
- **Personal attacks get named once, factually, in the room**: "let's keep this on the options." A second occurrence ends the meeting — continuing teaches everyone watching that the room is unsafe, and that costs far more than the agenda.
- **Two people in a fight the room cannot use**: park it out loud, with a named time. "You two and me at 15:00. Back to the decision."
- **Steelman before deciding.** Ask each side to state the other's position until the other agrees it is fair. It is slow, it works, and it is the only reliable way to tell a real disagreement from a misunderstanding.
- **A room that never disagrees is not aligned, it is quiet.** Silence after a contentious proposal is a prompt to ask by name, not a green light.

## The Hostile Stakeholder

- **Find out what they actually want before the meeting.** Hostility is usually about being surprised, bypassed, or made to look uninformed in front of others — and all three are fixable by a five-minute call beforehand (`preparation.md`).
- **Pre-wire, in this order: the loudest skeptic first, allies second, the decider last.** Someone who has already voiced their objection privately rarely relitigates it in public, and their objection usually improves the proposal while there is still time to change it.
- **Agree publicly on the parts you agree on**, first and explicitly. It shrinks the visible dispute to its actual size, which is almost always smaller than the room believes.
- **Ask for the objection as a condition**: "what would have to be true for you to support this?" An objection that cannot be stated as a condition is a preference, and naming that distinction politely ends most of them.
- **Do not win in the room.** A public defeat produces a private campaign, and the decision gets reversed in a meeting you are not in.
- **When the attack is about credibility rather than content**, address it once, briefly, in facts, then return to the agenda. Defending at length concedes that the meeting is about you.

## Escalation Meetings

The meeting where two groups who could not agree bring it to someone who can decide.

- **Escalate the decision, never the person.** "We need a call between A and B" travels; "Marketing is blocking us" produces a defensive meeting with no outcome.
- **Both sides state the other's position first.** It takes four minutes and removes the entire class of escalations that were a misunderstanding.
- **Bring the decision framed**: the question in one sentence, the two or three options, what each costs, and what happens if nothing is decided.
- **The decider decides in the room, or names the date and the missing input.** An escalation that ends in "let me think about it" with no date has made things worse — both teams now wait instead of working around it.
- **Tell the other side you are escalating, before you do.** A surprise escalation is remembered long after the decision is forgotten, and it makes the next one adversarial.
- **Record it as a decision with its rejected options** (`decision-rights.md`). Escalated decisions are the ones most likely to be relitigated by whoever lost.

## Saying No

- **No, then the reason, then the alternative** — in that order. A reason before the no reads as negotiable, and the other person spends it arguing with the reason.
- **Trade, do not refuse flatly, when the request is legitimate**: "yes, and it moves X by two weeks — your call which." That sentence converts a refusal into a decision the requester owns.
- **Never say no to a scope change live in a client meeting.** "That's a change — I'll come back today with what it costs and what it moves" (`external.md`).
- **No to a person with power over the work is a reshape, not a refusal**: ask for the priority call, offer the smaller version, name what drops.
- **A soft no is a slow yes.** "I'll see what I can do" with no intention behind it costs the relationship more than the clear no would have.

## Performance And Personnel

- **The conversation is never a surprise.** Serious feedback that first appears in a formal conversation is a process failure that happened weeks earlier — everything here assumes the pattern was already named in a 1-on-1 (`one-on-ones.md`).
- **Situation, behaviour, impact, then the required change with a date.** Observable behaviour only; intent and character are unarguable and produce defence rather than change.
- **Have it in person or on camera, alone, and never on a Friday afternoon** with nobody available for the aftermath.
- **HR present for anything with consequences**, and know before the meeting exactly what is decided and what is still open. Improvising a consequence in the room is how organizations end up in disputes.
- **Nothing goes to disk.** Ratings, compensation figures, health details, legal advice and unannounced personnel changes are off the record for this skill — write the resulting decision ("comp review completed for the team") and nothing else, not a redacted version.
- **The formal record for these lives in the employer's HR system**, not in a meeting workspace. If a record here already holds such content, delete the line and say so in one line.

## Interview Debriefs

**Output**: hire / no-hire per interviewer, with the evidence. **Length**: 25 minutes. **Attendees**: the panel.

- **Every interviewer writes their verdict and evidence before anyone speaks**, and submits it. Spoken-first debriefs converge on the first or most senior opinion, and the panel then believes it agreed independently.
- **Evidence, not impressions.** "Could not explain their own trade-off on the caching design" is data; "not a strong communicator" is a vote with a rationalization attached.
- **Disagreement is the useful part** — spend the meeting on the split, not on the unanimous parts. Two opposing verdicts usually mean the interviews probed different things, which is a signal about the loop, not about the candidate.
- **"Culture fit" gets rejected as a reason** and rewritten as an observable behaviour tied to a requirement, or dropped. It is where bias enters with the least resistance.
- **A no-hire with reasons is more valuable than a reluctant yes.** Say the reasons out loud so the loop improves.

## When It Goes Wrong Live

| It happens | Do |
|---|---|
| Someone starts crying | Stop, offer a break, do not fill the silence. Reconvene later if needed; continuing is not kindness |
| Someone shouts | Lower your own volume and slow down. "Let's take five minutes." Do not match the register |
| You are ambushed with something you cannot answer | "I don't have that answer and I'm not going to guess. I'll come back by <date>." Then do |
| You were wrong, publicly | Say so in one sentence, correct it, move on. Extended apology reopens the topic and costs more than the error |
| The decision is being made without the missing person | Name it and propose the smallest reversible version today, the full call when they are there |
| Someone commits you to something | Correct it in the room, immediately and neutrally. Correcting it afterwards is a dispute, not a clarification |
| The room has turned on one person | End the item. "Let's take this offline." Continuing costs the whole team's safety, not just theirs |
| Anything else that stops being productive | Name what is happening, propose a break or a new time, and say what you will each bring back |

## After

- **The written version becomes the version.** Send your summary the same day: what was decided, what was disputed and remains open, who does what by when. Whoever writes it first frames it, and in a contested meeting that matters more than anything said aloud.
- **Neutral wording, no scoring.** A recap that wins the argument on paper restarts it.
- **Check in with the person on the receiving end within a few days**, separately from the work. It is what determines whether the relationship recovers.
- **A hard conversation that worked is reusable.** Write the script — how it was framed, what landed, what to avoid — before the details fade.

**Write in the same turn**: the record block in `~/Clawic/data/meetings/records/<year>-<mm>.md` with the decision and what remains open — never the personnel or compensation content; each escalated decision as a row in `~/Clawic/data/meetings/decisions.md` with its rejected options and who decided; every commitment in `## Follow-Ups` with owner and date; how the person handles conflict, news or pressure into their `Context` in `~/Clawic/data/contacts/contacts.md`; the failure pattern into `## Pain Points`; and a framing that worked as `~/Clawic/data/meetings/artifacts/facilitation-<situation>.md` with its `## Boxes` line (`memory-template.md`). Anything the user marks off the record is not written anywhere, in any form. A hard conversation nobody wrote down gets remembered differently by each side within a week, and the difference is what the next dispute is about.
