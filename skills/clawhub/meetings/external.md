# External Meetings — Clients, Sales, Vendors, Investors And Boards

## What Changes When They Are Not Colleagues

Four differences, and every rule on this page derives from one of them:

1. **Words become commitments.** Internally, "we could probably do that by March" is a thought; externally it is quoted back in a procurement meeting. Everything said is on the record unless explicitly framed otherwise.
2. **There is no shared ledger.** Nobody on their side maintains your action items, so the same-day written recap is not politeness — it is the only ledger the relationship has.
3. **The decider is usually not in the room.** Your champion carries your case into a meeting you will never attend, so your job is partly to arm them with something forwardable.
4. **Formality is defensive.** Minutes, dated next steps and written scope changes exist for the day the relationship is under strain, not for the day they are written.

**Two roles per side, always.** One person leads and one takes notes and watches the room; the person leading cannot read faces while constructing the next sentence. If the user is alone, they record the close only and write the record afterwards.

## Client Status

**Output**: their risk register updated and the next step dated. **Length**: 25 minutes. **Cadence**: matched to the delivery rhythm, never more often than there is news.

- **Their agenda first, then yours.** Opening with your progress report tells them their concerns are item four; opening with "what's on your mind this week?" surfaces the escalation before it becomes an email to your boss.
- **Lead with your own late items** (`preparation.md`). Naming a slip before they do costs fifteen seconds and removes the leverage they were about to use.
- **Bad news comes with the plan and the new date, in the same sentence.** A slip announced without a recovery plan invites them to design one, and their design will cost more.
- **Never renegotiate scope live.** "That's a change to scope — I'll come back with what it costs and what it moves, by Thursday." Live scope changes are the most expensive habit in client work, and they are always agreed by the person least able to price them.
- **Confirm understanding in their words**: "so the blocker is that legal cannot sign before the 15th — is that right?" Paraphrasing is the cheapest defence against two organizations remembering different sentences.
- **Quote them verbatim on three things** in the record: commitments, numbers, and complaints.
- **Silence from a client is data.** Two status meetings with nothing from their side usually means the project has lost its sponsor internally, not that things are fine.

## Discovery And Sales Calls

**Output**: their problem in their own words, the qualifier answers, and a dated next step. **Length**: 25-50 minutes. **Talk ratio**: they should be speaking most of it.

- **Ask about the problem before mentioning the product.** Rackham's SPIN sequence — situation, problem, implication, need-payoff — works because the implication questions get *them* to articulate the cost of not acting, which no pitch can do on their behalf.
- **The qualifiers to leave with**, in some form of MEDDIC or BANT: who signs, what budget exists and in whose line, what their decision process and timeline actually are, what the metric of success is, and who loses if this goes ahead.
- **"What happens if you do nothing?"** is the single most useful question. If the honest answer is "nothing much", the deal is not real and the next meeting is a favour.
- **Find the champion and the blocker.** Every organization has one person who wants this and one who will stop it; the second one is rarely in the first meeting.
- **Give them something forwardable.** A one-page summary they can paste into their internal thread does more than a follow-up call, because the real decision meeting happens without you.
- **Never leave without a dated next step that has their name on an action too.** A next step only you own is a next step that is not happening.

## Vendor And Procurement

- **Multi-vendor comparisons need identical questions**, asked in the same order, or the comparison measures who demoed best. Write the question list before the first call.
- **Ask for the failure cases**: "what do your unhappy customers say?", "what is on the roadmap because it is missing?", "which of my requirements is a bad fit?" The answer to the last one is the highest-signal minute of the call.
- **References the vendor did not choose** are worth ten they did.
- **Nothing is agreed until the exit terms are.** Data export, notice period, price escalation on renewal, and what happens on acquisition — all decided while you still have leverage, which is before signing and never after.
- **Take the pricing conversation off the demo call.** Prices quoted in a demo are anchors, and negotiating against an anchor set in a room you did not design is the worst position available (`decision-rights.md` on rejected options).
- **Record what was rejected and why** — the next person to evaluate vendors in two years will otherwise redo the whole exercise.

## Investor Updates

- **Monthly, written, same shape every time**, and the meeting only when something needs a decision or an introduction. Investors read many updates; predictability is what makes yours read.
- **Fixed sections**: the headline metric with its trend, what changed since last month, what went wrong, the ask, and cash position with runway in months. Same order every time, so the reader can find the number they care about.
- **Bad news goes above the fold.** An investor who finds a problem buried in paragraph six stops trusting paragraph one — and the discount applies to every future update.
- **One specific ask per update.** "Intros to VP Engineering candidates at Series B fintechs" gets answered; "let us know if you can help" does not.
- **Never present a metric whose definition changed without saying so.** It is the fastest way to lose the room, and it is always noticed.
- **The board meeting is not the update.** If the numbers are new in the room, the meeting will be spent explaining them instead of deciding anything.

## Board And Steering

**Output**: the decisions that require the body, minuted formally. **Length**: 90-120 minutes quarterly. **Attendees**: members, plus invited guests for their item only.

- **The pack lands ≥5 days ahead.** A board pack sent the night before produces a meeting spent reading, and directors who cannot discharge their duty of care will say so.
- **Consent agenda for everything routine** — approvals, prior minutes, standing reports — voted as a single block, with any member able to pull an item out for discussion. It is the mechanism that buys the strategic hour back.
- **Structure the session**: consent agenda 10 · CEO context and metrics 20 · one or two strategic discussions 45 · governance and approvals 15 · executive session without management 15. The executive session is standard practice, not a signal of trouble, which is exactly why it should be on every agenda.
- **Bring decisions, not information.** A board meeting spent being informed is a board meeting that adds nothing the written pack did not.
- **Pre-wire every director individually before a contentious item** (`preparation.md`). A board surprised in the room defers, and a deferral costs a quarter.
- **Minutes are a different genre**: attendance and quorum, resolutions in their exact final wording, votes for/against/abstained, conflicts of interest declared, time of close. Decisions, never who argued what — naming positions in formal minutes creates discoverable liability and chills the debate (`recaps-and-minutes.md`).
- **Approved minutes are the record.** Draft within a week, circulate for correction, approve at the following meeting, and mark the approved version as such.

## The Follow-Up SLA

| Meeting | Recap by | Contains |
|---|---|---|
| Client status | Same day | Decisions, both sides' actions with dates, open questions, next meeting |
| Discovery or sales call | Within 24h | Their problem in their words, what you proposed, the dated next step |
| Vendor call | Within 24h | Questions asked, answers given, what remains open before a decision |
| Board or steering | Draft minutes within a week | Formal minutes for approval at the next meeting |
| Anything external at all | Within 24h | At minimum: what was agreed and the next dated step |

- **The recap is theirs to correct, and say so**: "corrections by Thursday 12:00, otherwise this is our record." That single line converts a summary into a record without asking anyone to sign anything.
- **Send it to everyone who was in the room, plus their absent decider** if you know who that is and it is not political to do so.
- **Actions on their side are named too**, with dates. A recap listing only your commitments reads as a to-do list you wrote for yourself.
- **External recaps are shorter and more careful than internal ones**: decisions, dates, next step. Anything that reads as a new commitment gets checked before sending.

## Commitment Hygiene

- **Three sentences that must never be improvised**: what it will cost, when it will be delivered, and whether something is technically possible. Each gets "let me confirm and come back to you today" — and then actually today.
- **"I'll check" is a commitment with a date**, not an escape. Say when you will come back, and put it in the ledger like any other item (`follow-through.md`).
- **A commitment made by anyone on your side is a commitment.** If a colleague overcommits in the room, correct it in the room — correcting it in the recap is much more expensive, and correcting it a month later is a dispute.
- **Anything agreed verbally that changes scope, price or date goes into writing the same day**, in the recap, phrased as a confirmation rather than a renegotiation.

**Write in the same turn as the external meeting**: a record block in `~/Clawic/data/meetings/records/<year>-<mm>.md` with the attendees, their verbatim commitments, numbers and complaints; each decision as a row in `~/Clawic/data/meetings/decisions.md`; both sides' actions in `## Follow-Ups` (theirs in `Waiting On`); every new person on their side in `~/Clawic/data/contacts/contacts.md` with who decides and who blocks in `Context`; a one-line decision summary in `~/Clawic/data/projects/<project>.md` when the work is tracked there; approved board minutes as `~/Clawic/data/meetings/artifacts/minutes-<body>-<period>.md` and the accumulating account brief as `artifacts/prep-<account>.md`, each with its `## Boxes` line (`memory-template.md`). Join passcodes, dial-in PINs and secret calendar links are credentials: pointer only, value stripped. An external commitment that exists only in someone's memory is the one that turns into a dispute.
