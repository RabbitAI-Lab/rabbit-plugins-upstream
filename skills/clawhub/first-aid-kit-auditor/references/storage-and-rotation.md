# Storage and Rotation

## Where the kit lives
- **One box, one place.** A kit split across bathroom, kitchen, and garage is three half-kits.
- Cool, dry, dark — not the bathroom (shower humidity kills adhesive and accelerates drug
  degradation) and not above a stove or radiator.
- Above child reach but accessible to adults in seconds; every caregiver knows the spot.
- Label it. A clear box beats a "mystery bin" at 2 AM.

## The car-kit problem
Interior car temperatures reach 60–70 °C in summer sun. At those temperatures:
- Adhesives fail within a season or two.
- Tablets/capsules degrade far faster than their printed date (heat accelerates hydrolysis).
- Liquid syrups separate and lose potency.

Practices: keep a heat-tolerant core in the car (gauze, wrap, gloves, trauma tools, mylar,
water), and swap the car meds on a spring/fall calendar — or carry the med kit in and out.

## Rotation calendar
| Cadence | Action |
|---|---|
| After each use | Restock the item the same week (audit → shop) |
| Every 6 months | Walk the box: expired? damaged packaging? flashlight batteries? |
| Yearly | Full `audit` against profile — especially after family changes (new baby, new allergy dx, new pet) |
| On travel | Re-audit against destination: motion-sickness meds for the route, antihistamines, ORS |

## Expiry reality check
- Expiry dates on OTC meds are conservative "full potency" guarantees; the FDA's Shelf Life
  Extension Program has found most solid-dose drugs retain potency for years past date. BUT:
  - **Never extend:** liquids/suspensions (children's syrups), nitroglycerin, epinephrine
    auto-injectors, insulin, anything lookings discolored/crumbly/smelling off.
  - Sterile packaging (gauze, dressings) has a date because sterility assurance degrades —
    respect it for anything that will touch an open wound.
- Aspirin that smells of vinegar has hydrolyzed — replace.

## Kid-specific storage rules
- Syrups in original child-resistant caps; never call medicine "candy".
- Keep the poison-control number (US: 1-800-222-1222) on the kit itself and in phones.
- Iron tablets and nicotine gum are the classic pediatric-overdose visitors — they don't belong
  in the first-aid box at child height.

## Training (the multiplier)
Contents without competence waste minutes. The verdict line reminds every audit:
a first-aid/CPR course is the best kit item. Stop the Bleed (~2 h) for adventure profiles;
pediatric CPR for households with infants.
