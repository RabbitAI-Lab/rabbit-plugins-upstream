Same side of the Moon: why we always see it, and tidal locking

Why do we always see the same side of the Moon? It is tidally locked: it spins once for every trip around Earth, so the same face points our way, give or take a slight wobble called libration. The Moon phases only change how much of that face is lit. Photo: NASA, the full Moon over Kennedy Space Center.

Lunar cycle length: 29.5 days from new Moon to new Moon

How long is a lunar cycle? The Moon phases repeat every 29.5 days, from one new Moon to the next, while one trip around Earth takes about 27.3 days. Photo: a NASA astronaut on the International Space Station.

Moon phases viewing guide: when the main phases rise and set

When can you see the main Moon phases? A first quarter Moon rises around midday and is best in the evening; a full Moon rises at sunset and sets at sunrise; a third quarter Moon rises around midnight. Save it for your next clear night. Photo: NASA/Bill Ingalls, the full Moon over the U.S. Capitol in the early evening.
