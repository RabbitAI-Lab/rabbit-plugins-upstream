# Call Negotiation Playbook — Reference

## Why scripts beat improvisation

Phone negotiations are lost in the gaps where improvisation happens: the pause after
"That's our policy," the moment you can't remember whether you already escalated, the
temptation to fill silence. A script isn't rigidity — it's **preloading your working
memory** so your attention stays on listening and note-taking, not on composing
sentences under stress.

## The anatomy of every difficult call

1. **Triage (0–15s)** — rep classifies: angry customer? upsell? retention? escalation?
   Your opening line picks the bucket. Bad openings ("I have a problem with my bill…")
   get generic handling.
2. **Ask (15–60s)** — one concrete, verifiable request. "Remove the $80 charge" not
   "fix my bill."
3. **Objection cycle** — they say no in one of ~5 predictable ways; you respond
   acknowledge → bridge → ask. 2–3 cycles max.
4. **Escalation** — front-line authority is deliberately small. Second no = transfer.
5. **Close** — confirmation number, name, date, what was promised. Written follow-up
   within 24h if it matters.

## Acknowledge → bridge → ask (the response pattern)

| Element | Function | Example fragment |
|---|---|---|
| Acknowledge | lowers defenses, signals cooperation | "I understand that's the policy" |
| Bridge | re-anchors on your facts/leverage | "—and my issue is the visit was quoted as free, and I have the chat log" |
| Ask | moves the call forward concretely | "Can you remove this specific charge?" |

Practice mode scores exactly these three elements because they're measurable proxies
for "did the response move the call forward."

## Leverage inventory (pick only what's true)

- **Evidence**: chat logs, emails, screenshots, ads, written quotes
- **Tenure & payment history**: years, autopay, no late payments
- **Alternatives**: competitor offers in writing; switching costs them more than the
  discount costs them
- **Regulators**: FCC (telecom), CFPB (banks/cards), state AG (most consumer issues),
  state insurance commissioner, housing inspectors — all free, all create work for them
- **Network adequacy / habitability / validation rights**: statutory hooks by domain
- **Social proof**: reviews, referrals (state facts only; never threaten)

## Scenario-specific notes

**Billing disputes.** The magic phrase family is "billing error dispute window."
Card chargebacks (60–120 days typically) are the nuclear fallback that costs the
merchant the amount + a fee — mentioning you know the window often ends the argument.

**Retention gauntlets.** Retention desks are paid to save you; their offers are better
than front-line's. "Please transfer me to cancellations" is not a threat, it's routing.
Never lie about having a competitor offer — you may be asked to produce it.

**Collections (US-flavored, verify locally).** Validation first: the letter within 5
days of first contact, your 30-day validation window, and — critically — never
acknowledge the debt or make a "good faith" partial payment before validation: both
can restart the statute of limitations on time-barred debt. Everything in writing.
Pay-for-delete is a negotiation, not a right.

**Insurance denials.** A large share of denials are administrative (wrong code, missing
authorization on file, network-status error). The reversal path is: call for the exact
clause → written rationale → internal appeal → external independent review (binding on
the insurer in many jurisdictions) → state regulator. Each step has a deadline; log them.

**Landlord repairs.** Habitability standards (heat, water, locks, structural safety)
are non-negotiable in most jurisdictions; "repair and deduct" and rent escrow exist in
many but not all — check local law before threatening either. The call's goal is a
**dated commitment**, which is why every response in that tree ends in "when?"

**Salary/rate calls.** Scope documentation beats market data beats threat. The
fallback ladder is title → remote/education budget → dated review. A dated review you
hold them to beats a vague "later."

## Cross-cultural note

Directness calibrated here is US-typical. In cultures where escalation reads as
aggression, the same tree works with softer tags ("I know this isn't your decision —
who could help me with this?"). The 5 rules survive translation; the phrasing
shouldn't.

## What this is not

- Not legal advice; collections/landlord/insurance trees cite rights that vary by
  jurisdiction — verify locally before relying on them.
- Not a guarantee of outcomes. Reps vary; some calls are unwinnable today. The script
  makes your side of the call optimal, which over N calls is worth real money.
- Not for abusive tactics. Every modeled response is firm, factual, and civil; anything
  that relies on deceiving the other party is deliberately absent.
