## Apply the research to writing decisions

Help the reader recognize the applicant's relevant capabilities, see how they would contribute and understand their interest in the work. Give the employer a reason to meet them.

1. **Choose the case before choosing the sentences.** Identify the employer's most important work, the applicant's strongest relevant capability, and the experience that makes that capability believable. Write a one-sentence internal governing argument for the interview, then build the letter around it. This guides reasoning without prescribing an opening sentence.
2. **Make the transfer explicit.** Name the relevant capability, show where the applicant exercised it, and connect it to this job. Different titles, sectors, or tools can still demonstrate the same underlying judgment and skill. Prefer a positive bridge over introducing an unasked-for deficiency.
3. **Give each example a persuasive job.** Select only the evidence needed to establish the strongest contribution; choose its amount and combination by persuasive value, not an example quota, chronological tour or requirement checklist. Explain the applicant's decision, action, scope, or useful result. An example without a number can still be strong.
4. **Use confident, proportionate advocacy.** State genuine strengths and ownership directly. Replace unsupported superiority claims with the strongest accurate achievement. Do not turn honesty into timid verbs, constant disclaimers, or compulsory self-deprecation.
5. **Explain the attraction to the actual work.** Identify what the applicant genuinely wants to work on and why. When known, connect this interest to a past choice, project, or recurring work preference. Company research makes that explanation specific; researched company praise cannot establish the applicant's feelings.
6. **Let interpersonal qualities appear in behavior.** A short example of making a difficult process workable for colleagues can communicate collaboration and competence together. Generic assurances of being warm, friendly, passionate, or a perfect cultural fit do less explanatory work.
7. **Make the argument easy to follow.** Put the paragraph's point near its start, use recognizable terms, and remove throat-clearing, repetition, and details that compete with the strongest case. Keep enough context for the achievement to mean something.
8. **Use narrative to explain judgment, not to perform a story.** A compact sequence of problem, choice, action, and result is useful when it makes capability tangible. A scene-setting hook is optional. Reuse a strong resume achievement when the letter adds decision-making, meaning, or employer-specific relevance instead of merely copying a bullet.
9. **Create a distinctive reason to meet this person.** Prefer a particular combination of strengths and experiences over interchangeable adjectives. Do not add eccentricity merely to stand out.

All sample sentences below are fictional teaching examples, not facts about the applicant.

## Nine primary sources and the writing choices they support

### 1. Translate experience into the capability the reader needs

**Brown, B. K., & Campion, M. A. (1994).** *Biodata phenomenology: Recruiters' perceptions and use of biographical information in resume screening.* Journal of Applied Psychology, 79(6), 897–908. [Article DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.79.6.897); [primary underlying dissertation abstract](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/dissertations/AAI9301262/).

**Finding and study type:** Three complementary studies involved 344 recruiters and line managers from 28 companies. Recruiters inferred abilities, motivation, leadership, and interpersonal qualities from biographical information; applicant attractiveness increased when information demonstrated attributes important to the job.

**Writing choice:** Explain the relevant capability inside an experience. Do not rely on matching titles or leave a cross-sector connection entirely to the reader.

**Example:** “Leading quality calibration across a distributed review team taught me to turn ambiguous standards into repeatable decisions, a capability directly relevant to your evaluation operations.”

**Limits:** Historical screening research including hypothetical ratings. This pass verified the primary dissertation abstract, not the full journal article. It supports interpretable capability evidence, not a promise that a bridging sentence changes callbacks.

### 2. State the competence rather than hoping it is inferred

**Bright, J. E. H., & Hutton, S. (2000).** *The Impact of Competency Statements on Résumés for Short-listing Decisions.* International Journal of Selection and Assessment, 8(2), 41–53. [Publisher abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1468-2389.00132).

**Finding and study type:** Sixty-two managers and HR consultants evaluated genuine applications experimentally varied to include competency statements. These improved suitability, interview-decision, and ranking ratings; location within the resume or cover letter did not significantly change ratings.

**Writing choice:** State a relevant strength plainly and follow it with evidence. The letter can interpret a strong resume achievement; an absolute ban on repeated subject matter would sacrifice useful evidence.

**Example:** “I bring experience making complex guidance usable: I built a shared review guide and trained new reviewers to apply it consistently.”

**Limits:** Controlled application ratings, not observed hiring. The study did not test the proposed strength–example sequence or repeat-versus-no-repeat letter rule. June 2000 is the journal issue date; the publisher separately shows a later online date.

### 3. Treat clarity as part of advocacy

**Wiles, E., Munyikwa, Z., & Horton, J. (2025).** *Algorithmic Writing Assistance on Jobseekers' Resumes Increases Hires.* Management Science, 71(12), 10144–10164. [Published article](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2024.04528).

**Finding and study type:** A randomized field experiment offered nongenerative resume-writing help to nearly half a million online labor-market entrants. The treatment improved readability and errors; applicants were hired 8% more often, with no evidence of lower employer satisfaction.

**Writing choice:** Edit so that the reader can readily recognize the applicant's actual contribution. Clear writing can reveal ability that poor expression obscures.

**Example:** Replace “I was involved in various operational initiatives” with “I coordinated weekly quality reviews and converted recurring errors into training updates.”

**Limits:** The 8% is relative and specific to this intervention and market. It is not a cover-letter or generative-AI estimate. The study does not isolate front-loading, paragraph length, reading time, or cognitive fluency as mechanisms.

### 4. Present strengths directly

**Waung, M., McAuslan, P., DiMambro, J. M., & Mięgoć, N. (2017).** *Impression Management Use in Resumes and Cover Letters.* Journal of Business and Psychology, 32, 727–746. [Publisher record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10869-016-9470-9); [author-uploaded full text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/307918867_Impression_Management_Use_in_Resumes_and_Cover_Letters).

**Finding and study type:** Analysis of 60 application packages informed a mock-application experiment with MTurk evaluators. Lower-intensity self-promotion and ingratiation improved perceived fit. The promotion manipulation mainly contrasted positive adjectives with stronger superlatives.

**Writing choice:** Make affirmative claims about genuine strengths; let relevant accomplishments support them. Do not confuse accurate ownership with arrogance or remove all evaluative language.

**Example:** “I am effective at bringing structure to ambiguous work. On one project, I turned inconsistent reviewer practices into a shared decision guide.”

**Limits:** Perceptions of a mock applicant, with resume and letter evaluated together, rather than actual callbacks. This did not directly compare substantiated achievements against unsupported adjectives; that preference is editorial synthesis. It does not show that every adjective or strong claim harms the applicant.

### 5. Answer capability and organizational interest separately

**Kristof-Brown, A. L. (2000).** *Perceived Applicant Fit: Distinguishing Between Recruiters' Perceptions of Person-Job and Person-Organization Fit.* Personnel Psychology, 53(3), 643–671. [Publisher abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1744-6570.2000.tb00217.x).

**Finding and study type:** Two recruiter studies, one using mock applicants and another a field setting, distinguished job fit from organizational fit. Knowledge, skills, and abilities primarily informed the former; values and personality informed the latter. Both perceptions uniquely predicted hiring recommendations.

**Writing choice:** Do not use enthusiasm or company admiration as a substitute for ability. Explain both the relevant contribution and the genuine attraction to this work or organization.

**Example:** “My background combines process design and reviewer coaching. Your focus on making evaluation usable by product teams is particularly appealing because that translation is the part of quality work I most enjoy.”

**Limits:** Interview-centered recruiter judgments, not a causal test of letter structure or proof that perceived fit equals performance. Organizational fit need not mean social similarity or copying a company's slogans.

### 6. Make genuine interest in the work visible

**Woolley, K., & Fishbach, A. (2018).** *Underestimating the importance of expressing intrinsic motivation in job interviews.* Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 148, 1–11. [Author-hosted article](https://kaitlinwoolley.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/1-s2-0-s0749597817300614-main.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2018.06.004).

**Finding and study type:** Five recruitment-role studies reported N=1,428. Participants underestimated how much evaluators valued expressed intrinsic motivation. Samples included employed online participants and MBAs assuming candidate or recruiter roles.

**Writing choice:** Give a real reason the work is appealing. Connect it to a real choice or activity to make the explanation personal and clear.

**Example:** “I enjoy turning difficult edge cases into guidance other people can use. That is what drew me to your team's work on evaluation standards.”

**Limits:** Primarily role-play judgments and pitch preferences, not observed hires. Some pitch comparisons paired high intrinsic with low extrinsic motivation and vice versa. The study does not prove that a specific motivation sentence, passion claim, or career-sacrifice story improves callbacks. Past-choice substantiation is an editorial extension.

### 7. Use genuine professional motive where it supports the contribution

**Derfler-Rozin, R., & Pitesa, M. (2020).** *Motivation Purity Bias: Expression of Extrinsic Motivation Undermines Perceived Intrinsic Motivation and Engenders Bias in Selection Decisions.* Academy of Management Journal, 63(6), 1840–1864. [Publisher abstract](https://journals.aom.org/doi/abs/10.5465/amj.2017.0617); [accepted manuscript](https://www.newswise.com/pdf_docs/158325394081441_amj.2017.0617.pdf).

**Finding and study type:** Four studies included naturally written student cover letters and experiments with business students and hiring managers. Expressed extrinsic motives reduced inferred intrinsic motivation and selection evaluations. Hiring managers in later experiments believed their judgments were consequential; these were not actual employer callbacks.

**Writing choice:** When selecting letter content, prioritize the genuine work-related interest and contribution. Compensation and benefits still belong in opportunity research and appropriate hiring discussions.

**Example:** “I want to apply my training-design experience to the practical challenge of helping reviewers make reliable decisions.”

**Limits:** The authors characterize the inference as a bias, not a valid employee-quality test. Do not convert this into pretending pay does not matter, never asking about benefits, or accepting poor conditions. The study does not establish that intrinsic and extrinsic motivation are incompatible.

### 8. Do not build a letter around a warmth-and-competence trick

**Veit, S., Arnu, H., Di Stasio, V., Yemane, R., & Coenders, M. (2022; online 2021).** *The “Big Two” in Hiring Discrimination: Evidence From a Cross-National Field Experiment.* Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 48(2), 167–182. [Full published text](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167220982900).

**Finding and study type:** A correspondence experiment varied warmth and competence signals in 13,162 applications across five European countries and six occupations. Warmth claims did not increase employer responses overall. The competence estimate was small and marginal (b=.01, p<.10), disappearing with analytic weights; its coefficient did not significantly exceed warmth's.

**Writing choice:** Convey cooperation through useful work behavior rather than adding a personality testimonial as a presumed callback lever.

**Example:** “I worked with reviewers to resolve recurring disagreements and turned the decisions into practical guidance.”

**Limits:** Competence was a bundle including motivation and added responsibilities across resume and letter. This is evidence against a universal signaling shortcut, not strong positive evidence for a particular wording strategy or a conclusion that interpersonal qualities never matter.

### 9. Narrative can help people interpret evidence; it is not hiring magic

**Niessen, A. S. M., Kausel, E. E., & Neumann, M. (2022; online 2021).** *Using narratives and numbers in performance prediction: Attitudes, confidence, and validity.* International Journal of Selection and Assessment. [Full published text](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ijsa.12364); [university manuscript](https://pure.rug.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/218079440/Int_J_Selection_Assessment_2021_Niessen_Using_narratives_and_numbers_in_performance_prediction_Attitudes_confidence.pdf).

**Finding and study type:** A preregistered prediction-task experiment with 68 Dutch psychology students compared personal statements with quantified ratings when predicting academic performance. Narrative information felt more informative and received somewhat more weight. Prediction confidence and accuracy did not improve.

**Writing choice:** Use a compact causal account when it explains judgment better than a list. Keep relevant outcomes or numbers inside it when available.

**Example:** “When reviewers interpreted the policy differently, I collected the disputed cases, worked through the decisions with the team, and created a guide for future reviews.”

**Limits:** Adjacent evidence, not professional hiring or a cover-letter experiment. It compared narratives with quantified ratings, not prose against achievement metrics. No evidence here establishes that storytelling improves recall, beats every other format, or causes interviews.

## Research interpretation and source checks

- Keep the existing direct cover-letter and AI-writing research; this pass adds a stronger motivational account and more explicit translation from behavioral evidence into writing decisions.
- Opening with a governing argument, choosing complementary examples, front-loading relevance, repeating an achievement with new interpretation, and creating a distinctive combination of strengths are editorial judgments supported by the broader evidence. They were not isolated experimental treatments in these studies.
- The best reason to use concrete details is that they identify work, clarify ownership, demonstrate relevant judgment, and make claims assessable. Do not reinstate a “concrete words seem truer” trick: the existing evidence file already records the inconclusive registered replications.
- No reliable basis was found in this focused pass for a universal recruiter attention span, mandatory emotional hook, pronoun ratio, ideal adjective count, ATS percentage, or experimentally guaranteed letter sequence. Do not pad the skill with these claims.
- The main additions are Woolley/Fishbach, Derfler-Rozin/Pitesa, Veit et al., and the bounded narrative reference. Brown/Campion, Bright/Hutton, Wiles et al., Waung et al., and Kristof-Brown were rechecked because they directly inform the practical changes.
- Publisher/primary records and targeted title-plus-correction/retraction searches did not surface a correction or retraction notice for this selected set. Do not add popular humblebragging, priming, or memorability findings simply to fill a psychology category.
