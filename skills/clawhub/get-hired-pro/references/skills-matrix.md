# Guided career and skills-matrix discovery

Guide the person through discovering the useful capabilities missing from their current résumé. Ask clarifying questions and **suggest related skills for them to accept, correct or reject**. Repeat that conversation as useful. Save the answers so the user does not have to reconstruct their career for every application.

Build this record once and update it as the applicant adds experience. Start from what is already available; an incomplete career reference can still support a useful application. Honor a user's choice to proceed or stop discovery.

## Start with their intended work

Read their chosen role or search goals first. If these are missing, ask what work they want to pursue. Use that direction to ask about relevant experience their résumé may omit. When the connection is weak, explain the specific mismatch, ask a focused question and offer alternatives. Let the person choose; preserve their accepted direction and rejected suggestions. A different previous title or industry is not itself a mismatch.

## Start with their work, not just résumé headings

First check whether the conversation or workspace already contains a résumé or career record. If neither is available, ask whether the user has a résumé they can share or paste. If they do not have one, guide them through their work history and substantial activities conversationally and build the initial career reference from their answers. You can start with their work history when they have no résumé. Do not ask for material already supplied.

Read their supplied résumé, work history and known corrections. Ask about independent work, consulting, business ownership, projects, publications, volunteering, teaching, caregiving or other substantial experience where useful. Read available project and publication material to understand what the person contributed. Do not require a catalogue of every article or recreate research already available.

Ask the user to describe what they did: work they owned, decisions they made, systems they used or built, problems they solved and contributions to their team. They may have omitted skills because those activities felt routine, fell outside their title or were already removed from an older résumé. Keep the full useful record even when an application should show only a selection.

## Ask concrete follow-up questions

Choose questions that fill actual gaps. Use what has already been answered and keep batches manageable. Do not send a long generic questionnaire or repeat settled questions.

- **Software and practical tools:** What did you use for communication, documents, spreadsheets, presentations, project tracking, reporting, customer records, accounting, design, publishing, marketing, data or development? Prompt with useful examples such as Slack, Google Docs/Sheets, Excel, Word, PowerPoint, CRM tools, WordPress, analytics tools, Python, SQL, JSON, APIs, Git or AI tools when related to their work. Ask what they did with them so the record contains usable experience, not just product names.
- **Team contributions:** Did you train or onboard people, write instructions, create onboarding or remediation materials, coach colleagues, improve standards, develop rubrics, lead calibration, recruit, interview, coordinate work, resolve escalations or communicate findings? What did your involvement make workable for the team?
- **Processes and automation:** Did you create a spreadsheet, report, database, script, integration, template, workflow, dashboard or tool that improved the work? Did you automate recurring checks, reporting, follow-up or handoffs? Ask about both personal and team work. AI assistance does not remove their ownership of software they built.
- **Delivery and business work:** How did you gather requirements, handle changes, work with customers or other teams, check quality, organize information, prioritize, report progress or complete a handoff? For independent work, ask about the relevant customer, pricing, marketing, support or fulfillment responsibilities.
- **Results and scope:** What improved, what was delivered, who used it, and what useful scale or outcome do they remember? Record supplied numbers and qualitative results. A missing metric is not a reason to discard substantial work.

Ask for clarification when the answer would materially change the record. Do not fill in employers, dates, tools, job duties or accomplishments just because people with a similar title often have them. Do not ask the person to prove facts they have already supplied.

## Suggest related skills, then let the person correct you

Use the activities they described to propose recognizable related capabilities. For example:

> From what you've described, related skills may include documentation, spreadsheet reporting, workflow coordination, onboarding and process improvement. Tell me which don't apply, which need different wording, and what I'm missing.

Make the actual suggestions specific to the person's answers. Training and calibration can lead to suggestions about instructional writing, coaching, quality standards and training effectiveness. Building a business tool can lead to questions about requirements, data imports, searching, reporting, integrations and automated follow-up. Using Slack or Docs is relevant software experience; do not silently turn that into workspace administration or software development.

After the person responds, record their accepted capabilities, corrections and rejected suggestions. If they approve a whole list, record that approval without making them confirm each item again. Use new answers to suggest another useful group of related skills or ask the next missing question. Do not re-propose rejected items without new information. Preserve the user's own descriptions when they communicate the work more accurately than a generic label.

## Build a reference the application rounds can use

Keep a readable career reference and skills matrix in the user's workspace. For each skill or capability retain:

| Field | What to save |
|---|---|
| Skill/tool/capability | The useful ability the user described or accepted |
| What they did | The actual activity or contribution, in their terms |
| Work context | Known employer, client, project, team or other setting |
| Supporting example | A concrete responsibility, implementation or accomplishment |
| Result/scope | Supplied metrics, qualitative outcomes, users or scope |

Connect capabilities to the known work context so selection can find a relevant example. Do not invent a context to complete a table. Keep related contributions from the same role distinguishable so the pipeline can select individual accomplishments instead of copying a whole job history.

This full reference supports many applications. The OSINT on each employer determines which capabilities and examples show how this person can solve that employer's problems. Do not turn the matrix into a compulsory inventory to include in every résumé or letter.

Use the reference to help the user take the next step. If they want opportunities, follow [job discovery](job-discovery.md) to search current openings from their capabilities and saved preferences. If their next step is unclear, ask whether they have a posting in mind or want help finding jobs. A supplied posting goes directly to the five-round application workflow; do not ask that question again when their request already answers it.

## Save and use user feedback

Use the [source and feedback instructions](sources.md). Save accepted additions, rejected suggestions, corrected facts, examples, presentation preferences and writing/process corrections. Keep the user's wording when summarizing it would lose meaning. Apply corrections directly to the record.

Before another suggestion batch or application, read the current record and relevant feedback. Selection, drafting, review and correction must receive and apply those answers. Record incremental changes rather than rebuilding the reference or requiring embeddings. Make sure the writing rounds use the saved record.
