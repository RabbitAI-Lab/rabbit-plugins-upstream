## The Refusal Ladder

A direct no is available in Japanese and is used — between friends, downward, and when the relationship can carry it. Everywhere else the refusal is graded, and the grade *is* the message.

| Weight | Form | What the reader hears |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ちょっと難しいかもしれません | Probably no, and there is room to negotiate |
| 2 | 難しいです / 厳しいです | No, softened |
| 3 | 今回は見送らせていただきます | No to this instance, the relationship continues |
| 4 | ご期待に添えず申し訳ございません | Final no with an apology |
| 5 | いたしかねます | Formal, structural no — the answer would be the same next time |
| 6 | お断りいたします | Explicit refusal; reserved for when softer forms were not heard |

Rules for using the ladder:

- **Name the constraint, not the preference.** リソースの都合で / 予算の関係で / 社内の方針で. A refusal attributed to a circumstance leaves the relationship intact; one attributed to a judgement does not.
- **One reason, one clause.** Three reasons read as excuse-making and invite the reader to solve them one at a time.
- **Offer the adjacent yes.** 今回は難しいのですが、9月以降であれば調整可能です. The counter-offer is what makes the refusal a negotiation instead of a door.
- **Do not apologise twice for the same no.** 申し訳ございません at the top and ご期待に添えず申し訳ございません at the bottom reads as insincere padding; pick the one that carries the weight.
- **難しい means no.** When a Japanese counterpart says 難しい, the correct reading is refusal, not difficulty. Treating it as an invitation to argue is the classic import from English negotiation.

## The Apology Ladder

Six forms, not synonyms, ordered by weight. Using one too light is dismissive; one too heavy is theatrical, and in Japanese theatrical apology reads as evasion.

| Weight | Form | Use |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | すみません | Spoken, minor, also means "excuse me" and "thank you" |
| 2 | ごめんなさい / ごめん | Personal, casual, never at work upward |
| 3 | 失礼しました | A procedural slip: wrong room, interrupted, wrong attachment |
| 4 | 申し訳ありません / 申し訳ございません | The standard business apology, written and spoken |
| 5 | 大変申し訳ございません | Something with consequences for the other party |
| 6 | 深くお詫び申し上げます | Formal, written, organisational; a 詫び状 or a public statement |

- **An apology without a remedy is not an apology.** The structure that closes an incident is: apologise → what happened in one line → what is being done and by when → apologise once more. The middle is where the credibility is.
- **申し訳ないです is contested.** 申し訳ない is an adjective, so ないです is grammatically the regular form and still reads as casual to many readers; 申し訳ありません is safe everywhere.
- **すみません carries three meanings** — apology, attention-getting, and thanks for a trouble taken. The third is the one non-natives miss: すみません on receiving a gift is thanks, not apology, and ありがとうございます is warmer where warmth is wanted.
- **Do not apologise for existing.** お忙しいところ恐縮ですが as an opener is correct once per message; three self-deprecations in one email transfer the discomfort to the reader.

## クッション言葉

The phrase that goes in front of a burden. One per sentence, never two.

| Cushion | Goes in front of |
|---|---|
| 恐れ入りますが | A request, a correction, an interruption |
| お手数ですが / お手数をおかけいたしますが | Work you are asking them to do |
| 恐縮ですが | A request that is genuinely inconvenient |
| 差し支えなければ | A question they may not want to answer |
| あいにくですが | Bad news you are delivering |
| 大変恐縮ですが | An escalation of the above |
| ご多忙のところ | Any request to a busy senior |
| 失礼ですが | A question about identity, name, age, affiliation |
| 申し上げにくいのですが | Something that will not be welcome |
| よろしければ | An offer, not a request |

The failure mode is stacking: 大変恐縮ではございますが、お手数をおかけいたしますが、差し支えなければ… reads as anxiety and buries the ask. One cushion, then the ask, immediately.

## Asking for Something

The shape of a request that gets a yes:

1. **The cushion** (one).
2. **The ask, concretely, with its size.** 数字だけ、10分ほどで — a request whose cost the reader can estimate is answerable; one whose cost is unknown gets deferred.
3. **The deadline as an absolute date with its 曜日.**
4. **The escape hatch.** 難しいようでしたら、そのようにおっしゃってください / 他の方をご紹介いただけるだけでも助かります.

The escape hatch is not politeness padding. Japanese social norms make an outright no costly, so a request with no low-cost exit gets answered with silence instead of with a refusal — and silence is unactionable for both parties (below).

Weight ladder for the ask itself: 〜てもらえる？ (casual) · 〜ていただけますか · 〜ていただけますでしょうか · 〜ていただけますと幸いです · 〜くださいますようお願い申し上げます (formal written). Going two rungs above the relationship reads as pressure, not politeness.

## Reading What Was Meant

The highest-value table in this file for anyone working with Japanese counterparts.

| They said | It usually means |
|---|---|
| 検討します | No, or "not now" — a real yes names a next step |
| 前向きに検討します | Slightly warmer no; still not a commitment |
| 社内で相談します | Genuinely unresolved, or a polite hold |
| 難しいです | No |
| ちょっと… (trailing off) | No, and they are giving you the chance not to make them say it |
| 大丈夫です | Ambiguous — accepting or declining. Ask which |
| 結構です | Same ambiguity, one register up |
| はい (repeated during your pitch) | I am listening. Not agreement (`speaking.md`) |
| 善処します | Bureaucratic no with a face-saving surface |
| また今度 | Not this time, and possibly not any time |
| 参考にさせていただきます | Received, not adopted |
| そうですね… (long pause) | Disagreement being assembled politely |
| 一応 | With reservations they are not stating |
| なるはやで | As soon as possible, with no deadline — always convert it to a date |

Two rules for using this table: **do not read every 検討します as a no** — the pattern is strong, not deterministic, and the way to resolve it is a concrete next step (では、来週の水曜までにお返事いただけますでしょうか), which is polite and forces the ambiguity to collapse. And **never say the subtext out loud** ("so that's a no?"): naming the indirection removes the exit the indirection existed to provide.

## Thanks

| Form | Weight |
|---|---|
| ありがと / サンキュ | Casual |
| ありがとうございます | Standard, present-tense: for something ongoing or just done |
| ありがとうございました | For something completed and closed |
| 助かります / 助かりました | Warm, peer, acknowledges effort |
| 恐れ入ります | Thanks for effort that cost them something |
| 感謝申し上げます | Formal, written |
| 誠にありがとうございました | Formal, closing |
| お世話になりました | Thanks for a relationship, at its end or at a milestone |

**The tense distinction is real**: ありがとうございました to someone still helping you closes the interaction prematurely. And a thank-you that names the specific thing (先日いただいた資料、そのまま社内提案に使えました) outperforms any amount of formal weight.

## Receiving a Compliment

Deflection is the norm and is not false modesty; it is the expected second half of the exchange.

- いえいえ、とんでもないです · そんなことないです · まだまだです · 恐れ入ります (formal)
- **Deflect once, then accept.** Refusing a compliment twice pushes the work back onto the giver.
- **ありがとうございます is increasingly acceptable** as a direct acceptance, especially among younger speakers and in international workplaces — and it is safer than a deflection that sounds rehearsed.
- **Never deflect on behalf of someone else** (a colleague, a team member, a child): うちの田中がよくやってくれました is the correct move, and deflecting their competence is a slight.

## Bad News

- **Channel first.** Anything with consequences goes to a call or a meeting, not to chat. Written notification follows and references the conversation (`speaking.md`).
- **Order**: apology → the fact → the cause in one clause → the remedy with its date → apology. The cause is one clause, because a long cause reads as an excuse.
- **Do not use 〜させていただく in an apology.** 説明させていただきますと reads as evasive at exactly the moment directness is required (`keigo.md`).
- **Give the recipient something to decide.** Bad news with two options attached is a working message; bad news alone transfers the whole problem.
- **Speed beats completeness.** A partial report today outranks a complete one on Friday; 現時点で判明していることをご報告いたします is a complete sentence and a legitimate message.

## 建前 and 本音

建前 is the socially appropriate position and 本音 is the actual one. Both are real, and neither is dishonest — the distinction is a shared protocol, not a deception.

- **建前 is the default in any formal or public setting**, and in 社外 communication almost always.
- **本音 surfaces in 飲み会, in 1-on-1s, in DMs, and after a relationship has been established** — which is a large part of why those settings exist.
- **The signal that 本音 is being offered**: a drop in register, a preface (正直なところ / ぶっちゃけ / ここだけの話), or a switch of channel.
- **When 本音 is offered, do not repeat it in a public channel.** Doing so ends the access permanently.
- **Do not force it.** 本音を聞かせてください from an outsider is a request the other party has no way to grant.

## Silence

Silence is a message in Japanese communication and has several distinct readings:

| Silence | Usually means |
|---|---|
| No reply to a request | No, delivered in the least costly way available |
| No reply after a proposal | Internal disagreement not yet resolved |
| A pause after your question in a meeting | Thinking, or waiting for the senior person to speak first |
| 既読 with no reply on LINE | Seen, answer coming later, or nothing to say (`chat.md`) |
| Silence in a negotiation | Frequently deliberate; filling it is a concession |

The correct response to a non-reply is not a chase that demands an answer, but a message that makes a no cheap: 行き違いでしたら恐れ入ります + resend + 難しいようでしたらその旨だけお知らせいただければ. In a meeting, name a person rather than asking the room, and only if the room's norms allow it.

## What Gets Written Down

Destinations, all in `memory-template.md`:

- **Anything that landed badly** — a refusal read as curt, a joke that did not travel, an apology read as evasive — → `## Pain Points`, with the cause, not just the event. This is the single highest-value section in the memory file for this domain, because pragmatic failures are invisible to the writer and obvious to the reader.
- **A person's pragmatic preferences** — they prefer directness, they dislike クッション言葉, they never answer chat and always answer email → their `## Recipients` row.
- **A phrasing that resolved something difficult** — the refusal that kept the relationship, the apology that closed the incident → `artifacts/template-<what>.md` with what it was for and what happened next.
