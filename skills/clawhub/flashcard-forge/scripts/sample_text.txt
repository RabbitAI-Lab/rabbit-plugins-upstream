Biology 101 — Chapter 3: Cellular Biology

The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life. All living
organisms are composed of one or more cells. Cells were discovered by Robert
Hooke in 1665 when he observed cork under a microscope.

There are two types of cells: prokaryotic cells and eukaryotic cells.
Prokaryotic cells lack a nucleus. Eukaryotic cells have a membrane-bound
nucleus.

The mitochondria is the powerhouse of the cell. It is responsible for producing
ATP through cellular respiration. Mitochondria have their own DNA, which
suggests they originated from free-living bacteria.

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants convert light energy into
chemical energy. It occurs in the chloroplasts of plant cells. The overall
equation is: 6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy produces C6H12O6 + 6O2.

What is DNA? DNA is deoxyribonucleic acid, the molecule that carries genetic
information in all living organisms. DNA was discovered by James Watson and
Francis Crick in 1953.

Unlike prokaryotes, eukaryotes have membrane-bound organelles. The nucleus,
mitochondria, and endoplasmic reticulum are all examples of organelles.

The cell membrane is defined as a selectively permeable barrier that surrounds
the cell. It controls what enters and exits the cell.

Osmosis refers to the diffusion of water across a semipermeable membrane.
Mitosis is a type of cell division that produces two identical daughter cells.

There are four phases of mitosis: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, and telophase.

Insufficient insulin results in diabetes. Smoking causes lung cancer and
increases the risk of heart disease.

DNA stands for deoxyribonucleic acid. RNA stands for ribonucleic acid.
