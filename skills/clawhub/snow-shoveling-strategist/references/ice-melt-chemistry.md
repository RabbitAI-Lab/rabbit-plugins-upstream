# Ice-Melt Chemistry — Reference

## The one thing everyone gets wrong: pavement temperature, not air

Melting performance depends on the temperature of the surface you're
treating — which lags air temperature by hours (thermal mass). A sunny
afternoon at −12 °C air can have pavement at −4 °C (rock salt works);
a cold night after a warm day can have pavement 5 °C colder than air.
Infrared thermometer or the back-of-hand-on-concrete estimate; when in
doubt, assume pavement = tonight's low.

## The melters

| Melter | Working floor (pavement) | Speed | Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NaCl (rock salt) | −9 °C | Moderate | $ | The default; chlorides + concrete/pet issues |
| CaCl₂ | −25 °C | Fastest (exothermic) | $$ | Generates heat as it dissolves; hygroscopic; paw irritant |
| MgCl₂ | −15 °C | Fast | $$ | Gentler on concrete, common "pet-safe" base |
| KCl | −4 °C | Slow | $$ | Mostly a blend filler |
| CMA | −5 °C | Slow | $$$ | Kindest to concrete + environment; weak in cold |
| Sand/ grit | any | None (traction) | $ | The honest answer below −25 °C or on vulnerable surfaces |
| Urea | −4 °C | Slow | $$$ | Agricultural runoff issues; skip |

Blends (rock salt + CaCl₂) are the commercial sweet spot: CaCl₂ melts and
releases heat, brine lowers the freeze point, NaCl finishes the volume.

## Surface compatibility

- **Concrete < 1 year:** NO chlorides. Curing concrete still has free
  water and unreacted lime; salt + freeze-thaw spalls the surface
  permanently. Use sand, or MgCl₂ sparingly at low rate.
- **Mature concrete:** rock salt OK at label rate; more causes pitting
  over years. Sealed concrete resists better.
- **Asphalt:** salt-tolerant; the risk is runoff, not the pavement.
- **Brick / flagstone / mortar joints:** chlorides + freeze-thaw burst
  mortar. CMA or sand.
- **Aluminum (ramps, rails):** chlorides corrode — sand.
- **Interlock with polymeric sand:** fine, but tracked-in residue
  accumulates in the joints.
- **Lawns/beds alongside:** any chloride at excess rates burns turf;
  spring burns show as the stripe where you over-applied.

## Rates (over-application is the norm, not the exception)

- Rock salt: ~30 g/m² (a coffee cup per ~10 m²)
- CaCl₂/MgCl₂: ~25 g/m²
- CMA: ~30 g/m²
- Sand: ~100 g/m² (it's traction, not chemistry)

Doubling the rate does NOT double melting speed — it doubles residue,
runoff, paw irritation, and concrete damage. Melting is limited by
temperature and brine dilution, not by quantity.

## Strategy

1. **Bulk snow first.** Melters work on thin bonded layers and glaze;
   through 20 cm of snow they drill tiny holes and vanish. Waste.
2. **Pre-treat** (thin frost/glaze events, BEFORE): prevents the ice-pave
   bond; the best use of product there is.
3. **Post-treat** (after clearing): attack the bonded remainder. Sun and
   traffic assist; below −10 °C expect partial results and re-apply after
   traffic polishes.
4. **Black ice on slopes/steps:** treat AND re-route. Don't trust a
   melting deadline on a morning schedule.
5. **Timing:** apply in the afternoon sun when pavement is warmest; a
   evening application works overnight against refreeze.

## Pets & plants

- Wipe paws after every walk on treated surfaces; boots for dogs that
  tolerate them.
- CaCl₂ and NaCl irritate paws and GI tract if licked in quantity;
  "pet-safe" labeled products are usually MgCl₂ or blends + a bitterant.
- Store sealed and dry (hygroscopic products pull water and cake).
- Shovel a treated-snow pile away from the lawn's crown if you care about
  the spring stripe.

## Antifreeze note (while we're on winter chemistry)

Ethylene glycol tastes sweet and kills dogs and children in teaspoons.
Clean up drips immediately; propylene glycol products are the less-toxic
substitute. Nothing about ice-melt skill matters more than this one.
