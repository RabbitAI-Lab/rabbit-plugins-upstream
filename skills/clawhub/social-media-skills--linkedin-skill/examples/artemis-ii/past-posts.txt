I spent a week reading every NASA caption from the Artemis I mission. The best detail was not in a headline.

The Orion capsule's heat shield came back with more charring than the models predicted, and the engineers wrote that down in plain words. That is the part of space flight I like most: someone writing down what did not go to plan.

A planetarium visitor asked me why the Moon looks bigger near the horizon.

It isn't. Measure it with a coin at arm's length and you get the same size high in the sky. The trick is in our heads, and nobody has a complete explanation for it yet.

I told her that, and she looked more interested than when I thought I had an answer.

Three things I learned writing about the James Webb Space Telescope for a year.

The mirror is gold because gold reflects infrared light well. The sunshield is the size of a tennis court. And most of the work was done years before launch, by people whose names are in no press release.

Which part of a big project do you think gets the least credit?

---
I get asked how to start reading about astronomy without a science degree.

Pick one object you can see tonight. The Moon, Jupiter, the Pleiades. Read one good page about it, then go outside and look. Repeat next week with another one.

It works better than any book list I could give you.

Our club ran its first public telescope night in two years. 140 people came, in the cold, on a Tuesday.

The most common question was not about black holes. It was "can I look again?"

A small correction to something I wrote last month: Saturn's rings are not solid. They are billions of pieces of ice and rock, from dust grains to chunks the size of a house.

Thanks to the reader who emailed me. I would rather be corrected than be quoted wrong.
