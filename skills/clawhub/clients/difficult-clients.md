# Difficult Clients — Rescue or Exit

Scope: what to do when the relationship, not the work, is the problem. Money problems are `getting-paid.md`; scope problems are `scope.md`. This file is for behaviour.

Read the client's `roster/<client-slug>.md`, their `contact-log/<client-slug>.md` and their row in `## Roster` before diagnosing anything — the question "is this a pattern or a bad month" is answerable only from the record.

## Diagnose Before Deciding

The disagreement in the field (SKILL.md's Where Experts Disagree) resolves on one distinction: **process problems are usually yours; behaviour problems are not fixable by better process.**

| Symptom | Usually a process problem | Usually a behaviour problem |
|---|---|---|
| Endless revisions | No revision cap, no consolidated feedback, no definition of done | Feedback that changes direction to assert authority, after approvals |
| Requests at all hours | No working agreement, or one you have not held | Contact after you have restated hours, twice |
| Constant scope pressure | Vague deliverables, no change-order path | Requests continuing after a change order was accepted and paid |
| Slow approvals | Wrong approver mapped, no deadline clause | Deliberate delay used to extract concessions near your deadlines |
| Disputing invoices | Poor invoice hygiene, no PO, no milestone acceptance | Disputes that appear only when payment is due, on work already approved |
| Rudeness in messages | A stressed person having a bad week | A pattern across weeks, or aimed at anyone junior |
| Anything else | Check whether the agreement covers it; if it does not, that is the fix | Two dated instances of the same behaviour after it was raised once |

Fix your side first, always. It costs a week, it works surprisingly often, and if it does not, you now have a clean record showing the problem was not the process.

## The Rescue Plan

One meeting, one document, one review date. Written to `artifacts/rescue-<client>.md`.

1. **Ask for the conversation directly**, without a list of grievances: "I want to reset how we're working — can we take 30 minutes?" A rescue framed as a complaint gets a defence, not a change.
2. **Open with what you will change.** Whatever your side of the diagnosis was, name it first and concretely. It makes the rest hearable.
3. **State the two or three things that must change**, as operating changes rather than character notes: requests in one channel, one consolidated feedback round, approvals within three days, contact within working hours.
4. **Write it down and send it the same day** as a short revised working agreement, with the effective date.
5. **Set a review date, 30 days out, in `## Due`.** A rescue without a review date is a hope.
6. **On the review date, decide.** Improved → continue and note it. Unchanged → exit, and the fact that you tried, in writing, makes the exit clean and defensible.

Do not renegotiate price during a rescue. Mixing money into a behaviour conversation lets both sides trade one against the other and neither gets fixed.

## Types, and What Each Actually Needs

| Type | What you see | What works | What does not |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The micromanager** | Constant check-ins, wants to watch the work | More frequent scheduled reporting — they are buying certainty, not control. Give them a dashboard and the check-ins stop | Resisting the contact; anxiety escalates when starved |
| **The ghost** | Disappears for weeks, then urgent | Written decision deadlines with default outcomes: "unless I hear otherwise by Thursday, I proceed with B" (`delivery.md`) | Waiting. Their silence becomes your delay |
| **The committee** | Every decision reopens with new people | One named approver in writing; others' input arrives through them (`stakeholders.md`) | Serving each stakeholder individually |
| **The bargain hunter** | Every invoice and quote is negotiated | Fixed published prices and no exceptions; the negotiation stops when it stops working | One discount "to keep the peace" — it converts every future invoice into an opening bid |
| **The crisis generator** | Everything is urgent, always outside hours | Rush pricing applied every time, without exception (`pricing.md`) | Absorbing urgency; it is infinite and free |
| **The scope artist** | Fluent, friendly, endless small additions | The ledger, shown at the fourth request (`scope.md`) | Refusing each one individually; you become the obstacle |
| **The credit-taker** | Presents your work as theirs internally | Usually harmless and sometimes useful — a champion who owns your work defends it. Ensure the economic buyer knows where it came from | Fighting for public credit; it costs the relationship and wins nothing |
| **The abuser** | Insults, shouting, contempt, aimed at you or at juniors | Nothing. This is the immediate-exit row | Tolerating it once; it always recurs and it always escalates |

## The Cost of Keeping Them

Make it a number before deciding, because "difficult" is an emotion and this is a business decision.

**True effective rate = fee ÷ (delivery hours + unpaid extras + meeting time + chasing time + the hours lost to dread).** That last term is real: a client who occupies your attention on the days you are not working for them is consuming capacity you cannot sell.

Worked example: a 3,000 EUR/month retainer, nominally 20 hours. Actual: 20 delivery + 6 unbilled extras + 5 meetings + 2 chasing = 33 hours → 91 EUR/h against a 150 EUR/h nominal rate. If the replacement client pays 150 EUR/h at 20 hours, the difficult client is costing roughly 1,000 EUR a month plus the capacity to serve anyone else.

Then check the other side honestly: what share of income are they (`portfolio.md`), how long would replacement take, and what does your pipeline actually look like. Exiting a client you cannot replace is a decision to take a pay cut — sometimes the right one, but it should be chosen rather than discovered.

## When to Exit Immediately

No rescue, no review period:

- Abuse, discrimination, or hostility toward anyone on your side, once.
- Being asked to do something illegal, fraudulent, or dishonest — including backdating documents, falsifying results, or misrepresenting who did the work.
- Repeated non-payment after the ladder has been fully run (`getting-paid.md`).
- Demonstrable dishonesty toward you: a denied agreement that exists in writing, a rewritten scope after acceptance.
- Anything that puts your professional standing or licence at risk.

The remedy in every one of these is the exit procedure, executed calmly and in writing. Emotional exits cost money and referrals.

## The Exit Procedure

Order matters, and the money comes first:

1. **Invoice everything outstanding, including work in progress**, before any mention of ending. An exit announced with an unpaid balance turns collections into a negotiation.
2. **Check the notice period** in the agreement, and honour it. If none is written, 30 days is the usual professional norm for ongoing work; a project mid-phase ends at the phase boundary where possible.
3. **Write the handover pack** — status, credentials as pointers, files, decisions, what a successor needs (`offboarding.md`). It is the difference between "professional" and "difficult" in the story they will tell.
4. **Send the message** (below), once, in writing.
5. **Hold the date.** Counter-offers, apologies and sudden improvements are common; if the exit was for behaviour, the improvement lasts about six weeks.
6. **Revoke and return.** Their access removed on your side, your access removed on theirs, files delivered, credential pointers deleted from your notes.
7. **Collect the final invoice** on the ladder like any other, and do not let the ending become a reason to skip rungs.

## The Exit Message

Short, factual, no grievance list. Grievances invite rebuttal and turn an ending into an argument.

> "I've decided to wrap up our work together. My last day on the project will be 30 August, which gives us four weeks to hand over cleanly. I'll deliver the handover pack and everything in progress by then, and I'll invoice the balance on completion. It's been good working on X — I'm glad we got it live. If it's useful, I'm happy to recommend someone who'd be a good fit for what's next."

- One reason, or none. "It isn't the right fit for how I work" is complete and unanswerable.
- Never itemise their failings, even when invited to. Nothing good comes of it and it will be forwarded.
- Offer a referral only if you would genuinely make it. Sending a difficult client to a peer without warning them costs you a peer.
- Never exit in the message where you are angry. Write it, wait a night, send it in the morning (SKILL.md Traps).

## Afterwards

- **Write the post-mortem** to `artifacts/postmortem-<client>.md`: the first observable signal and its date, what you tried, what it cost, and the qualification question that would have caught it (`pipeline.md`).
- **Update the decline list** if the pattern is recognisable at lead stage. This is the entire return on a bad engagement.
- **Keep the roster row** with status `fired` or `past` and a one-line reason. Difficult clients come back, often through a new contact and a new name, and the row is what stops the second round.
- **Tell the champion, not the internet.** Public complaint about a named client ends other relationships, permanently, in a business that runs on referral.

**Write before you move on:** the diagnosis and rescue terms go to `artifacts/rescue-<client>.md` with a review date in `## Due` and a `## Boxes` line; the client's `Health` cell in `## Roster` changes with its date; every incident goes to `contact-log/<client-slug>.md` with the observable behaviour, not the impression; an exit sets the roster status to `fired` or `past` with the reason, closes the project file with `status: closed`, and produces `artifacts/postmortem-<client>.md`; any new decline signal goes into the decline list you keep in `## Declined Leads` reasoning and, when it generalises, into `config.yaml` under `risk_posture`.
