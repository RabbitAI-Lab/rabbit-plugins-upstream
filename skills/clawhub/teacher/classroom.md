# Classroom — Routines, Behaviour, and the First Days

Behaviour is a design problem before it is a discipline problem. Almost every recurring disruption is a missing routine, an unclear task, or a lesson pitched where a third of the room cannot enter it.

**Before planning behaviour work with a named group**, read `## Dynamics`, `## Seating` and `## What Works With This Group` in that group's class file and in `memory.md`. A technique that worked with one class and failed with another is two records, not a contradiction — and the school's escalation ladder, recorded under the behaviour posture preference area, governs every step below.

## The First Days

The first two weeks set what is normal for the year, and re-setting it in November costs five times as much.

1. **Teach content on day one.** A day of rules and icebreakers signals that this room is not primarily for work. Teach something real, with a check, in the first lesson.
2. **Run a prerequisite check in the first week** (`planning.md`). It tells you the actual range in the room, which is almost never the range on the data sheet.
3. **Teach three routines to fluency** before anything else: entry, getting attention, and transitions. Everything else can be added later.
4. **Be precise, not severe.** Warm and highly specific beats stern and vague. The common advice to be harsh early confuses precision with coldness; what students actually calibrate to is whether the stated standard is enforced consistently.
5. **Learn names by the end of week one.** A correction from someone who does not know your name is a correction of a category, not a person, and it lands that way.
6. Record what you learn about the group in the class file as you go: the pairs that do not work, the seating that settles them, the starter format that gets 100% response.

## Routines Are Taught, Not Announced

A routine is content: model it, practise it, correct it, re-practise it. A poster is not a routine, and a routine that was never rehearsed does not exist under pressure.

Routines worth building, in order of minutes recovered:

| Routine | What it specifies | Recovered |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | Where to sit, what is out, the starter already on the board | 3-5 min per lesson |
| Attention signal | One signal, the same every time, and what students do on it | 1-2 min per use, several times a lesson |
| Distributing and collecting | Who moves, in what order, in what direction | 1-3 min per event |
| Transitions between tasks | The instruction sequence and the count | 60-90s each, ×6 per lesson |
| Asking for help | Hand, card, or list, and what to do while waiting | Removes the queue at the desk |
| Finishing early | The named extension task, permanent and known | Removes the largest single source of low-level disruption |
| Packing away and dismissal | By table, on the teacher's signal, never the bell | Ends the lesson on your terms |

Rehearse a routine that decays rather than escalating consequences over it. Decay is normal after holidays and after any timetable change.

## The Transition Budget

Six transitions at 60-90 seconds each is 6-9 minutes a lesson: over a year, roughly a week of teaching. Compress with three moves:

- **Instructions before movement, always.** Any instruction given during movement is heard by nobody.
- **One instruction at a time, in the imperative, with the finish line named**: "Pens down. Look at me. In thirty seconds, page 42, question 1." Multi-clause instructions produce a room of students asking each other what was said.
- **Count down and mean it.** A countdown that ends without the state being reached teaches that countdowns are decorative.

## Attention Signals

- One signal, taught in week one, used identically every time. Changing signals resets the routine to zero.
- Wait for 100%. Talking over the last two conversations concedes that the signal is optional; that concession is remembered.
- Do not raise your volume to compete — lowering it usually works faster and costs nothing.
- Give a positive close after the signal ("thank you, table three") rather than a criticism of the slowest.

## The Least Invasive Intervention Ladder

Use the smallest intervention that works, and escalate one step at a time. Skipping to a public consequence turns a two-second correction into a five-minute contest for the room (SKILL.md Rule 7).

| Step | Move | Cost to the lesson |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Non-verbal: eye contact, a gesture, a pause | ~0 |
| 2 | Proximity: stand near, keep teaching | ~0 |
| 3 | Anonymous group correction: "we're all writing" | 2s |
| 4 | Private word at the desk, quietly, with take-up time | 10-20s |
| 5 | Named expectation with a choice: "pen down and start, or we speak at the end" | 5s, public |
| 6 | Named consequence per school policy, delivered flatly, then move on | 10s, public |
| 7 | Removal per school policy, with the follow-up conversation booked | The rest of the lesson for that student |

- **Take-up time**: give the instruction, then look away and move on. Standing over a student waiting for compliance forces a public choice between obeying and saving face, and adolescents often choose face (Rogers).
- **Correct the behaviour, name the expectation, do not narrate history.** "Book open, question 3" outperforms "you always do this".
- Keep positive-to-corrective interactions at 4:1 or better (PBIS guidance). Teachers consistently over-estimate their own ratio; count for one lesson.
- Deliver a consequence with a flat affect and no lecture. Anger converts a consequence into a relationship event, which is what escalation is made of.

## Scripts That Defuse

| Situation | Script | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Answering back | "I hear you. Book open, question 3 — we'll talk at the end." | Partial agreement plus redirection; refuses the argument without a rebuttal |
| Refusal | "You can start now, or we sort it at break. Your call." | Two acceptable options, both compliant; removes the audience contest |
| The audience is watching | Deliver it privately, at the desk, at conversation volume | The audience is the fuel; take it away |
| "It wasn't me" | "It might not have been. Right now I need question 3 started." | Does not litigate; keeps the lesson |
| Escalating, raised voice | Lower yours, slow down, increase physical distance, offer a way out that is not surrender | Matching volume guarantees escalation |
| Personal insult | Do not respond in the moment; after the lesson follow policy and write the account — date, what was said, what you did — into that student's rows in the class file | An in-the-moment response is a contest you cannot win in front of thirty people |
| Phone out | The routine states where phones live; name it once, then policy | Improvising a phone rule per incident is a daily negotiation (`ai_policy` and the phone policy live in the preference areas) |

## Consequences

- **Certain and small beats severe and rare.** Predictability is the mechanism; escalation is not.
- The consequence must be one you will apply every time to every student, or it corrodes the whole system.
- Never collective punishment: it punishes the compliant, who then stop being compliant.
- Never remove the thing that is helping — break, food, the practical, the trip — where the behaviour is a communication of unmet need.
- Follow through visibly. An unenforced detention costs more than never setting it.

## Repair and Restorative Conversations

After a removal or a serious incident, before the student is back in the room, two minutes at the door:

1. What happened, in their words — you listen, without correcting the narrative yet.
2. Who was affected and how.
3. What needs to happen now, agreed and specific.
4. A clean slate stated explicitly: this lesson starts at zero.

The clean-slate statement is the part that gets skipped and the part that determines whether the next lesson starts with the same student in the same posture.

## Seating

- **Assigned seating, always, and revisit it on a cadence** (a `## Due` row, typically each half-term). "Sit where you like" is a decision to let the group's social structure set the learning conditions.
- Design by interaction, not by attainment: the strongest-with-weakest pairing helps neither by default. Pair by who works when adjacent.
- Front rows and the teacher's circulation path go to the students who need most contact — not as a punishment, and never announced as one.
- Record the plan and the reason in the class file. Next term's plan starts from what worked, not from memory.

## The Specific Cases

| Case | Move |
|---|---|
| Chronic low-level chatter | It is a routine gap, not a discipline gap: tighten entry, starter and transitions before any consequence |
| One student destabilises the room | Change the conditions first — seating, task entry point, when they get your attention. Then a private conversation about a specific, small agreement |
| The class is fine until group work | Roles and interdependence undefined (`engagement.md`) |
| Disruption clusters at the end | Independent practice ran out or was never planned; the permanent extension task is the fix |
| Disruption clusters at the start | The starter is not on the board before entry, or entry was never taught |
| A student who never disrupts and never works | The quietest and most-missed case. It is an attainment and welfare question, not a behaviour one (`struggling.md`) |
| Behaviour worse with a substitute | The routine belongs to you, not the room — write the routine into the cover notes |
| One class only, same students fine elsewhere | Timetable slot, seating, or the relationship. Ask three of them separately; observe a colleague with the same group (`observation.md`) |

## Covering Someone Else's Class

- Take a task that requires no prior knowledge and produces a visible product to hand back.
- Set the standard in the first minute: seating plan enforced, entry routine stated, work started within three minutes.
- Do not import their teacher's routines you cannot enforce, and do not invent new consequences you cannot follow up.
- Leave a written handover of what was completed and who did what. It belongs with the cover notes for the regular teacher, **not in `~/Clawic/data/`** — it is their class and their record, and it is worthless a week later. It is the only reason they can pick the class up cleanly.

## When It Is Not Behaviour

Before treating a pattern as behaviour, rule out these, in this order:

1. **Access**: the work is above the student's current reading or prior knowledge, so refusal is protective (`differentiation.md`).
2. **A need**: an unmet or unrecorded accommodation, sensory environment, hearing or vision, medication timing.
3. **Something outside school**: a sudden change is a signal, not a character trait — and some signals belong in the Red Flags table of `SKILL.md`, not in a behaviour log.
4. **The lesson**: no entry point, no check, too much input, nothing to do when finished.

**When a behaviour approach works or fails with a specific group**, write it to `## What Works With This Group` in `memory.md` — technique, group, observed effect. **When a routine, seating rationale or behaviour plan is settled**, it goes to `~/Clawic/data/teacher/artifacts/<kebab-name>.md` with its `## Boxes` line, and the seating rotation becomes a `## Due` row. **When a specific student's pattern needs a plan**, it belongs in that class file's `## Interventions` with a review date, never as a character note (`memory-template.md`).
