# Struggling — The Student Who Is Failing

"Failing" is a symptom with five common causes, and each has a different move. Intervening before the cause is identified is how a term of effort produces no change.

**Before starting an intervention**, read that student's rows in the class file — `## Roster`, `## Needs`, `## Interventions` — and check whether something was already tried and reviewed. Repeating a failed intervention is common and reads to the student as being unheard.

## Diagnose the Cause First

Spend one lesson gathering evidence before doing anything. The five causes and the evidence that distinguishes them:

| Cause | Evidence that points here | First move |
|---|---|---|
| **Attendance** | Missed sessions against topics missed; the gaps map to absences | Attendance route first; teaching around it does not work (below) |
| **Prerequisite gap** | Fails current work, also fails a check on the prior objective | Teach the prerequisite, in a slot, not in the margins of the lesson |
| **Comprehension in the lesson** | Present, prerequisite intact, still cannot follow | Access: reading load, pace, missing accommodation, seating (`differentiation.md`) |
| **Production** | Understands in discussion and in checks, produces nothing on paper | Output barrier, organisation, or refusal — different again |
| **Outside the classroom** | Sudden change across subjects, affect, hygiene, attendance and marks moving together | Pastoral, same day; not a subject intervention (Red Flags in `SKILL.md`) |

How to gather it in one lesson: a five-item diagnostic covering the prerequisite and the current objective; two minutes reading their last three pieces of work for pattern rather than mark; one private thirty-second question — "what is the part that stops making sense?"; and a check with the pastoral or form tutor for whether this is subject-specific or global. That last question changes everything and takes one message.

## Attendance

- **Chronic absence is conventionally defined as missing 10% or more of sessions** — roughly one day a fortnight, or about 18 days a year. That threshold matters because it is the point at which attainment effects become visible and reliable.
- Map absences to content: a student who missed the two lessons on the prerequisite has a content problem that looks like a comprehension problem. That map takes five minutes and redirects the whole intervention.
- Never assume the reason. Illness, anxiety, caring responsibilities, transport, bullying and disengagement all present as absence and none share a remedy.
- Catch-up must be selective and specific: the two load-bearing objectives, on paper, with a worked example — never "here is everything you missed", which is received as a punishment for being ill.
- Attendance is a whole-school system in most settings. Your job is to make the record accurate, flag the pattern early, and be the reason the student wants to be in your lesson.

## Prerequisite Gaps

- Test the gap explicitly rather than inferring it. A five-item check on the prior objective settles in three minutes what a term of guessing will not.
- Teach the gap in a dedicated slot — an intervention session, a starter sequence, a set of worked examples to do at home with the answers — not by slowing the whole class, which trades one student's gap for thirty students' pace.
- Gaps compound: fractions block rearrangement, decoding blocks comprehension, notation blocks everything downstream. Fix the earliest gap in the chain, not the most recent symptom.
- Give the student the map: "these three things, in this order, and then the current work is accessible". Students tolerate a lot of remediation when they can see the end of it.

## Comprehension in the Lesson

Work through the access checks before concluding the material is too hard:

1. Can they read the material at the speed the lesson assumes? Reading level is the most under-checked barrier there is.
2. Is an accommodation missing, unrecorded, or being applied only on request (`differentiation.md`)?
3. Hearing, vision, seating, sight lines to the board?
4. Is the pace of new elements above four per chunk (`explaining.md`)?
5. Are they copying rather than processing? Copying is the visible form of overload.

## Work Not Produced

Understands, cannot or will not produce. Separate the three:

- **Organisation**: nothing is recorded, nothing is found, deadlines are invisible. Fix with structure — a checklist, a single place for the task, a check at the door, a two-minute weekly review with you.
- **Output barrier**: handwriting, spelling anxiety, processing speed. Change the medium (voice, bullets, laptop, scribe) and hold the cognitive demand.
- **Refusal**: producing means being judged, and not producing preserves "I could have". This is the one that responds to a private, low-stakes success and a guaranteed short first task — not to consequences, which confirm the model.

## Life Outside the Classroom

- Sudden change across several subjects at once, changes in affect, hygiene, weight, attendance and engagement: this is a pastoral referral the same day, with a record of what was **observed**, never what was inferred.
- Anything in the Red Flags table of `SKILL.md` — a disclosure, self-harm, a threat, a safeguarding concern — stops everything and follows the school's route immediately. Never in a personal file, never promised as a secret.
- Your job in these cases is a reliable, low-demand classroom: predictable routine, an achievable task, and one adult who notices. That is a real intervention, and often the one that matters.

## The Intervention Itself

An intervention is a hypothesis with a review date, not a folder.

1. **One cause, one change.** Two simultaneous changes make the result uninterpretable.
2. **Small and daily beats large and weekly.** Ten minutes of the right practice four times a week outperforms an hour on Friday.
3. **Name the measure before starting**: the check that will tell you it worked, and its current value.
4. **Set the review date now** — 3-4 weeks is usually right; a `## Due` row so it actually happens.
5. **Tell the student the plan, in their words**: what is being tried, why, for how long, and what success looks like. Interventions done to students produce compliance at best.
6. **Tell the guardian** at the start, not at the review: the person goes in the shared `~/Clawic/data/contacts/contacts.md` and what was agreed goes in that student's rows in the class file (`parents.md`).
7. At the review: keep, change, or escalate. An intervention with no visible effect after four weeks is evidence about the diagnosis, not about the student's effort.

## The Quiet Student Nobody Flags

The most-missed case in every school: attends, behaves, produces just enough, and is invisible because nothing about them costs the teacher anything.

- Use the check-response data, not your impression of who is struggling. Whole-class response formats surface these students; hands-up conceals them by design (`checking.md`).
- Scan the mark book for flat, mid-range attainment with no strong pieces: that pattern is often a student managing rather than learning.
- Thirty seconds of specific private attention a fortnight is disproportionately effective here, precisely because it is unexpected.

## The Bright Underperformer

- Distinguish boredom from avoidance. Boredom presents as fast, careless, correct-but-minimal work; avoidance presents as unfinished work on exactly the tasks that could be judged.
- For boredom: extension by depth, a genuine responsibility, and honesty about what the current work is for (`differentiation.md`).
- For avoidance: the risk of failing at something they are supposed to be good at. Lower the stakes, require drafts, praise the process, and never invoke potential — "you could do so much better" is the sentence that maintains the pattern.

## Escalation and Referral

Escalate when: two documented interventions have produced no measurable change; the concern spans several subjects; a suspected unidentified need is blocking access; attendance crosses the chronic threshold; or anything in the Red Flags table appears.

What a referral needs to carry, in this order: the observable evidence (marks, checks, attendance, dated examples of work), what was tried and for how long, what changed and what did not, the guardian contact made, and the specific question you are asking the specialist. A referral that says "concerned about X" with no evidence goes to the back of a long queue.

## When Nothing Works

- Re-examine the diagnosis before increasing the dose. Four weeks of no movement usually means the cause was misidentified, not that the intervention was too small.
- Check whether the student agrees with the plan. An intervention the student has quietly opted out of will not work at any intensity.
- Look for the constraint you cannot see: sleep, hunger, a job, caring, a phone at 3am, a relationship in the room.
- Accept the limit honestly. A teacher is one adult in a system, and some causes are outside the classroom entirely. Making the referral, keeping the record accurate, and keeping the lesson reliable is the whole job in those cases — and is not failure.

**When an intervention starts**, write the row in that class file's `## Interventions`: concern, date started, what was tried, and the review date — and add the review to `## Due` so it happens. **At the review**, update the same row with the measured result and the decision. **When a guardian is contacted about it**, the person goes in shared `~/Clawic/data/contacts/contacts.md` and what was agreed goes in the class file. **Never** record a disclosure, a diagnosis, or an inference about a family in any of these files (`memory-template.md`).
