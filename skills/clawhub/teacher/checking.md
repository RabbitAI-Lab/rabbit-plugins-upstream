# Checking — Formative Assessment That Changes the Next Five Minutes

Formative assessment is not a small test. It is a check whose result you act on immediately; a check you record and move past is a quiz wearing a different name.

## The Decision Rule

Every check produces a count, and the count selects one of three actions. Same thresholds everywhere in this skill (SKILL.md Rule 4; Rosenshine's ~80% guided-practice success rate, Wiliam's hinge threshold):

| Result | Action | Why not otherwise |
|---|---|---|
| **≥80% correct** | Move on; pick up the few individually during practice | Stopping the class for 4 students wastes 24 students' lesson |
| **50-79%** | Split: reteach to the group that missed while the rest extend | Whole-class reteach bores the majority and they stop attending to reteaching generally |
| **<50%** | Whole-class reteach in a *different* representation, then re-check | Continuing builds the next idea on a foundation most of the room does not have |

A check with no pre-decided action is theatre. Write the three branches into the plan before the lesson (`planning.md`).

## Hinge Questions

A hinge question is the checkpoint the lesson hinges on: it takes under a minute to answer, under 30 seconds to interpret across the class, and its wrong options are diagnostic.

Rules for writing one:

- **Every distractor is a real misconception**, each one identifying a different one. A distractor nobody picks is a wasted option.
- **Right for the wrong reason must be impossible.** If a student can arrive at the correct answer by a wrong route, redesign the item — this is the single most common flaw.
- **Answerable in under a minute** by a student who understands. Longer means you are measuring speed, working memory or reading.
- **Interpretable at a glance**: with letter cards or whiteboards you should be able to read the room in 20 seconds and know which reteach to run.
- Place them at the seams: after the first input chunk, and before independent practice. Two per lesson is usually right; five turns a lesson into a quiz.

Example shape, chemistry: *A candle burns in a sealed flask. The mass of the sealed flask afterwards is: (A) less — the wax was used up · (B) the same · (C) more — oxygen was added · (D) less — gas escaped.* A picks conservation-of-mass-by-consumption, C picks one-way mass addition, D picks a sealed-system error. Each wrong answer names a different reteach.

## Whole-Class Response Formats

Response rate is the number that matters. Volunteers tell you about volunteers.

| Format | Response rate | Cost | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mini-whiteboards | 100% | 30-60s per question, needs kit | Short constructed answers; you see the *working*, not just the choice |
| Letter cards A-D | 100% | Instant, near-zero kit | Hinge MCQs; least writing, fastest read |
| Digital poll | 100% | 60-90s with login friction | A record you keep; large or online classes |
| Fingers 1-4 to the chest | 100% | Instant | Quick MCQ where devices and boards are unavailable; low visibility to peers |
| Everybody writes, then sample | 100% think, sample read | 2-4 min | Extended reasoning; sample 5 and read while circulating |
| Choral response | 100% | Instant | Facts that must become automatic; useless for reasoning |

- Whiteboards beat cards when the wrong *method* matters; cards beat whiteboards when speed matters and the distractors already encode the diagnoses.
- **Show simultaneously, on a signal.** Staggered reveals let students copy, and the count becomes fiction.
- Never let a format become a compliance ritual — if the same three students always show the same wrong answer and nothing follows, the check has stopped functioning.

## Questioning Technique

- **Wait time 3-5 seconds** after asking, and a further 3 seconds after the answer before responding (Rowe). Typical unmanaged wait time is under a second, which selects for students who are fast rather than students who are right. Extended wait time lengthens answers, raises the number of volunteers, and increases student-to-student responses.
- **Ask, pause, name** — never name first. Naming first excuses everyone else from thinking.
- **Say the question once.** Repeating and rephrasing while they think restarts the processing clock.
- **Probe every answer, correct or not**: "how do you know?", "what would change if…?", "who can add to that?". Probing only wrong answers teaches the class that a probe means you are wrong.
- **No opt-out**: a student who says "I don't know" gets the answer from another student, then is returned to and asked to restate it. The norm being taught is that not knowing is temporary, not an exit.
- Bounce the answer: "Priya said X — Tom, do you agree, and why?" This turns one response into whole-class thinking.

## Cold Call Without Fear

Cold call raises participation across a term when it is predictable and safe; it damages the room when it is a surprise or a sanction.

- Announce the practice and the reason: "I call on people, not hands — everyone gets thinking time first."
- **Always give think time or a pair-share before the first cold call** of a sequence.
- Start with questions that have a floor: retrieval facts and read-aloud before opinions.
- Never cold call as punishment for inattention. The moment it is a sanction it becomes a threat, and the compliant response is silence.
- Distribute deliberately — a tally on the seating plan for one lesson usually reveals a distribution nobody would defend.

## Exit Tickets

- **Three questions maximum, 3-5 minutes**: one on today's objective at the level it was taught, one on the prerequisite, one transfer or "what is still unclear".
- Collect at the door. Uncollected reflection is a diary entry.
- Sort into three piles as you read: got it, partial, not yet. The piles are tomorrow's starter, tomorrow's grouping, and tomorrow's reteach — that is the entire point.
- Reading 28 exit tickets takes about 5 minutes if the questions are short. If it takes 20, the ticket was too long.

## Circulating

The only check that sees process rather than product.

- Circulate with a purpose stated in the plan: "I am looking at step 2, the sign change".
- Read work, do not scan behaviour. Standing behind a student for four seconds reading their line of work is worth more than a lap of the room.
- Track what you see against the seating plan; three tallies on the same error is a whole-class reteach, not three individual corrections.
- Intervene at the smallest useful level: point at the line, say nothing, walk on. A full explanation to one student during independent practice costs the other 27 your attention.

## Diagnostic Multiple Choice

- **Three options is enough.** Meta-analysis finds three-option items perform as well as four or five (Rodriguez 2005) — writing a fourth plausible distractor is usually the moment implausible filler appears.
- Every distractor traceable to a real error: an arithmetic slip, a reversed relation, the previous topic's rule misapplied.
- No "all of the above", no "none of the above", no negatives in the stem unless the negative is the thing being tested.
- Keep options parallel in length and grammar — the longest, most qualified option is the answer more often than chance, and students know it.
- Items that survive and discriminate get reused; record them with their statistics in `assessments/<year>.md` (`assessment.md` defines `p` and `D`).

## Self and Peer Assessment

Useful for volume, unreliable as measurement.

- Self-assessment against a checklist of observable features works; self-assessment against "how well did you do?" does not — students who cannot yet do the task cannot yet judge it.
- Peer assessment works when the criteria are concrete, an exemplar has been marked together, and the feedback is against a checklist rather than a grade. Peers grading peers on a scale produces friendship marks and resentment in equal measure.
- Both are formative only. Neither belongs in a recorded mark (`grading.md`).

## What Checking Cannot Tell You

- **In-lesson performance is not learning.** Everything is easy at the end of the lesson that taught it; the delayed check is the one that means something (Bjork). Judge retention on a starter a week later, never on the exit ticket.
- **Confidence self-reports correlate weakly with accuracy**, and the weakest students are the most miscalibrated. A show of fingers on "how confident are you" is a mood reading.
- **Silence is not agreement**; nodding is not understanding. Both cost nothing to produce.
- A check tells you *how many*; only reading the work tells you *why*. Do both in the same lesson.
