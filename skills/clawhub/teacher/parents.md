# Parents and Guardians — Communication That Ends Well

Almost every difficult guardian conversation is about one of three things: they heard bad news for the first time too late, they think their child is being treated unfairly, or they are frightened about the future. Address the actual one.

**Before any guardian contact**, read that student's rows in the class file and the person's row in `~/Clawic/data/contacts/contacts.md`: the preferred channel, what was agreed last time, and the date. Contacting a guardian without knowing what was promised three weeks ago is how a routine call becomes a complaint.

## The First Contact Is Not the Bad News

- **Make an early positive contact** with the guardians of any student you expect to need a conversation later. Two minutes in week two buys the entire relationship. When the difficult call comes, it is the second call, not the first.
- **Nothing at a conference or on a report should be new.** A guardian hearing at parents' evening that their child has been failing since September is right to be angry, and the anger is about the delay, not the marks.
- Set the cadence deliberately: for at-risk students, a fortnightly contact — even a single line — is a `## Due` row and it prevents nearly every escalation.

## Writing the Message

Structure, in this order:

1. **Fact, dated and observable.** "In the last three weeks, four of six pieces were not submitted" — not "poor attitude".
2. **Consequence, stated plainly.** What it means for the course, the grade, the exam.
3. **What the school is already doing.** The seat change, the catch-up sheet, the intervention with its review date.
4. **What is asked of them, specifically and small.** "A ten-minute check on Sunday that the homework is done" beats "please support at home".
5. **The next contact, dated.** Every message ends with when they will hear from you again.

Rules: short paragraphs; no jargon, no acronyms, no specification codes; the child's name, not "your son"; never sarcasm and never a joke that could be read cold; and never a mark or a comparison to another child. Read it once as if you were tired, worried and reading it on a phone at 11pm — because you are.

## Templates by Situation

Shapes, not sentences to paste. Adapt to the family and to `student_naming` in anything stored.

| Situation | Opening | What must be in it |
|---|---|---|
| Early positive | "I wanted to say how [X] has started the year" | One specific thing, named. Generic praise reads as a form letter |
| Missing work | "Four of the last six pieces have not been submitted" | Dates, the effect on the grade, the catch-up route, the deadline, next contact |
| Behaviour | "In today's lesson [X] did [observable]. I have [action taken]." | The behaviour, the consequence, the clean slate, what you are asking at home |
| Attainment concern | "The last two assessments were [figures], against [expectation]" | Numbers, the specific gap, what changes at school, the review date |
| Attendance | "[X] has missed 12 of 60 sessions this term" | The count, the content missed, the catch-up plan, an offer to help remove the barrier |
| Good news, unprompted | "I wanted you to know…" | Send more of these than you think. They cost a minute and change every later conversation |
| Following an incident | Facts only, what happened, what was done | Cleared by the line manager first if it is serious; never a first draft sent in anger |

## The Conference

Five to ten minutes, and the structure decides whether it works:

1. **Something specific and genuine that is going well.** Specific, or it is heard as the opening move of bad news.
2. **The concern, with evidence in front of you.** The book, the marks, the attendance figure. Evidence turns a disagreement about opinions into a shared look at a page.
3. **Listen.** Ask what they see at home. The most useful information in the meeting usually arrives here, and it is often the reason for everything else.
4. **One agreed action each** — you, them, the student. More than one each is a plan nobody executes.
5. **The date of the next contact**, said aloud.

Practicalities: know the child before they sit down; have their work with you; if the student is present, speak *to* them, not about them; never overrun so far that the next family waits twenty minutes, because that family arrives already annoyed.

## The Angry Conversation

- **Let the first ninety seconds run.** Interrupting an angry parent resets the clock. Take a note while they speak; being visibly recorded as heard de-escalates faster than any sentence.
- **Reflect the content, not the emotion**: "so the two issues are the grade and how the incident was handled". Naming the emotion is often heard as a technique, and it is.
- **Concede what is true immediately.** If something was missed, say so plainly. Defending an indefensible detail costs the whole conversation.
- **Do not defend a colleague you have not spoken to**, and do not throw them under the bus. "I do not have the details of that lesson; I will find out and come back to you by Thursday."
- **Never negotiate a mark under pressure** (`grading.md`). Offer the process: re-mark against the rubric, moderation, the appeals route.
- **Move to writing** once positions harden: it slows the exchange and creates a record.
- **End it if it becomes personal or intimidating**: "I am going to stop here and we will continue with my head of department present." Then inform the line manager the same day and write the account — facts, dates, what was said — into that student's rows in the class file while it is fresh; that is the Red Flags row for this domain.

## Grade Disputes

1. Re-mark against the rubric before replying. Sometimes the student is right, and knowing that first changes the conversation entirely.
2. Reply with the artifact, not the position: the rubric row, the descriptor, the evidence in the work, the anchor that defines the boundary.
3. Explain the process available — moderation, second marking, the formal appeal — and follow it exactly.
4. Never adjust one student's mark privately. If a mark changes, it changes for everyone affected by the same error, and you say so.
5. Record the dispute, the date, the evidence shown, and the outcome in that student's rows in the class file `~/Clawic/data/teacher/classes/<class-id>.md`. A second dispute is a different conversation and the record is what makes it a calm one (`grading.md`).

## Separated Families and Who Gets Told

- Follow the school's record of parental responsibility, not the person who emails most. Getting this wrong has legal consequences in many systems.
- Where both hold responsibility, both receive the same information at the same time. Sending to one and not the other is how a school ends up in a dispute that has nothing to do with the child's work.
- Never take sides, never comment on the family arrangement, never pass a message between adults.
- Where there is a court order or a restriction on contact, that is a school-office matter — check before you send anything.

## Language Barriers

- Use the school's interpreting service for anything consequential. A bilingual child interpreting news about themselves is not an interpreter, and it puts the child in an impossible position.
- Write in plain sentences; avoid idiom, phrasal verbs and educational jargon, which survive machine translation worst.
- Send written notes ahead of a meeting so they can be read with time and help.
- Check comprehension by asking what they will do next, not by asking whether they understood.

## Boundaries and Channels

- **One channel, published, with a response window.** "Email, replies within two working days, term time" prevents the drift into 11pm messaging that ends in burnout (`workload.md`).
- No personal phone number, no personal social media, no messaging app that is not the school's system. This protects the teacher and creates the record that protects the student.
- Never reply to a hostile message the same evening. Draft, sleep, send in the morning; and have anything serious read by a colleague first.
- Emotional labour is real work and it is invisible on a timetable. Batch guardian contact into a fixed slot rather than reacting to arrivals, and put the slot in `## Due`.

## What Never Goes in Writing

- A diagnosis, a suspicion of one, or anything about a sibling or another student.
- A comparison to another child, ever, including "several other students manage this".
- Speculation about home circumstances; record what was observed, not what it might mean.
- A safeguarding disclosure — that goes through the school's channel and system the same day (`SKILL.md` Red Flags), never in an email to a guardian and never in a personal file.
- A promise you have not confirmed you can keep: a grade, a set, a placement, an exam arrangement.
- Anything you would not want read aloud in a meeting with the head teacher and the family present, because that is where difficult messages end up.

**After any guardian, colleague or mentor conversation**, put the person in the shared address book `~/Clawic/data/contacts/contacts.md` — identity is `Key`, the email in lower case; read before adding and update the row in place, never a second row for the same person; match the columns already there if the file exists, and never rewrite a header another skill wrote. Their `Context` names the student in the `student_naming` form and their preferred channel; retire the row when the relationship ends and note the date. **What was agreed, and the next contact date**, goes in that student's rows in the class file plus a `## Due` row. Never store a minor's phone number or address in any of these files (`memory-template.md`).
