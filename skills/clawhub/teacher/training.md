# Training — Corporate Workshops, Bootcamps and Adult Learners

Adults arrive with a job to do, prior experience that may be right or wrong, and a manager who will decide whether any of this survives contact with Monday. Training that ignores those three is a well-reviewed event with no effect.

## Needs Analysis Before Content

The requested training is often not the intervention that fixes the problem. Four questions, asked of the sponsor, before any design:

1. **What is happening now that should not be, or not happening that should?** In observable terms, with a number if one exists.
2. **Is it a skill problem?** If people can do it when their life depends on it, it is not training — it is process, incentives, tooling, or management. Training aimed at a non-training cause fails predictably and is blamed on the trainer.
3. **What will be different on Monday?** Name the behaviour, the person doing it, and how anyone would know.
4. **Who owns the follow-up?** If the answer is nobody, design for a smaller behaviour change, because nothing else will hold.

Write the answers into the brief and get them agreed. A one-page brief agreed with the sponsor is the difference between "the workshop was good" and "the workshop worked".

## What Changes With Adults

Andragogy (Knowles) in its operational form:

| Principle | What you do differently |
|---|---|
| They need to know why | Lead with the problem it solves in their work, not the syllabus |
| They bring experience | Elicit it and use it as material; contradicting it directly makes an enemy of the room |
| They are problem-centred, not subject-centred | Organise by the situation they face, not by the structure of the discipline |
| They want immediate application | Every block ends with the participant applying it to their own real case |
| Self-direction matters | Give real choices about depth, order and case selection |
| Their time is expensive and visible | Never waste it; an unnecessary icebreaker costs 20 people 15 minutes |

Two extras that matter more than the theory: adults are afraid of looking incompetent in front of colleagues, and the seniority mix in the room decides what anyone will admit not knowing. Design the practice so that being a beginner is safe, and separate cohorts when the gap is severe.

## Designing a Session

Backward design still applies (`planning.md`) with the terminal evidence being a workplace behaviour:

1. Behaviour on the job → 2. What must be practised here to make it likely → 3. The practice, with feedback → 4. The minimum input needed to make the practice possible.

- **Input is the smallest part.** A common ratio for skills training is roughly 30% input, 50% practice with feedback, 20% planning the application. Sessions that invert this — 80% slides — are the default and the reason most training does not transfer.
- **Practise the real thing, on their material.** Generic case studies produce generic learning. Ask participants to bring a live example; if they cannot, use one from their organisation with permission.
- **Cap the content.** Three things people can do beats twelve things they have heard. Ruthlessly park the rest into a reference they can read later — and actually give them the reference.

## The Half-Day and Full-Day Arc

Change the mode at least every 20 minutes — input, activity, discussion, application. Shown for a half day; a full day is two of these with the second built around the lowest-energy slot.

| Block | Minutes | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Frame | 10 | The problem, what will be different by the end, the shape of the session |
| Diagnostic | 10-15 | A short real task that surfaces the actual gap — also tells you who is in the room |
| Input 1 | 15-20 | The smallest model that makes the first practice possible |
| Practice 1 | 25-30 | On their own case, with feedback |
| Break | 15 | Non-negotiable; the alternative is a room reading email |
| Input 2 | 15-20 | The second element, only if practice 1 landed |
| Practice 2 | 30-40 | Harder, closer to the real conditions |
| Application planning | 20 | Each person writes what they will do, when, with whom, and the first obstacle |
| Close | 10 | Commitments said aloud; what follow-up exists and when |

- **The post-lunch slot** takes the most active work in the day, never a lecture.
- **Build slack**: one activity you are willing to cut. Every workshop overruns somewhere, and cutting the application planning — the only part that drives transfer — is the wrong recovery.

## Facilitation

- **Set the contract in the first five minutes**: devices, confidentiality, how questions work, when breaks are. Retrofitting a norm after it has been broken costs far more.
- **Name what you will not cover.** It prevents the drift where one participant's edge case consumes an hour.
- **Park and return, visibly.** A parking board with items dated and answered is the cheapest credibility device in facilitation.
- Silence after a question is longer with adults than with children and means thinking more often. Count to eight.
- With senior participants, hand them a role — reviewing, challenging, contributing a case — rather than teaching at them.
- Read the room's energy and change mode rather than pushing through. A room that has stopped is not persuaded by more input.

## Transfer to the Job

Most of the effect is decided outside the room, and mostly by the manager conversation before and after (Brinkerhoff). Design for it:

- **Pre-work that costs under 15 minutes** and requires them to bring a real problem. Long pre-work is not done, and the room then splits into two groups.
- **A manager brief** — three lines: what their person is learning, the one behaviour to look for, and the question to ask in the next one-to-one.
- **An implementation intention, written**: "when [specific trigger] happens, I will [specific action]". If-then plans transfer far better than intentions to "apply the learning".
- **A follow-up touch point at 3-4 weeks**: 30 minutes, what worked, what blocked, one adjustment. This single meeting predicts sustained change better than anything that happens on the day.
- **Job aids beat memory.** A one-page checklist at the point of work outperforms recall of a full-day workshop, and is cheaper to produce than a slide deck.

## Measuring Past the Happy Sheet

Kirkpatrick's four levels, with the honest cost and value of each:

| Level | Measures | When | Worth |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Reaction | Whether they liked it | End of session | Cheap; correlates weakly with learning. Useful for logistics, not for effect |
| 2 Learning | Whether they can now do it | End of session, and again at 4 weeks | The delayed one is the real number; the immediate one is inflated by the session itself |
| 3 Behaviour | Whether they do it at work | 4-8 weeks after | The level that matters, and the one usually skipped |
| 4 Results | Whether the business number moved | Quarter+ | Rarely attributable to training alone; claim it only with a control or a clean before/after |

- Ask reaction questions that predict something: "what will you do differently on Monday?" and "what would have made this more useful?" beat a satisfaction score.
- Level 2 at four weeks is the cheapest honest evidence available: five retrieval questions by email. Almost nobody does it, which is why almost no training programme knows whether it works.
- Record the level measured, the date and the number in `assessments/<year>.md`; a training function with no measurement history renegotiates its value from zero every year.

## Bootcamps and Intensive Cohorts

- **Intensity destroys spacing.** Eight hours a day of new content violates every retention principle at once; build daily retrieval of previous days and weekly cumulative review, or week one is gone by week four.
- **Projects are the assessment, and provenance is the risk.** Checkpoints, code review, and an oral walkthrough of their own work (`integrity.md`).
- **Cohort dynamics are the retention mechanism.** Pairs, standups, and a visible progress signal keep people in; isolation is the main predictor of dropout.
- Expect a bimodal cohort by week two. Plan the split explicitly — extension for the fast, targeted catch-up sessions for the rest — rather than teaching to the middle and losing both ends (`differentiation.md`).
- Sleep and fatigue are the constraint nobody designs for. A schedule with no consolidation time produces exhausted graduates who cannot demonstrate what they built.

## Onboarding Curricula

- Sequence by what a new joiner must do in week one, then month one, then quarter one — never by the org chart or the history of the company.
- Just-in-time beats front-loading: the compliance module in week one is forgotten by the time it applies. Deliver each piece as close to first use as possible.
- Pair every module with a real task and a named person to ask.
- The best onboarding artifact is a checklist of first tasks with links, maintained by whoever last joined — put it in `artifacts/` and update it after each cohort.

## Difficult Rooms

| Situation | Move |
|---|---|
| Mandated attendance, visible resentment | Name it in the first two minutes, honestly, then make the first activity useful to them personally. Do not pretend it is voluntary |
| The dominant expert | Give them a formal role — reviewer, case contributor — and use structures where everyone writes before anyone speaks |
| Wide seniority mix | Structures with individual writing first; separate practice groups by level; never make juniors perform in front of their manager |
| Wildly mixed prior knowledge | The diagnostic in the arc exists for this; run parallel practice tracks after it |
| Sponsor wants content you consider useless | Deliver it briefly, in its own block, labelled — and put the effort where the behaviour change is. Note the disagreement in the brief |
| Room is exhausted, or a crisis is happening at the client | Shorten, cut to the single most useful thing, and offer to reschedule the rest. Pushing through burns the relationship |

## Working With a Client

- Agree the brief in writing: audience, numbers, prior knowledge, the behaviour target, logistics, what you need in the room, and what happens after.
- Ask what training they have already run on this, and why it did not stick. The answer usually names the real constraint.
- Confirm the room, the kit and the timings the week before. Live experience of a broken projector or a room with fixed rows is a design problem, not an inconvenience.
- The client contact is a person you will work with again: they belong in the shared address book, not in your inbox history.
