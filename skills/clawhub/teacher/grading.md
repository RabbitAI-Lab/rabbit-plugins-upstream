# Grading — Marking and Feedback That Fit in a Week

Marking is the largest recurring cost in teaching and the one with the widest gap between effort and effect. The goal is not less feedback; it is feedback that gets acted on, produced inside a budget the teacher can survive.

**Before marking a batch**, read the rubric artifact and its anchor scripts in `~/Clawic/data/teacher/artifacts/`, and the `## Needs` table in the class file (an access arrangement changes what is being marked). **Before starting the clock**, record the collection date: `grading_turnaround_days` sets the return date and it belongs in `## Due` the day the work comes in.

## The Budget Comes First

`weekly hours = minutes_per_piece × pieces_per_week × class_size × classes ÷ 60` (SKILL.md Rule 8).

| Design | Arithmetic | Weekly hours |
|---|---|---|
| Full essay, 12 min, 1 piece, 28 students, 4 classes | 12 × 1 × 28 × 4 ÷ 60 | 22.4 |
| Structured question, 5 min, 1 piece, 28, 4 | 5 × 1 × 28 × 4 ÷ 60 | 9.3 |
| Sample-marked (8 scripts) + whole-class feedback, 4 classes | (5 × 8 × 4 + 4 × 20) ÷ 60 | 4.0 |
| Self-marked quiz + teacher reads the misses, 4 classes | 2 × 28 × 4 ÷ 60 | 3.7 |

- Run the arithmetic **when the assessment calendar is written**, not the weekend it arrives. The number is a design constraint, exactly like a room booking.
- When the number exceeds the hours available, change the assessment. The alternatives, in order of what preserves the most value: sample-mark plus whole-class feedback; fewer and larger pieces; self- and peer-marking against a key for anything with right answers; comparative judgement for extended writing; oral feedback in class during independent practice.
- **Never buy the gap with turnaround.** Feedback beyond about two weeks arrives after the student has forgotten their reasoning and is a grade with words attached.

## Feedback That Changes Work

Roughly a third of feedback interventions in the research literature *reduced* performance, and the worst offenders were directed at the person rather than the task (Kluger and DeNisi 1996). The design rules that follow from that:

- **Task, not self.** "The second paragraph asserts the cause without evidence" — never "you're careless" or "you're a natural at this".
- **One required action per piece.** Three comments produce zero actions; one produces one.
- **The action must be doable today**, with what they have. "Add a topic sentence to paragraph 2" is an action; "develop your argument" is a wish.
- **Protect the time.** Book 5-10 minutes of the next lesson for acting on feedback and check the redraft. Feedback with no slot to respond is feedback the student reads and files.
- **Feedback should cause more thinking for the student than it cost the teacher to write** (Wiliam). A comment you spent four minutes on that produces a tick is a bad trade.
- Questions often beat statements: "what happens to this argument if the sample was volunteers?" makes them do the work.
- For anything procedural, mark the *first* error and stop. Everything after it is a consequence, and marking consequences buries the cause.

## Comment-Only vs Grades

A grade next to a comment removes attention from the comment; students given comments alone improved where students given grades, or grades plus comments, did not (Butler 1988). Operationally:

| Work type | What appears | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Formative — practice, drafts, class work | Comment only, one action | The point is the next attempt |
| Summative — end of unit, mock, terminal | Grade, plus criteria-referenced feedback | The point is the claim about attainment |
| Draft of a graded piece | Comment only, with the rubric row named | Preserves the grade's meaning and the comment's audience |

- Where the LMS shows a grade automatically, either withhold release until the comment has been acted on, or accept that the comment is decorative. Setting `lms` matters here: several platforms surface a mark the moment it is entered.
- Students conditioned to expect a grade on everything will ask. Say the rule once, in the syllabus, and hold it (`curriculum.md`).

## Whole-Class Feedback

The highest-value-per-minute technique available, and the one that makes sample marking safe.

1. Read the whole set without writing anything, sorting into three piles and noting patterns on one sheet.
2. Build one page: the three most common errors with examples; two anonymised extracts, one strong and one weak, for joint improvement; the model answer or exemplar; the required action for everyone; the named short list who need something individual.
3. Spend the first 15 minutes of the next lesson on it: read the sheet, improve the weak extract together, then everyone acts on their own piece.
4. Individual written comments only for the students the sheet does not serve.

Reading a set through without writing anything is far cheaper than marking it — time both once on your own set rather than trusting a figure from anyone else — and the students act on more of what comes back.

## Marking Faster Without Marking Worse

| Technique | Saving | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Mark question-by-question across all scripts | Moderate, and a steadier standard | Requires all scripts at once |
| Codes with a legend (`E` evidence, `T` topic sentence, `//` new paragraph) | Large on written work | Students must be taught the legend, and the legend must be visible |
| Mark against the rubric row only, not line-by-line | The largest single cut on extended writing | Needs a good rubric (`assessment.md`) |
| Live marking during independent practice | Removes the pile entirely for that piece | Reaches only a handful of students per lesson — count how many on your first attempt |
| Verbal feedback with a written action recorded by the student | High | Needs a routine, or nothing is recorded |
| Self-marking with an answer key, teacher reads only the misses | Largest of all on closed-answer work | Requires a norm that self-marking is honest, taught not assumed |
| Stop marking accuracy of copying, presentation, and titles | Reclaims a surprising share | None; it was never feedback |

The Saving column is an ordering, not a measurement: how much each one gives back depends on the subject, the length of the piece, and how you mark now. Time one batch the old way and one the new way before rebuilding a week around any of them, and write the figures you got into the decision artifact.

Two rules underneath all of them: **do not mark what you will not act on**, and **do not mark what the student can mark themselves**.

## Comparative Judgement

For extended writing, judging *which of two scripts is better* is faster and more reliable than scoring each against a rubric, because relative judgement is a much easier cognitive task than absolute scoring.

- Useful when: essays, extended writing, portfolios, open-ended design work, and when several markers must agree.
- Not useful when: the feedback must be criterion-specific, or the cohort is small.
- Practical shape: repeated pairwise decisions produce a rank order; the rank is converted to grades against anchors. It gives a defensible order and a fast one — but it produces no comment, so pair it with whole-class feedback.

## Comment Banks

- Build the bank from your own marking: after any batch, the five comments written most often become numbered entries with their required action.
- Each entry is a *comment plus an action plus, where useful, a practice item*. A bank of adjectives is not a bank.
- Personalise the first clause, reuse the second. Students detect wholesale copy-paste and stop reading.
- The bank is an artifact: it goes to `~/Clawic/data/teacher/artifacts/<kebab-name>.md`, is reused every year, and is the single highest-return thing to write down after a heavy marking week.

## Holding the Standard

- Norm on 3-5 anchor scripts before the first mark (`assessment.md`), and re-check against them every 20 scripts. Standards drift downward with fatigue and upward after a strong script.
- Mark the same question across scripts in one sitting; changing question mid-batch resets the standard.
- Shuffle the order between batches so the same students are not always marked last.
- Blind-mark boundary work where the stakes are real. Knowing whose script it is measurably moves marks in both directions.
- Second-mark a sample with a colleague at least once a cycle. Disagreement on a script here and there is ordinary and mostly lands on boundary work; disagreement on a large share of the sample means the rubric is not shared and re-norming on anchors comes before any further marking. Fix your own two thresholds the first time you do it and reuse them, rather than judging each cycle afresh.

## Grade Calculation

- **State the combination rule in the syllabus and follow it exactly**: weights, whether the lowest is dropped, how a missing piece is treated, when rounding happens (once, at the end — rounding at each step compounds).
- Mean is distorted by zeros: a single missing piece scored zero can outweigh a term of good work. Decide deliberately whether a missing piece is a zero or an incomplete, and say which in the syllabus (`curriculum.md`).
- Do not average across scales. Percentages, letters and level scores are different measurements; convert once, at a published boundary, or keep them separate.
- Effort, behaviour and attendance are reported separately from attainment or they corrupt both.
- The school's gradebook or SIS is the system of record for individual marks. `assessments/<year>.md` holds aggregates and item analysis only.

## Late, Missing, and Retaken Work

- Write the rule before the first request, publish it, and apply it identically. Case-by-case leniency is experienced as favouritism whatever the intent.
- Distinguish a **penalty** (a grade deduction, which corrupts the attainment claim) from a **consequence** (completion required at a set time, which does not). Prefer the second where policy allows.
- Retakes: publish whether they exist, their ceiling, and the precondition — a retake with no intervening teaching just measures the same thing twice.
- Extensions granted for an access arrangement or a documented circumstance are not late work, and never carry a penalty.

## Grade Disputes

1. Re-mark against the rubric before responding to anything. Occasionally the student is right, and finding that out first changes the entire conversation.
2. Respond with the artifact: the rubric row, the descriptor, the evidence in the work, the anchor script that defines the boundary.
3. Never negotiate a mark on the basis of effort, need, or persistence. Offer what is actually available: the improvement route, the retake if the policy allows one, the next assessment.
4. If a mark changes, change it for everyone the same error affected, and say so.
5. Record the dispute, the date, and the outcome in the class file; a second dispute with the same guardian is a different conversation, and the record is what makes it a calm one (`parents.md`).

**When a comment bank, rubric, mark scheme or exemplar set is produced**, it goes to `~/Clawic/data/teacher/artifacts/<kebab-name>.md` with its `## Boxes` line in `memory.md`, in the same turn. **When a batch is marked**, write the distribution in `~/Clawic/data/teacher/assessments/<year>.md` and close the `## Due` turnaround row. **When a marking-load decision is made** — sample marking, comparative judgement, dropping a piece — record it as a decision in `artifacts/` with the arithmetic that justified it, so it survives the next timetable change (`memory-template.md`).
