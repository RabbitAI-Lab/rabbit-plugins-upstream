# Observation — Being Watched, and Getting Better on Purpose

An observation is one lesson sampled from about 180. It is a weak measurement and a strong opportunity: the measurement is unreliable, but the conversation afterwards is the only structured feedback most teachers get all year.

**Before an observation cycle**, read `## Practice Goals` in `~/Clawic/data/teacher/memory.md`: the target from the last cycle and whether it was met is the first thing worth saying in the meeting, and the first thing forgotten without a record.

## What an Observation Can and Cannot Measure

| Visible in one lesson | Not visible in one lesson |
|---|---|
| Whether routines exist and are followed | Whether learning is durable — that needs a delayed check |
| Response rate and how checks are used | Whether the sequence over six weeks was well designed |
| Clarity of explanation and the worked example | Whether feedback gets acted on |
| How the room is managed, and by what means | Relationships built over a year |
| Whether tasks match the stated objective | Whether the assessment was valid |

- Inter-observer agreement on lesson quality is generally poor: two trained observers watching the same lesson often disagree, and the same teacher scores differently across lessons and across classes. Treat any single judgement as one data point about one hour.
- The reliable use of observation is **formative**: one specific, observable thing to change. Graded observation adds anxiety and no information.
- The most common invalid inference is judging learning from engagement. Busy, cheerful and compliant is not evidence of anything; the check data is.

## Preparing Without Performing

- **Teach the lesson that comes next in the sequence.** A showcase lesson misrepresents your teaching, teaches the students less, and cannot be repeated — so the feedback applies to a lesson that does not exist.
- **Give the observer the context in five lines**: where this sits in the unit, what the group's prior attainment is, what you have been working on, and what you would like them to watch for. Directing the observation is the single best move available to you and almost nobody does it.
- Have the class file's accommodations visibly running; an observer who sees an adjustment delivered by default is seeing the strongest thing in the room.
- Do not introduce a new technique for the observation. Under pressure, unrehearsed techniques fail, and the feedback then addresses the failure of something you do not normally do.
- Prepare the students honestly: "someone is watching me teach, not you". Elaborate briefings produce a strange, silent class.

## During the Lesson

- Run your normal routines and hold your normal standards. An observer who sees a correction handled calmly learns more than one who sees an artificially perfect room.
- **Do the checks and act on the results visibly.** If the hinge question comes back at 60% and you split the class to reteach, that is the strongest ten seconds of the observation — it shows the whole cycle working (`checking.md`).
- Do not fill silence because someone is watching. Wait time looks longer from the front than from the back.
- If something goes wrong, handle it and continue. The recovery is more informative than a lesson where nothing happened.
- Ignore the observer's face. Note-taking is not disapproval, and reading it costs attention you need.

## The Feedback Conversation

- **Ask for one thing.** A list of eight observations produces zero changes; a single specific action produces one.
- Ask for the evidence behind each judgement: "what did you see that made that look unclear?" This converts an opinion into something actionable, politely.
- Ask what they saw that you could not — where students were lost, who did nothing for ten minutes, which explanation lost the back row. That is the whole value of a second pair of eyes and it is usually left unsaid.
- Where you disagree, say so with the reason, and offer the data: the check results, the delayed retrieval next week, the marks.
- Close by agreeing the target, how it will be measured, and the review date — then write it to `## Practice Goals` in `memory.md` with the review in `## Due`. An improvement target with no review date is a comment.

## Choosing One Improvement Target

Good targets are observable, frequent and small. Bad targets are dispositions.

| Weak target | Strong target |
|---|---|
| Improve questioning | Three seconds of wait time after every question, checked by recording one lesson |
| Better differentiation | Every task has a stated entry support and a permanent extension task |
| Higher expectations | Every student responds to the hinge question, on whiteboards, twice a lesson |
| More engaging lessons | Independent practice is at least a third of every lesson, timed |
| Improve behaviour | Entry routine taught and enforced identically for four weeks |

- Pick something that occurs several times per lesson: frequency is what makes practice possible.
- One target at a time, for four to six weeks. Two targets means neither is practised.

## Deliberate Practice for Teachers

Teaching improves fastest with the same conditions as any other skill: a specific component, immediate feedback, and repetition.

- **Rehearse the ten-second move**, not the lesson: the exact words of the correction, the exact phrasing of the explanation, the countdown. Say them aloud before the lesson.
- **Record yourself once a term** — audio alone is enough and is much less intrusive. Count your wait time, your positive-to-corrective ratio, and how many different students spoke. All three are usually a surprise, and none is visible from the front.
- **Script the hard thirty seconds** in advance: the redirect, the refusal, the "I don't know" from the student, the wrong answer you did not expect.
- Practise with a colleague where the culture allows it. Rehearsing a correction out loud feels absurd for ninety seconds and then works.
- Change one thing at a time and hold it for four weeks. Teaching has too many variables to attribute anything otherwise.

## Observing a Colleague

- Agree the focus beforehand, and watch that. An unfocused observation produces generic feedback.
- **Watch the students, not the teacher.** Where the learning is visible is in what students do: how many respond, what they write, when they stop.
- Collect data rather than impressions: tally who answers, time the transitions, note the wait time, count how many students started within a minute. Data is discussable; "the pace felt slow" is not.
- Give one strength with the evidence, and one question rather than one instruction. A question ("what were you looking for when you called on the back row?") leaves the expertise where it belongs.
- Never observe and grade. It converts a colleague into a subordinate and ends honest conversation about teaching.

## Learning Walks and Drop-Ins

- Short, frequent, unannounced visits sample more honestly than a scheduled hour and are less stressful once they are genuinely routine.
- They are only tolerable when nothing is graded and something is given back — a line of feedback within a day, or nothing at all.
- A drop-in during independent practice sees more than one during teacher exposition, because you can read the work.

## Appraisal and Evidence

- Keep evidence continuously, not in the week before the meeting. What is worth keeping: the delayed check data, the score distributions and item analysis in `assessments/<year>.md`, the intervention records and their reviews, the artifacts produced and reused, the coverage audit, guardian contact records.
- Where targets are attached to student outcomes, insist on the comparison being fair: same group, same measure, same time of year. Value-added against a mismatched cohort is not a measurement of you.
- Bring the improvement target from the last cycle and what happened to it. Being the person who tracked their own target changes the tone of every appraisal.

## A Bad Observation

- Separate the judgement from the information. Even an unfair judgement usually contains one true observation; take it and leave the rest.
- Ask for a second observation of a different class. Any single lesson is a sample, and this is a reasonable request.
- Where the process is unfair, follow the formal route and keep the record — dates, what was said, what evidence was given. **This one lives outside `~/Clawic/data/`**, in your own personal files: it is an employment matter that a union representative or a tribunal may read, not teaching data, and it must survive leaving the job. Emotional recovery and procedural response are separate tasks — do the second one in writing, later, and calmly (`workload.md`).
- Do not change five things in response. That is how a competent teacher becomes an incoherent one; take the single most credible point and work on it.

## Improving Without an Observer

- The check data is the honest observer: response rates, hinge results, delayed retrieval, item analysis. It sees every lesson, not one.
- Ask the students, structured: three questions, anonymous, mid-term, and report back what you will change (`higher-ed.md`). Do this and the answers become useful; do it once and ignore them and they never will be.
- Read a colleague's marking of the same task; standards drift alone and nowhere faster.
- Keep one thing per unit that you would show someone — the plan whose timings held, the rubric, the marked exemplar set — in `~/Clawic/data/teacher/artifacts/<kebab-name>.md` with its `## Boxes` line. The habit of producing something reviewable is most of the improvement, and by the appraisal the evidence is already written.

**After any observation, appraisal or peer feedback**, write the target to `## Practice Goals` in `~/Clawic/data/teacher/memory.md` — what was seen, the one change, how it will be measured — and put the review date in `## Due`. **At the review**, update the same entry with what actually happened; a target with no outcome recorded is why the same feedback arrives every year. **When you observe a colleague**, they belong in the shared address book `~/Clawic/data/contacts/contacts.md`, and the focus and data you collected stay with you, never in a shared file (`memory-template.md`).
