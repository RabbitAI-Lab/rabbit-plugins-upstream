# Higher Education — Lectures, Seminars, TAs and Scale

Scale changes the mechanics, not the principles. A 300-seat lecture obeys the same rules about response rate and retrieval as a class of 28; what changes is which techniques are physically possible and how much of the teaching is done by people who are not you.

## The Lecture Problem

A lecture transmits information at roughly reading speed, to people who could have read it, with no check that anything landed. It survives because it is cheap per student, and it is defensible only when it does something reading cannot: model expert reasoning, resolve confusion in real time, or synthesise sources that do not exist in one place.

- **Decide what this lecture does that the reading cannot.** If there is no answer, the lecture should be a reading plus a problem class.
- Recorded lectures make live attendance a choice students make on value. That is uncomfortable and is also useful information.
- The strongest available structure is flipped: information acquisition before the session, expert reasoning and problem-solving in it. It only works when the pre-session task is short, checked and consequential — an unchecked reading is not done by most of a large cohort.

## Interactive Lectures at Scale

Everything from `checking.md` applies; these are the versions that survive 300 people.

| Technique | Mechanics at scale |
|---|---|
| Clicker / polling question | The core tool: a hinge MCQ every 10-15 minutes, with distractors from real misconceptions |
| Peer instruction | Poll → if 30-70% correct, discuss in pairs → re-poll. The re-poll almost always improves, and the discussion is where the learning happens (Mazur) |
| One-minute paper | "What was the main point? What is still unclear?" Read a sample of 30, address in the next session |
| Think-pair-share | Works at any size; only the reporting is sampled |
| Worked example on the visualiser | Modelling reasoning is what the lecture is for; slides of finished results are not |
| Deliberate error | Announce that one thing said today is wrong; the room stays awake and reads critically |

- Poll results in the 30-70% band are the productive ones: that is where peer discussion moves people. Above 80%, move on; below 30%, reteach rather than discuss, because pairs converge on the wrong answer when almost nobody has the right one.
- Do not lecture for 50 minutes and take questions at the end. The questions arrive from the four people who already understood.

## Seminars and Tutorials

- **Preparation must be consequential.** A seminar where the reading is optional becomes a lecture with fewer people. Use a short pre-submitted response, a rota of discussion leads, or an entry question.
- **Structure the first ten minutes hard**: everyone writes a position, then pairs, then the open discussion. Cold openness selects for confidence and for the students who were already comfortable.
- Silence is a tool. Ask, wait, and do not rescue; the tutor who fills silences trains the group to wait them out.
- Assign roles across the term — discussion lead, devil's advocate, synthesiser, note-taker — so participation is structural rather than personality-driven.
- Small groups fail the same way large ones do when interdependence is missing (`engagement.md`).

## Reading That Actually Gets Read

- **Set less and require use.** Dense academic prose runs at roughly 20-30 pages an hour at undergraduate level; a 200-page week is a week where nothing is read.
- Say what each reading is *for*: the argument, the method, the counter-case, the data. Students read differently when they know the purpose.
- Give a reading question that cannot be answered from the abstract.
- Teach the skim: title, abstract, headings, figures, conclusion, then decide. Nobody is taught this and everyone is expected to have it.
- Annotated or social reading tools raise completion because the reading becomes visible; the same effect can be had with a required two-sentence post before the seminar.

## Problem Classes and Labs

- Problem classes are where the learning happens in quantitative subjects; staff them properly and structure them as guided practice, not as a room where students sit stuck.
- Publish worked solutions after, never instead of, attempts — and make the attempt the entry ticket.
- Labs default to following a recipe, which teaches procedure and not method. Add a prediction before, a decision during, and an interpretation after; a lab with no decision in it is a demonstration (`subjects.md`).
- Lab and problem-class staffing is where TAs make or break a module (below).

## Office Hours

- Attendance is usually low and skewed toward the students who need it least. Three changes move it: rename them (student hours), state explicitly what they are for with examples, and require one short visit early in the term from everyone.
- Group office hours are more efficient and less intimidating: the same question is asked by six people, and hearing another student ask it is what gives permission.
- Booked slots with a stated topic beat a drop-in queue for both parties.
- Keep them where policy places them and hold the boundary: an always-open email policy has no closing time, and the cost lands on the same person every year (`workload.md`).

## Running TAs and Demonstrators

Most contact hours in a large module belong to people you do not observe. Managing that is a teaching job, not an administrative one.

- **Brief before each session**, not once per term: what to cover, the common errors, what to do when a student is stuck, and what not to give away.
- **Give them the mark scheme with anchor scripts** and run a norming meeting before marking opens. Unnormed TA marking is the largest source of unfairness in a large module (`grading.md`).
- **Specify what to escalate**: a student in distress, an integrity concern, a systematic misunderstanding across the group, anything about accommodations.
- **Collect what they see.** TAs know which problem is misunderstood by half the cohort a week before you do; ask for three lines after each session.
- Observe each TA once, formatively, and give one specific action (`observation.md`). Most TAs have had no training and want it.
- They are colleagues you will work with again: they belong in the shared address book with their role.

## Marking at Scale

- The budget arithmetic (SKILL.md Rule 8) is unforgiving with cohorts of hundreds. Decide the assessment form from the marking capacity, before publishing the syllabus.
- Moderation is not optional at scale: a sample second-marked across markers, plus all boundary scripts, plus a check that no marker's distribution is systematically shifted.
- Comparative judgement for extended writing is faster and more reliable across multiple markers than rubric scoring (`grading.md`), at the cost of producing no comment — pair it with cohort-level feedback.
- Publish cohort-level feedback within days even when individual marks take weeks: the common errors, the model answer, the distribution.

## The Syllabus as Policy

At this level the syllabus is quasi-contractual and will be quoted back to you. It must state, unambiguously: assessment weights and dates; late penalties and their ceiling; extension and mitigating-circumstances routes; resit rules and capping; the AI and collaboration policy per assignment type (`integrity.md`); accessibility arrangements and their deadline; attendance requirements where they exist; and the response window for messages.

Anything not written there will be adjudicated in your least favourite meeting. Write it once, keep it in `artifacts/`, and revise it after each run (`curriculum.md`).

## Supervising Projects and Dissertations

- **Milestones with artifacts**, not check-ins: proposal, literature map, method, pilot data, draft chapter. A supervision where nothing was submitted is a conversation.
- Agree the scope early and in writing; the classic failure is a project three times too large, discovered at the point where it cannot be shrunk.
- Feedback on writing is developmental until the final draft: comment on structure and argument long before line-level editing, which is otherwise wasted on text that will be rewritten.
- Say what you will not do: rewrite their text, guarantee a mark, or read unlimited drafts. State the number of drafts you will read.
- Keep a short dated record of each meeting and what was agreed in `## Supervisions` in `~/Clawic/data/teacher/memory.md` — supervisee, date, what was agreed, what they will submit next — and read that supervisee's entries before the next meeting. In a dispute about supervision, that dated sequence is the entire evidence base.

## Course Evaluations

- Student evaluations measure satisfaction and are biased by discipline, class size, grading leniency, and demographic characteristics of the instructor. Read them for actionable specifics — pacing, clarity of assessment, availability of materials — and ignore the aggregate score as a measure of teaching quality.
- Free-text comments repeated by many students are signal; individual comments are usually about one experience.
- Ask mid-term instead, when you can still act: three questions, anonymous, and report back what you will change. The act of reporting back changes the end-of-term evaluation more than any of the changes do.
- Record the changes you made and their effect next run in `artifacts/`, or the same feedback returns every year.
