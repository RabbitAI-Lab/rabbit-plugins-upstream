# Online, Hybrid and Async — Rebuilding What the Room Gave You

Online teaching removes three things the room provided for free: peripheral vision of who is lost, the social cost of not participating, and the default that everyone is present at the same time. Everything below is a replacement for one of the three.

**Before planning an online sequence**, read `## Environment` in `~/Clawic/data/teacher/memory.md`: the platform quirk, the school's device baseline, the bandwidth reality and the tool that is blocked on the student network are all facts that cost effort to discover once and should never be discovered twice.

## What Actually Breaks

| Lost | Consequence | Replacement |
|---|---|---|
| Peripheral vision | You cannot see who stopped | Explicit response events every few minutes, with a count |
| Social cost of silence | Non-participation is invisible and free | Named turns and formats where everyone submits |
| Circulating | Process errors are invisible until the product arrives | Shared documents you can watch; work-in-progress submissions |
| Ambient timing | Nobody knows how long anything should take | Every task carries a stated duration and a visible timer |
| Corridor conversation | The quiet struggling student never surfaces | Scheduled short one-to-ones, and a low-friction private channel |
| The room's rhythm | Sessions drift long and dense | Shorter blocks with hard stops; mode change at least every 20 minutes |

## Live Online Sessions

- **Shorter than in person.** A 50-minute in-person lesson becomes about 30-35 minutes of live input plus independent work off-call. Holding thirty faces for a full period produces attendance, not learning.
- **State the shape at the start**: what will happen, in what order, and when they will be asked to do something. Predictability replaces the room's ambient cues.
- **Every 5-8 minutes, a response event.** In person you can read faces; online, silence is unreadable and always means something different from what you assume.
- Use two channels deliberately: your voice for the explanation, the chat for continuous low-stakes response. A monitored chat is the closest online analogue of scanning the room.
- Record when policy allows, and say so at the start. The recording carries most of the value for absent, ill and rewatching students, and it changes what students are willing to say — plan for that, do not be surprised by it.
- End with the same exit check as in person, submitted, not spoken.

## Response Rate Without a Room

| Format | Response rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Chat waterfall — everyone types, nobody sends until "go" | 100% | Prevents the first answer anchoring everyone else; the closest thing to whiteboards |
| Poll / quiz tool | 100% | Best diagnostic online; use hinge questions with real distractors (`checking.md`) |
| Shared document, one row per student | 100%, and you see the work forming | The online replacement for circulating; watch, do not narrate |
| Reaction icons | 100% | Fast mood or yes/no only; not a measurement |
| Named turn ("Sam, then Priya, then Alex") | Per student | Removes the awkward unmuting scramble; announce two names ahead |
| Private message to a student | 1 | The online private word; the least invasive intervention still applies |

- **Wait longer than feels right**: unmuting, typing and lag add several seconds on top of normal think time. Count to eight before filling the silence.
- Never accept "any questions?" as a check. Online it reliably returns silence from a class that understood nothing.

## Cameras

- A camera mandate is unenforceable, inequitable, and buys nothing measurable. It reveals home environments, penalises bandwidth, and produces performance rather than attention.
- Ask for cameras at specific moments and for a reason — a discussion, a first meeting, a presentation — and let them go off in between.
- Build the participation evidence out of *submissions*, not faces. That is a more accurate signal in person too.

## Breakout Rooms

Fail by default; they need the same conditions as any group work (`engagement.md`) plus three online-specific ones:

- **A written brief in the room**, because the spoken instruction is gone the moment the room opens. Post the task, the time, the roles and the product.
- **A product per room**, on a shared document you can see. Without one, half the rooms are silent.
- **Time boxed and announced**, with a warning before the return.
- Groups of 3-4 as always; two people in a breakout room is a stand-off.
- Visit rooms briefly and announce that you will. Arriving unannounced into a room mid-conversation is disruptive and rarely tells you anything.

## Asynchronous Teaching

The mode with the best access and the worst completion, and the difference between a good and bad async course is structural, not stylistic.

- **A rhythm with fixed points.** Weekly release, a fixed deadline, and one live or synchronous touch point. Fully self-paced with no dates has completion rates measured in single-digit percentages outside of highly motivated cohorts.
- **Every unit closes with something submitted.** Watching and reading generate no evidence, and unassessed content is not learned.
- **Discussion boards need a question with disagreement in it** and a required response to two peers, or they are thirty identical paraphrases of the prompt.
- **Reply visibly and fast at the start.** The first week sets whether students believe anyone is on the other end; after that, presence can be batched.
- Build in retrieval across units — a weekly quiz drawing on earlier material — because spacing has no natural home in async unless it is designed (SKILL.md Rule 5).

## Video That Gets Watched

- **Segment: 6-10 minutes per video, one objective each.** Long recordings of live sessions are archives, not teaching materials.
- Talking head plus worked example beats slides read aloud (redundancy effect, `explaining.md`).
- **Embed a question in or immediately after every video.** A video with no check is a video that was played at double speed in another tab.
- Give a transcript and captions: accessibility requirement, comprehension aid, and searchable revision material.
- Record for reuse — a well-made explanation of a hard topic is an artifact you use for years, and it belongs in `artifacts/` with its script.

## Course Structure in an LMS

Where `lms` is set, the platform's default structure decides how much of the course students can find. The rules that survive every platform:

- **One place for everything.** Two places is zero places; a link in an email that is not also in the LMS does not exist.
- **Consistent unit shape**: objective, materials, activity, submission, check. Same order every week — navigation should cost no working memory.
- **Deadlines in the platform's own calendar**, not only in the text of a page.
- **Name files and assignments by content**, not by week number: "Unit 3 — moles calculation practice", not "Week 7 upload". A student returning in May cannot decode week numbers.
- **Announcements are not teaching.** Anything that must be known goes in the structure; announcements are reminders only.
- Check the student view before releasing. Half of all LMS support requests are items that are invisible or unreleased.

## Hybrid, the Hardest Mode

Simultaneous in-person and remote teaching is genuinely harder than either alone, and the standard failure is teaching the room and broadcasting to the remote students.

- **Design for the remote student, then let the in-person students use the same materials.** The reverse never works.
- Every task lives in the shared document, including for the students in the room. That gives one artifact, one set of instructions and one place to look.
- Alternate deliberately: first response from remote, second from the room, and repeat. Without a rule, remote students are addressed roughly never.
- Audio is the whole game. A room microphone that does not pick up student contributions makes discussion invisible to remote students; repeat every student contribution yourself if the kit cannot.
- Never make the remote students watch a group activity they cannot join. Give them a parallel task with a product.

## Assessment Online

- Unsupervised online exams measure access to help. Either accept that and design open-book, judgement-based tasks, or supervise properly — remote proctoring is invasive, error-prone and inequitable, and it is a policy decision, not a teacher one (`integrity.md`).
- Better route: assess the process (drafts, versions, checkpoints), personalise the task so a generic answer does not fit, and add a short oral check on the student's own submission.
- Randomised item pools and shuffled options slow copying and do not stop it; they also make item statistics harder to interpret (`assessment.md`).
- Timed online tests measure bandwidth and typing speed as well as knowledge. If those are not the construct, widen the window.

## Access and Equity

- Assume a phone, not a laptop, unless you know otherwise: check that materials work on a small screen, and never require software that is not free.
- Assume limited bandwidth: audio-only fallback, downloadable materials, no mandatory video upload without an alternative.
- Assume a shared space and a shared device: no mandatory cameras, no mandatory live attendance without a recording, deadlines that survive a sibling needing the computer.
- Assume no printer. Anything that must be written should be answerable digitally or on paper the student already has.

**When something about the platform, network, device baseline or timetable costs effort to find out**, write it to `## Environment` in `memory.md` — that is the file that stops the same twenty minutes being spent next term. **When a video, async unit structure, discussion prompt or hybrid setup works**, save it to `~/Clawic/data/teacher/artifacts/<kebab-name>.md` with its `## Boxes` line, in the same turn. **When an online checking format works with a group** — the chat waterfall gets 100% where open questions get three responses — that goes in `## What Works With This Group` (`memory-template.md`).
