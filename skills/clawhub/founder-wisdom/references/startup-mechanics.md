# Startup Legal Mechanics

The legal and structural decisions you make in the first months of a company set the floor for everything that comes after. Most of them are cheap to get right and expensive to get wrong. This file draws on Carolynn Levy, Jon Levy, and Jason Kwon's YC Startup School lecture on legal mechanics.

This file is not legal advice. Treat it as a checklist of what experienced founders typically do, with the recognition that your specific situation may differ and a qualified startup attorney should sign off on the details.

> **Law and tax figures carry a vintage.** The thresholds in this file — the QSBS exclusion cap and gross-asset limit, the OBBBA tiered holding periods, the 83(b)/Form 15620 filing mechanics, the trust-fund recovery penalty, the D&O runoff convention — reflect U.S. federal law and market practice as of July 2026. Congress and the IRS change these. The personal-exposure section below adds rules that vary by state rather than by year — veil-piercing tests, officer liability for unpaid wages, director duties near insolvency — plus immigration timing set by federal rulemaking; none of those have a single national answer. The mechanics (incorporate clean, file on time, assign IP, don't co-mingle, cap what you guarantee) are durable; the specific numbers are not. Verify current law with a startup tax specialist, and your personal exposures with startup counsel, before acting on any figure here.

## Core axioms

**Incorporate as a Delaware C-Corp from day one.** Delaware is the default for venture-backed tech startups because the legal precedent is mature, the process is fast, and investors expect it. The C-Corp structure (versus LLC or S-Corp) is required for venture investment in nearly all cases. Founders who incorporate as an LLC "to keep things simple" usually end up paying lawyers to convert to a Delaware C-Corp before their first priced round. Skip the conversion cost. *(Stage: Pre-seed.)*

**Use a startup-focused incorporation service.** Clerky and Stripe Atlas are the standard tools. They don't stop after formation — they handle the post-incorporation documents (board resolutions, stock purchases, IP assignments) that most founders forget about. Forming the entity is 20% of the work; getting the rest of the paperwork right is the 80% that protects you.

Quick comparison: **Stripe Atlas** optimizes for a clean entity formed fast, with integration into Mercury/Brex for pre-EIN banking. **Clerky** optimizes for the legal workflows a venture-track company generates for years after formation: SAFEs, hiring docs, equity grants. Pick on which of those you'll lean on harder, not on the price gap — both reprice, and both are dramatically cheaper than paying a lawyer to draft from scratch.

**Treat the corporation as a separate entity from day one.** Open a corporate bank account. Don't pay company expenses from your personal card and "expense them later." Don't pay personal expenses from the corporate account. The discipline isn't about taxes (though it matters there too); it's about establishing that the corporation is a real, separate legal entity. Co-mingling can pierce the corporate veil and put your personal assets at risk if anything ever goes wrong.

**All founders are subject to vesting — including the founders.** Standard is four years with a one-year cliff. If you walk at month 11, you keep zero shares. If you walk at month 14, you keep 14/48ths — about 29% (the cliff vests the first year's 25%, then vesting continues monthly). This is non-negotiable for any company that will raise outside capital — investors will require it if you don't have it. Founders who skip vesting because "we trust each other" learn the hard way when one co-founder leaves at month 18 with 25% of the company.

**Allocate stock by future execution, not past contribution.** All the hard work and value creation is ahead of you. Founders who allocate based on "I had the idea first" or "I spent six months on the prototype" are pricing the past, which is cheap compared to the future. The negotiation should be: who's committed to working full-time, who's contributing what skills, who's accepting what cash compensation tradeoff, and who's bringing what network. Past contribution is one factor, not the deciding one.

**Founders contribute IP to the company as part of the stock purchase.** This is the step founders forget. When you buy your founder shares, the consideration is usually a small amount of cash plus the assignment of any IP you've created related to the business. If you wrote prototype code at MIT and the company never formally takes ownership of it, the IP belongs to you (or possibly to MIT) — not the company. Investors will catch this in diligence and either kill the round or force you to fix it under time pressure. Get the IP assignment right at incorporation. *(Stage: Pre-seed.)*

**File the 83(b) election within 30 days of purchasing founder stock.** Critical for tax reasons that take 20 pages to explain properly. The short version: filing 83(b) lets you pay tax on the stock at its (very low) value today rather than at its (much higher) value as it vests. If you miss the 30-day window, you can owe ordinary income tax on the vested value of your stock every year for the next four years. This has destroyed founders' personal finances. As of 2025, the IRS supports electronic filing via Form 15620 through ID.me — substantially better than the certified-mail-and-pray method that used to be the only option. File the same day you buy your stock so you don't forget. *(Stage: Pre-seed.)*

**Section 1202 (QSBS) is the most consequential tax provision founders ignore.** Qualified Small Business Stock allows founders and early shareholders to exclude up to $15M in capital gains at exit for shares issued after July 4, 2025 ($10M for earlier shares), or 10x basis if greater — federal tax of $0 on a qualifying exit. This is real money, often the largest single financial decision in a founder's career, and it depends on choices made at incorporation. The eligibility requirements are non-trivial:
- The company must be a domestic C-Corp at the time of issuance. LLCs, S-Corps, and partnerships don't qualify. Converting an LLC or partnership later does produce QSBS, but the clock starts at the conversion date and the excluded gain is measured from fair market value at conversion — so pre-conversion appreciation is taxed in full.
- Aggregate gross assets must stay under $75M (post-July 2025; $50M for shares issued earlier) at all times before the issuance and immediately after it — and the "immediately after" test counts the cash raised in that round, so a financing whose own proceeds cross the threshold taints the shares it issues. The converse is the relief: crossing the line later does not taint shares already issued.
- At least 80% of assets must be used in an active qualified trade or business. SaaS qualifies. Professional services (law, medicine, consulting, finance) don't.
- The stock must be originally issued — bought directly from the company, not on a secondary market.
- Under the 2025 OBBBA tiered structure for shares issued after July 4, 2025: hold 3 years for 50% exclusion, 4 years for 75%, 5 years for 100%. Earlier shares require the full 5-year hold for any exclusion.

The S-Corp trap is the most expensive QSBS mistake. Section 1202 requires a C-Corp at issuance, so stock issued while an S election (Form 2553) is in effect can never be QSBS — revoking the election later does not cure it, and those shares stay disqualified for their whole life. An S election made after issuance is a separate problem with the same ending: the corporation has to be a C-Corp for substantially all of the holding period, so an election taken for a year of tax convenience can taint stock that was clean when it was issued. Don't do it unless you've consulted a startup tax specialist and explicitly decided QSBS doesn't apply to your situation.

**Section 1045 lets you defer QSBS gains by rolling into new QSBS.** If you sell qualifying stock before hitting the 5-year (or new tiered) holding period, you can defer the gain by reinvesting the entire proceeds into newly-issued QSBS within 60 days. Useful for serial founders and early-stage angels. Original stock must have been held for more than 6 months. The replacement stock's basis is reduced by the deferred gain.

**Pre-money vs. post-money option pool: the silent down-round.** When investors require an option pool expansion to cover hires before the next round, they almost always insist it be created **pre-money** — which means the dilution falls entirely on the founders and existing shareholders, not on the new investors. A $10M pre-money round with a "10% pre-money option pool" effectively reduces your true valuation by 10% before the new money comes in. Negotiate for the smallest option pool that genuinely covers planned hires through the next round, and understand that this is one of the most consequential terms in the term sheet — often more impactful than the headline valuation.

**Set up vesting documentation before your first hire.** Every employee, advisor, and contractor who gets equity should sign a stock option agreement with vesting terms, board approval, and a current 409A valuation. Cap-table cleanliness is one of the things investors diligence first. Sloppy early grants compound into months of legal cleanup at exactly the wrong moment.

**Use SAFEs or convertible notes for early money, then equity for priced rounds.** YC's standard SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) is the simplest legal instrument for seed-stage capital. Convertible notes are more complex but sometimes appropriate. Priced equity rounds (Series Seed, Series A) come later, when valuation is more credible. Founders who try to do priced rounds at the very earliest stage often overpay in legal fees and dilute themselves more than necessary. *(Stage: Pre-seed–Seed.)*

**Use Y Combinator's free legal documents where appropriate.** YC publishes free, lawyer-vetted standard forms for SAFE notes, founder agreements, employee option grants, and other common documents at ycombinator.com/documents. Using standard documents reduces legal cost and signals to investors that you're not trying to negotiate non-standard terms. Negotiate only the things worth negotiating.

**Advisory boards rarely earn their equity.** Founders set up advisory boards because it feels like a milestone — "we have advisors!" — but most advisors deliver less than the equity they're granted is worth. Be ruthless: a great advisor who gives you 30 high-impact hours a year is worth 0.25%. An advisor who shows up to one dinner a quarter is worth zero. Use the Founder Institute's FAST agreement (Founder/Advisor Standard Template) with monthly vesting and clear deliverables, not nebulous "open to call when needed" arrangements.

**Don't form a formal board too early.** At the seed stage, your board is usually just the founders. You don't need an "advisory board" or "board of directors" with outside members until investors require it (typically at Series A). Adding board members early creates governance overhead without corresponding value. Keep decision rights with the people doing the work. *(Stage: Pre-seed–Seed.)*

## Founder personal exposure

The corporate form is why a failed company costs a founder a hard year instead of a hard decade. What follows is how that protection actually gets breached — rarely by a court, usually by a signature the founder didn't register as a signature, a lapsed policy, or a tax line item. The vintage note above binds hardest here: have counsel inventory your personal exposures once, early, while it costs an hour, rather than discovering them in the week you have the least money and the least time. That week has a shape of its own — see `crisis-resilience.md` for the wind-down sequence these exposures surface during.

**The office lease is the largest personal guarantee most founders sign without noticing.** A landlord with no operating history to underwrite prices that risk by asking you to guarantee the term personally. The exposure is not the months you occupy — it's the rent remaining on the whole term; it survives the company's dissolution, because a guarantee is a separate contract with you rather than with the entity. Equipment leases, bank lines, and small-business credit cards carry the same clause in smaller sizes; corporate cards underwritten against the company's own cash (Brex, Ramp) are the exception, which is most of why founders use them. It is negotiable far more often than founders attempt — cap it at a stated dollar figure, burn it down on a payment-history schedule, or take the New York market's standard good-guy form, which ends your liability for future rent once you vacate and surrender on the required notice. Keep every guarantee on one page with its cap and release condition, because founders reliably cannot list them from memory. See `bootstrapping.md` for what signing one does to the decision to shut down.

**Veil-piercing is rare, cheap to prevent, and attempted almost only against an already-empty company.** The co-mingling axiom above names one input. What courts weigh is the whole alter-ego picture: personal and company money run together, an entity capitalized far too thinly for what it actually did, formalities skipped (no consents, no minutes, no separate books, stock never actually issued), a founder spending company assets as personal ones. The tell a plaintiff's lawyer looks for first is the signature block: signing "Jane Doe" rather than "Jane Doe, CEO, Acme, Inc." hands over the argument that you are personally a party, for free. The prevention runs about an hour a quarter — separate accounts, a written consent for every material decision, an arm's-length written agreement for anything you lend to or transact with the company, and signature blocks that name the entity and your office. The tests vary by state — some require a further showing of fraud or injustice on top of the alter-ego facts — but the habits are what every version of them looks for first.

**The veil was never between you and your own signature or your own conduct.** Guarantees you signed are your own contracts, and they do not dissolve when the entity does (above). Fraud and torts you personally commit were never behind the veil at all. Several states add unpaid wages to the list, imposing personal liability on officers, directors, or large shareholders — the trigger and scope vary by state.

**The corporate form does not stand between you and unpaid payroll taxes.** The income tax and employee-side FICA you withhold are held in trust for the government from the moment of withholding — the company is a custodian, not a debtor. Under the trust-fund recovery penalty (26 U.S.C. § 6672), a responsible person who willfully fails to remit is assessed personally for the entire trust-fund amount. Responsible means significant control over which bills get paid, and it lands jointly and severally: you, the CFO, the controller, whoever signs the checks. Willful is a low bar — paying any other creditor while knowing the withholding is unpaid satisfies it, no bad motive required. The liability survives the corporation, survives dissolution, and is generally nondischargeable in personal bankruptcy. The limit is scope: § 6672 reaches only the withheld employee-side portion, not the employer's share of FICA or FUTA, which stay debts of the company. So skipping a deposit to make payroll is not the same move as stretching a vendor — the vendor is a creditor of the company, the IRS is a creditor of you. If cash is short, cut the payroll, including your own salary, before you touch the withholding.

**D&O is claims-made: the policy that matters is the one live when the claim lands.** Cancelling it during a wind-down is an obvious cost cut and the specific mistake. A terminated executive, a disgruntled shareholder, or a creditor brings a claim about conduct from two years ago — a period the company was fully insured for — and there is no coverage, because there is no live policy for it to attach to. Tail coverage (an extended reporting period, called runoff in a deal context) is the instrument. The window to elect it is set by the policy and closes at or shortly after expiration, so it is bought on the insurer's schedule rather than yours. In an acquisition a multi-year runoff is standard, with six years the common ask *[bench 2026-07]*. Charter indemnification is not a substitute: an indemnity from an insolvent company is worth exactly what the company has. Which is why Side A coverage — the part that responds when the company cannot indemnify you — is the piece to confirm you actually carry. *(Stage: Series A+.)*

**A founder whose immigration status runs through the company is on a clock the company controls — and the wind-down that costs one founder a hard year costs the other the country.** When status is employer-sponsored, ending the employment relationship opens a grace period set by federal rule, and the clock runs from the end of employment, not from the day you start looking. It does not apply to every classification; where it does, it runs about two months at the outside — shorter if your authorized stay ends sooner, available only once per validity period, and discretionary besides. For some founders it is effectively zero: a green-card process tied to this employer can go with the job, and material changes to the role, the ownership, or the entity can require a new filing, so the restructuring that solves the business problem can create an immigration one. That clock is also what makes the co-founder decisions asymmetric — the one who wants out cannot simply leave, and whoever holds the power to terminate holds an immigration consequence over the other, carried silently through every equity conversation the two of you have. Get immigration counsel in the room while there is still runway to sequence around it, and name the asymmetry out loud while the decisions are still hypothetical.

**Near insolvency, board decisions start being judged later by people who weren't in the room.** The shorthand — duties shift to creditors — is imprecise and, in Delaware, wrong as stated; directors keep owing their duties to the corporation. What insolvency changes is who can enforce them: creditors join stockholders as residual claimants and gain standing to sue derivatively on the corporation's behalf (the *Gheewalla* line, clarified in *Quadrant v. Vertin*). Other jurisdictions frame it instead as an obligation to weigh creditor interests as insolvency nears. Practically, this reprices the exact move a cornered founder wants to make: bet the last of the cash on one more pivot, take prepayments you may not be able to deliver against, repay the founder loan ahead of the trade creditors.

**None of that is automatically unlawful, and the protection you get is procedural.** Directors who decide disinterestedly, in good faith, and on an informed basis are generally protected — but what earns that protection is a real board process with contemporaneous minutes and counsel present, which is precisely what a founder running on adrenaline skips. Insider repayments are the likeliest to be clawed back afterward. The limit is that this argues for bringing the board and counsel in earlier, not a bright line for when to stop. Which instrument you then use to wind down is a separate decision — see `crisis-resilience.md`.

## Common founder mistakes

- Incorporating in California or Nevada because it's where they live. Almost always re-incorporated in Delaware later at significant cost.
- Operating as a partnership or LLC for the first year, then converting. The conversion is doable but expensive and creates tax complications — and that first year of appreciation falls outside the QSBS exclusion, taxable in full at exit.
- Electing S-Corp status (Form 2553) for short-term tax convenience. Stock issued during the election can never be QSBS and revoking doesn't cure it; an election taken after issuance can taint shares that were clean. Can cost millions at exit.
- Skipping the 83(b) election because the founder didn't know about it or thought it didn't apply. Painful and irreversible.
- Accepting a "pre-money option pool" without negotiating its size. A 10% pre-money pool reduces effective valuation by ~10% — often more impactful than the headline number that founders fixate on.
- Splitting equity 50/50 with a handshake and no vesting. The single most common founder-breakup catastrophe — see `cofounders-equity.md`.
- Letting an employee or contractor work for months without signed IP assignment paperwork. The work they produce may not legally belong to the company.
- Promising equity verbally to early employees ("we'll figure it out, you'll get 1%") without board approval, stock option agreements, or 409A valuations. These promises become disputes at exit — the co-founder version of the same mistake is in `cofounders-equity.md`.
- Hiring a generalist lawyer instead of a startup specialist. Startup law has its own conventions and a non-specialist will either overcharge or miss things.
- Giving up board seats to angel investors. Board seats are scarce and powerful; once granted, almost impossible to take back. Use observer rights or just regular investor updates instead — but treat observer rights as the fallback you concede, not a default you offer, because they multiply across rounds (see `fundraising.md`).
- Signing a lease or credit-line guarantee without capping it, negotiating a burn-down, or writing it down anywhere. Founders who can't list their personal guarantees from memory find out what they signed at the worst possible moment.
- Skipping a payroll tax deposit to make payroll. The withheld portion is trust money and reaches responsible persons individually — stretching the IRS is not the same move as stretching a vendor.
- Talking to the IRS alone after a missed payroll deposit. Responsibility and willfulness are contestable findings, established through a formal interview and carrying appeal rights — get a tax controversy specialist before you answer.
- Cancelling D&O to save money during a wind-down. Claims-made coverage has to be live when the claim arrives, not when the conduct happened, and tail coverage must be elected on the insurer's schedule.
