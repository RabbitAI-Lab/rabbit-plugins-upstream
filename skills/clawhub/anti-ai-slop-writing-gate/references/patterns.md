# Pattern Guide

Use judgment. These are suspects, not a mechanical ban list. Preserve a phrase when it is precise, necessary, quoted, or genuinely part of the writer's voice.

## Voice preservation

- Make the minimum effective edit.
- Leave strong human sentences alone.
- Keep useful bluntness, humor, profanity, uncertainty, fragments, digressions, and uneven cadence.
- Do not make every paragraph equally polished or symmetrical.
- Do not make the writer more certain than the evidence allows.
- If the writer has established vocabulary, preserve it when it performs real work. Never insert signature phrases merely to imitate the writer.

## Common suspects

Rewrite when they add heat without information:

- unlock, transform, reimagine, elevate, supercharge
- leverage, utilize, facilitate, empower, streamline, foster
- robust, cutting-edge, innovative, game changer, paradigm shift
- pivotal, transformative, paramount, strategic imperative
- delve, tapestry, realm, beacon, multifaceted, meticulous, intricate
- future of work, fast-paced, ever-evolving, at scale
- this changes everything, it is important to note, at the end of the day
- in today's world, at its core, the reality is, the truth is, let's dive in

Adverbs such as `just`, `actually`, `simply`, `fundamentally`, `importantly`, `crucially`, `inherently`, and `inevitably` should stay only when they carry real emphasis, contrast, uncertainty, or spoken rhythm.

## Structural patterns

### Unnecessary contrast

Smell: “It's not X. It's Y.” or “The question isn't X; it's Y.”

Fix: State Y directly unless the contrast corrects a real misconception or carries necessary reasoning.

### Negative list

Smell: “Not X. Not Y. Z.”

Fix: Say Z.

### Throat-clearing

Smell: “Here's the thing,” “Let me be clear,” or “The uncomfortable truth is.”

Fix: Begin with the claim. Keep an admission when it reveals real uncertainty or character.

### Faux insight

Smell: “What nobody tells you,” “The part everyone misses,” or “What most people get wrong.”

Fix: Remove the self-flattering setup and make the claim stand on evidence.

### Dramatic reveal

Smell: a label or colon used to stage an ordinary claim as a revelation.

Fix: Write a plain sentence. Keep colons for real lists, labels, explanations, and quotations.

### Superficial analysis

Smell: trailing clauses built around `highlighting`, `underscoring`, `reflecting`, or `showcasing`.

Fix: Explain the actual mechanism or consequence.

### Importance puffery

Smell: “marks a pivotal moment,” “stands as a testament,” or “plays a vital role.”

Fix: State the concrete fact and let the reader judge its importance.

### Weasel attribution

Smell: “experts agree,” “studies show,” or “many argue.”

Fix: Name and verify the source, qualify the claim, or remove it.

### Abstract noun pileup

Smell: strategy, transformation, optimization, alignment, enablement, or innovation without an actor or mechanism.

Fix: Name who does what, under which constraint, and what changes.

### Grand metaphor

Smell: recasting an ordinary habit or function as infrastructure, fuel, an operating system, a weapon, or another grand metaphor.

Fix: State the literal mechanism. Keep the metaphor only when it adds precision or belongs naturally to the writer.

### Robotic rhythm

Smell: repeated sentence shapes, stacked punchy fragments, identical paragraph lengths, or too many three-part lists.

Fix: Combine fragments that perform drama instead of thought. Let cadence follow meaning.

### Rhetorical setup

Smell: “What if I told you…,” “Think about it,” “Plot twist,” or a self-answered question.

Fix: Make the point.

### Fake-profound ending

Smell: a mic-drop metaphor, slogan, recap, or aphorism that claims more depth than the piece earned.

Fix: End on the clearest supported consequence, decision, tension, question, or action.

### Formatting performance

Smell: decorative emoji headings, scattered bold emphasis, tiny sections, or bullets where prose is clearer.

Fix: Let structure follow content.

### Em-dash dependency

Smell: em dashes used as the default rhythm.

Fix: Prefer periods, commas, parentheses, or sentence restructuring. Keep an occasional dash when it clearly improves the line.

## Decision clarity

When the draft supports a decision, the reader should be able to identify:

- what is known and unknown;
- what information would improve the decision;
- what has already been decided and what remains open;
- who owns the decision or next action; and
- whether the message requests action or is informational only.

Use the minimum structure the channel needs. Do not turn a short note into a status template, but do not hide the ask or uncertainty inside polished background prose.
