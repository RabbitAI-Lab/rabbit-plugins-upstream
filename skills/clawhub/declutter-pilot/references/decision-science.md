# The Decision Science of Decluttering

## Why decluttering fails: decision fatigue

Every object in a cluttered space demands a micro-decision. Experimental
psychology has repeatedly shown decision quality degrades after a sequence of
choices (the "ego depletion" literature is contested in its strong form, but
the practical effect in sorting tasks is robust: people increasingly choose
"do nothing" as a session continues). At a kitchen table full of memorabilia,
"do nothing" means the pile survives.

The countermeasure isn't discipline — it's **sequencing**:

1. **Front-load easy, high-volume decisions.** Broken things, expired things,
   duplicates, things you forgot you owned. Difficulty 1–2 on our scale.
   These produce bags fast, and visible progress is the fuel that carries the
   session into harder territory.
2. **Never open the sentiment box first.** Photos, kid drawings, inherited
   items (difficulty 5) each take minutes and emotional bandwidth. Do them
   when momentum exists, one box per session, with a hard stop.
3. **Cap sessions at 20 minutes.** The sortie planner enforces it. Three
   20-minute sorties a week clear ~1 m³ a month — a whole garage season,
   with zero burnout and no half-sorted piles left in doorways.

## The physics: volume is the only honest metric

"Clean the garage" fails because it has no units. Liters have units.

- A full 60 L trash bag ≈ 20–25 minutes of easy sorting.
- A standard moving box ≈ 60 L.
- A hatchback load ≈ 800 L (rear seats folded).
- A mid-size garage that's "full" typically holds 4–8 m³ of stuff — that's
  60–130 bags, 5–10 car-loads. Seeing that number in advance converts dread
  into scheduling.

The planner ranks by **volume ÷ difficulty** — cubic meters of space freed
per unit of emotional labor. That ratio *is* the strategy.

## The cost of keeping (the space-rent model)

Storage isn't free. Rough market value of dry, usable indoor space:
**$180/m³/year** (small-unit self-storage rates minus margin; your apartment
is often *more* expensive per m³ than a storage unit, because you pay
residential rates for the volume).

Worked examples:

| Item | Volume | Cost/yr | Typical fate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Broken printer "to fix someday" | 40 L | $7 | never fixed |
| Box of cables | 60 L | $11 | 4 usable cables inside |
| Old textbooks | 45 L | $8 | superseded editions |
| Exercise bike as clothes rack | 150 L | $27 | used 0×/yr |

Add the unpriced costs: attention (every cleanup touches it again), guilt
(every glance recalls the intention), moving cost ($100+ per m³ on a
long-distance move). The `decide` command prices the space part; the rest you
supply yourself.

## The five decision tests

1. **12-month test.** Used in the last 12 months? (Seasonal gear: last two
   seasons.) If no — the base rate says you won't in the next 12 either.
2. **Replacement test.** Replaceable in under 30 minutes for under $30? The
   "might need it" hedge is usually cheaper to exercise later than to store
   now.
3. **Sunk-cost test.** "I paid $200 for it" — that money is spent. Keeping
   the object doesn't refund it; selling for $20 recovers $20 and stops the
   storage meter.
4. **Cost-of-keeping test.** Space rent + attention vs item value. An $11/yr
   box of cables storing $6 of usable cable is a losing position.
5. **Active-project test.** Does it serve a current project/life, or a
   hypothetical future self? Hypothetical selves don't pay storage; you do.

**Maybe pile discipline:** a maybe is a decision deferred with extra steps.
Allowed once: into a dated 30-day box. Anything still in the box at day 30
leaves without re-opening the decision. (Aging maybes get flagged by the
tool.)

## Sentiment items (difficulty 5): the protocol

- Photograph, keep the photo, release the object. The memory is in the
  moment, not the mass.
- One-box rule: a single "memory box" per person, bounded size. When it's
  full, something must leave before something enters.
- Inherited items: you are not the storage of ancestors' stuff. Keep the one
  object that carries the story; the story survives the rest.
- If every item is difficulty 5, that's not a decluttering problem — see the
  scope note below.

## Selling: the rule of thirds

Listing items for sale is a part-time job: photograph, list, answer messages,
schedule no-shows. Apply the rule of thirds:

- Sell only items worth **≥ $25 and listable in ≤ 10 minutes**.
- Everything else: donate (get the receipt — deductible value in many
  countries, $5–60 per item at thrift-store fair value).
- Bulk-lot the rest (cables, books, toys) — one listing, one pickup,
  everything gone.

## Donated-value table (thrift fair-value style)

| Category | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Clothing, good condition | $5–15 |
| Jeans/coats/boots | $10–30 |
| Small appliances, working | $10–40 |
| Books (per volume) | $1–4 |
| Furniture, solid, clean | $30–120 |

The manifest uses volume × $0.5/L capped to the table's shape — a heuristic,
not a tax assessment.

## Runbook: whole-house purge in a season

1. **Week 0 — registry, not action.** Walk each room with the phone; log the
   obvious 30–60 items with volumes (estimates fine). No decisions yet.
2. **Weeks 1–2 — easy volume.** Difficulty 1–2 only. Target: 1 m³ out. This
   is the visible-win phase; don't be tempted to do harder things.
3. **Weeks 3–6 — the middle.** Difficulty 3 (sell/donate decisions, partial
   duplicates). Manifest the donation runs; list the ≥$25 sellers.
4. **Weeks 7–10 — sentiment, rationed.** One box per sortie max. Photos
   first. 30-day boxes for maybes.
5. **Week 12 — reset.** `status` shows freed volume; re-inventory what's
   left. Most people discover 20–40% of the original volume left the house.

## Scope note (honest limits)

This tool is for clutter, not for hoarding disorder. Hoarding disorder is a
recognized clinical condition involving genuine distress at discarding;
forced clearouts typically make it worse. If this describes the household,
the useful move is professional support (CBT-based programs work); this tool
can later help structure gentle, consented sessions alongside that — never
as a shock treatment.
