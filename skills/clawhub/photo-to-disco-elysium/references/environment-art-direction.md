# Environment Art Direction

Treat the photograph as source material for an environmental concept painting, not as a surface that needs a painterly filter. Preserve the place's essential identity, then redesign how that place feels, reads, and organizes attention.

## Apply B2.5 reconstruction freedom

B2.5 sits between a substantial redesign and a near-total reimagining.

Preserve:

- the core building, geographic formation, or landmark identity;
- decisive silhouettes and major mass relationships;
- key roads, paths, shorelines, entrances, or navigable connections;
- the principal subject and enough spatial evidence that the source place remains recognizable.

Freely redesign when it strengthens the scene narrative:

- weather, cloud structure, time atmosphere, light direction, shadow depth, and color temperature;
- horizon placement, sky proportion, foreground occlusion, depth compression, and focal emphasis;
- limited local positions, secondary proportions, peripheral geometry, and non-essential detail;
- palette, edge behavior, material rhythm, and the boundary between form and atmosphere.

Do not turn the scene into another city or biome, delete its primary anchor, add unrelated buildings or people, or preserve the entire photograph beneath uniform painterly texture.

## Interpret the place before painting it

Build a visible scene narrative from source evidence and user-provided context. Ask: "What kind of place would this be as concept art?"

Use four concise decisions:

- `scene narrative`: what the place appears to be doing or enduring;
- `emotional thesis`: the dominant visible tension;
- `environmental mood`: weather, light, scale, quiet, pressure, exposure, or enclosure;
- `worldbuilding interpretation`: how visible architecture, wear, infrastructure, ecology, or occupation suggests a lived world without inventing real history.

Translate each decision into composition, atmosphere, light, palette, edge, and mark behavior. Avoid mood adjectives with no visible consequence.

## Reconstruct palette relationships

Preserve color relationships rather than literal colors. Record the source's warm-cool balance, value hierarchy, contrast path, and focal accent, then design:

- one target emotional palette;
- a dominant hue family;
- a shadow family;
- a light family;
- one or two restrained accent colors.

Let adjacent neutrals absorb nearby hues. A changed sky, non-literal shadow, or amplified warm passage is valid when it supports the narrative and preserves the original visual relationships.

## Redesign atmosphere as a subject

Sky, fog, snow, water, glare, and large shadow fields are compositional forces, not background fill.

- Merge clouds into a few large directional masses instead of painting literal cloudlets.
- Permit intensified weather, fog, dusk, overcast color, sunset, or directional light.
- Treat water as a broad color field with sparse long marks and broken reflections.
- Treat roads, floors, walls, roofs, and open ground as large perspective-bearing planes.
- Do not use dense small strokes to describe continuous fields.

Atmospheric redesign is not identity drift by itself. Judge it by whether the place anchors and scene logic survive.

## Allocate detail semantically

Mark scale depends on both information density and narrative importance.

For every major element, assign:

```text
identity importance: high | medium | low
detail budget: high | medium | low
```

Use broad low-frequency masses for low-information continuous regions: sky, water, fog, snowfields, roads, ordinary wall planes, roof planes, lawns, distant color fields, and large shadows.

Use selective directional precision for identity-bearing or narratively useful structures: landmark silhouettes, gothic spires, rose windows, carved capitals, special entrances, window rhythms, signs, railings, lamps, wires, roof structures, sculptures, tree trunks, main branches, foliage clusters, flowers, rock ridges, birds, and animal anatomy.

Do not preserve every tile, brick, air conditioner, leaf, wave, or window. Preserve the few details that make the object or place itself.

### Architecture

Use `broad structure, selective precision`:

- block buildings into large value and color masses;
- keep decisive silhouette, volume, rhythm, and perspective economical;
- restore finer lines and directional marks only around identity-bearing ornaments and structural joints;
- let ordinary wall and road detail disappear so landmark precision has meaning.

### Vegetation, rock, and animals

Do not reduce all nature to soft green or brown blobs.

- retain trunk and main-branch direction, foliage-cluster rhythm, and characteristic silhouette;
- retain rock planes, ridges, fractures, and terrain direction where they define geography;
- retain an animal's silhouette, pose, limb structure, head direction, and decisive texture;
- avoid leaf-by-leaf, feather-by-feather, fur-by-fur, or pebble-by-pebble rendering.

## Build depth hierarchy

Assign different jobs to the three depth layers:

- `foreground`: strongest material contrast, large dark or light occlusion, and a deliberate entry into the image;
- `middle ground`: highest semantic information, principal landmark, and scene narrative;
- `background`: atmospheric simplification, compressed values, larger masses, and greater merging with sky or weather.

Depth compression may move layers visually closer, enlarge the sky, alter the horizon, or make the foreground more obstructive. Preserve the essential route and anchor relationships rather than photographic depth uniformity.

## Build edge hierarchy

- `primary edges`: keep selected landmark silhouettes, focal structures, principal figures or animals, and important path intersections clear;
- `secondary edges`: break and soften ordinary buildings, vegetation clusters, and middle-ground transitions;
- `lost edges`: merge distance, fog, cloud, large shadow, and low-importance boundaries.

Avoid outlining every window, tree, cloud, or building equally. The strongest edge contrast belongs near the visual and narrative center.

## Branch distinctions

### Architecture environment

Prioritize landmark identity, road and entrance relationships, structural rhythm, signs of occupation, and selective precision in meaningful details. Weather and palette may change substantially. Do not replace the place with a generic ruin, fantasy city, or neon street.

### Natural landscape

Prioritize horizon, terrain or shoreline geometry, weather mass, major vegetation or rock silhouettes, water direction, and any visible animal anchor. Use the widest mark-scale contrast: very large strokes for sky, water, snow, or grass fields; finer directional marks for branches, ridges, rock fractures, flowers, or animals. Do not invent buildings, people, roads, or symbolic props to make the scene seem more narrative.

## Reject false success

Reject the result when:

- every region uses similar small oil strokes;
- the photo remains spatially and chromatically unchanged beneath texture;
- atmosphere is literal and passive rather than redesigned;
- architecture is either uniformly over-described or blurred into anonymous masses;
- trees, branches, rock, flowers, birds, or animals lose their defining structure;
- every edge is equally sharp;
- the result is stylish but no longer recognizably the same place or natural system.
