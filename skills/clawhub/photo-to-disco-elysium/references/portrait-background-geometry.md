# Portrait Background Field

Turn the uploaded portrait background into a source-derived character field. The field should create visible compositional tension without claiming a diagnosis, hidden biography, morality, or protected trait.

## Distill evidence before choosing a field

Identify two to four visible background facts: wall divisions, windows, doorways, furniture, lamps, signs, foliage, shadows, roads, horizon, architecture, or negative space. Preserve at most one literal object only when it is essential to identity or a user-provided narrative.

Do not translate every fact into a shape. Select the few facts that can support one primary field grammar, then rebuild them as one to four irregular painted masses. Add a restrained line rhythm only if it continues a source direction or reinforces the figure.

## Choose one primary field grammar

Choose one grammar, with at most one quiet supporting behavior:

- `flat_field_and_counterblock`: one broad ground against one or two resisting blocks; useful when a wall, open space, or light-dark division dominates;
- `vertical_pressure`: bands, partial grid segments, or interrupting uprights distilled from columns, walls, windows, poles, or doorframes;
- `diagonal_pressure`: wedges, dragged slashes, or tilted planes that continue a road, shadow, gesture, gaze, or architectural angle;
- `arc_or_enclosure`: an irregular ring, disk, or arc that isolates, shelters, repeats, or exposes the gaze;
- `black_field_or_erasure`: a dark absorbing mass, scrape, or absence that interrupts the figure rather than describing the room;
- `organic_absorption`: softened vegetation, crowd, cloth, or shadow reduced into merged, breathing masses.

Field masses must be painted and irregular: scraped, broken, dry-brushed, or partially lost where appropriate. They are not clean UI primitives or a mandatory assortment of circles, rectangles, and lines.

## Build relational color

Choose a dominant field family, a counter-field, and at most one high-saturation accent. Color meaning is relational:

- red may warm, threaten, perform, interrupt, or bind;
- blue may cool, isolate, discipline, distance, or shelter;
- green may suggest recovery, atmospheric unease, bureaucracy, growth, or absorption;
- yellow may expose, energize, caution, intellectualize, or make a region brittle;
- violet may join warmth and cold into ambiguity, fatigue, ceremony, or distance;
- bone, gray, brown, and black may create blankness, age, weight, silence, or resistance.

Do not assign one color a universal personality meaning. Contaminate nearby neutrals so the field feels painted into the same world as the face and clothing.

## Make the field act on the figure

Require at least two interactions:

- a field mass crops, replaces, or merges with a hair, shoulder, collar, or jaw edge;
- an arc or counterblock shelters, isolates, repeats, or exposes the gaze;
- a vertical pressure divides head from body or presses toward the skull;
- a diagonal continues a gesture, gaze, collar, or facial angle;
- a broken line passes behind and through selected silhouette edges;
- a color field is resisted by, reflected in, or released from the face.

Keep one hard-edged passage near the focal zone and let the remaining field soften, scrape away, or merge. The figure and field must feel co-designed.

## Reject decorative abstraction

Reject the portrait field when it is:

- a blurred copy of the photograph;
- generic smoke, mist, bokeh, splatter, or grunge;
- a random collection of shapes with no source relationship;
- equally detailed everywhere;
- cleaner and more graphic than the repainted face;
- copied from a recognizable game portrait, skill icon, UI motif, or artbook composition.
