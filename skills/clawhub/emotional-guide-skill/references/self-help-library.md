# Low-Intensity Emotional Self-Help Library

This page contains brief exercises for ordinary stress and mild-to-moderate emotional distress. They are careful paraphrases of authoritative self-help materials and broadly evidence-informed methods. They do not constitute a complete implementation of an official program and do not replace professional treatment.

## Principles for Use

- Listen first, then ask whether the user would like to try an exercise.
- Select only one exercise at a time. State the approximate time required and that the user may stop at any point.
- Ask about the user's actual experience afterward. Do not assume that the exercise must help.
- Do not use this page instead of a safety referral when the user faces immediate danger, prominent psychosis-like experiences, mania, severe dissociation, heavy substance effects, or an inability to maintain basic safety.
- If attention to breathing or the body intensifies panic, a trauma response, or dissociation, stop immediately and switch to external visual, tactile, and environmental orientation.

## 1. Reorient to the Present

Suitable for: an emotional surge, repetitive mental replay, mild anxiety, or stress overload.

1. Notice and gently name what is here: "I notice that anxiety/hurt is here."
2. Place both feet firmly and notice the support of the floor, chair, or an object in the hand.
3. Name three specific things that are visible and two sounds that are audible.
4. Return attention to the smallest real task immediately in front of the user.

Do not instruct the user to take large, deep breaths. If the user wishes, invite natural, comfortable breathing with a slightly slower exhale; stop if it becomes uncomfortable.

## 2. Notice and Name a Thought

Suitable for: treating a self-critical or catastrophic thought as established fact.

Change "I am definitely going to fail" into:

> I notice that my mind is producing the thought, "I am definitely going to fail."

The purpose is not to prove the thought wrong. It is to create a little observing distance so the user can choose the next step. Do not use this exercise to dismiss real danger or structural injustice.

## 3. Make a Little Room for an Emotion

Suitable for: struggling intensely against grief, shame, anger, or anxiety while still being able to remain safe.

Invite the user to notice where the emotion is felt in the body and describe its shape or movement. Let it be present for several seconds with natural breathing while also noticing the feet and the surrounding environment. Emphasize that allowing a feeling to exist briefly does not mean liking it, agreeing with it, or giving up on change.

If the emotion rises quickly, a flashback appears, or the user loses a sense of present reality, stop and return to external orientation.

## 4. Values and One Small Step

Suitable for: indecision, avoidance, or a situation in which pain cannot yet be removed but a next step is needed.

Ask only one item at a time:

- "What kind of person do you want to be in this situation?"
- "What is the smallest action through which that value could show up today?"

Keep the action concrete, low-cost, and reversible, such as drafting an unsent message, making an appointment, eating one meal, tidying for five minutes, or contacting a trusted person.

## 5. Examine a Thought More Evenly

Suitable for: a user who wants to examine a clearly identified automatic thought.

Help the user distinguish, in sequence: what happened, the automatic thought, the resulting emotion, evidence that supports the thought, evidence that does not fully fit it, and a fuller statement that does not sugarcoat the situation.

Example of a balanced statement:

> This feedback does point to a problem, but one mistake does not prove that I am incapable in every area. I can first identify the one part that most needs correction.

Do not cognitively reframe real discrimination, violence, or danger as the user merely thinking incorrectly.

## 6. Sort the Sphere of Control

Suitable for: a problem that feels enormous, chaotic, or entirely outside the user's control.

Sort the concern into three areas: "I can influence today," "requires another person or more time," and "cannot currently control." Choose only one action from the first area. Do not ask the user to construct a complete life plan at once.

## 7. Say One Non-Harming Sentence to Yourself

Suitable for: intense self-blame, shame, or disappointment without immediate danger.

Invite the user to imagine what they would say to someone they genuinely care about who had experienced the same event. Turn the most honest, least exaggerated sentence toward the self. For example:

> This really hurts right now, but being in pain does not mean I am unworthy of care.

Avoid hollow positivity or pressuring the user to believe something that directly contradicts their experience.

## 8. Map Real-World Support

Suitable for: loneliness, a need for company, or a difficulty larger than one conversation can hold.

Help the user identify three forms of real-world connection: someone who can listen, someone who can provide practical help, and a professional or public support entry point. One person may fill more than one role. Do not place the AI agent itself on the real-world support list.

## Source Boundaries

- Reorientation, noticing and naming, distancing from thoughts, making room for emotions, values-guided action, and self-kindness draw primarily on WHO's *Doing What Matters in Times of Stress* and the WHO/ITU chatbot adaptation package. See S1-S3 in [source-register.md](source-register.md).
- The appropriate scope of structured self-help draws on the WHO 2026 implementation materials. See S4-S5.
- Thought examination, problem decomposition, and motivational communication use general evidence-informed principles only as low-intensity education and reflection. Do not claim to deliver a complete CBT, ACT, or psychotherapy protocol.
