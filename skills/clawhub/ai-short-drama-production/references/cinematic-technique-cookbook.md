# Cinematic Technique Cookbook for Original Short Drama

Use this reference to select storytelling, staging, transition, and VFX techniques. Learn principles from successful filmmakers; do not reproduce protected scenes, costumes, dialogue, music, character designs, or recognizable shot-for-shot sequences.

## 1. Originality rule

Named filmmakers are references for craft analysis, not templates to clone. Transform every influence through:

- original character design and relationships;
- new scene geography and action;
- new dialogue and jokes;
- new shot order and blocking;
- new production design and palette;
- new sound and music.

A technique is valid only when it serves story, character, or retention. A stylish shot that does not change information, power, emotion, or physical state is decoration.

## 2. Retention-oriented episode spine

Use as a diagnostic, not a guarantee of virality:

- 0–3s: impossible image, humiliation, threat, decision, or contradiction;
- 3–10s: promise of a concrete problem;
- 10–30s: pressure and escalation;
- 30–60s: complication or reversal;
- 60–90s: cost, sacrifice, or reveal;
- 90–110s: decisive action or emotional payoff;
- 110–120s: irreversible consequence and next question.

“爆款” is not guaranteed. Optimize for clarity, curiosity, emotional contrast, rewatch value, and a strong shareable moment.

## 3. Filmmaker principle matrix

### Stephen Chow — underdog comedy with emotional truth

Use for comedy-fantasy, ensemble satire, and underdog stories.

- protagonist keeps dignity while being humiliated;
- straight-faced performance inside absurd rules;
- rule-of-three escalation, then a fourth beat that breaks the pattern;
- visual gag combined with a sincere emotional beat;
- fast tonal switch from silly to tender;
- eccentric supporting characters with one strong want each;
- a catchphrase or gesture that changes meaning across the episode.

AI translation: alternate wide visual gags with close-up sincerity; keep the character’s face consistent while the situation becomes absurd.

### Johnnie To — precision ensemble and sudden violence

Use for standoffs, triads, suspense, and group power scenes.

- static wide shot establishes geometry before conflict;
- triangular or asymmetrical blocking reveals alliances;
- off-screen threat creates pressure;
- long restraint followed by a short burst of action;
- parallel action lines with precise screen direction;
- environment—neon, glass, stairs, corridors—organizes power;
- sound drops out before the decisive beat.

AI translation: lock geography and eyelines; use a master/wide, then insert/reaction shots; do not overcut the standoff.

### Jackie Chan — prop-driven action comedy

Use for physical comedy and clear action.

- environment and props are set up before use;
- action is readable in wide shots;
- objects change function under pressure;
- comic recovery after failure;
- body movement has weight and consequence;
- no hidden teleportation between shots.

AI translation: shot-list the prop state and hand; use wide/medium/insert/reaction coverage; keep action continuous across generated clips.

### Bong Joon-ho — vertical space and tonal control

Use for social satire, class conflict, and genre mixing.

- vertical architecture encodes power;
- ordinary objects carry symbolic pressure;
- tonal shifts are staged through space, not explained;
- ensemble actions cascade from one mistake;
- comedy and dread share the same frame.

AI translation: use stairs, balconies, basement windows, and layered foreground; keep the same prop visible across the tonal shift.

### Hayao Miyazaki — quiet observation and world texture

Use for family fantasy, nature, and wonder.

- let the audience watch a character notice something;
- weather, food, wind, and small creatures build world texture;
- strong silhouettes and readable flight/vertical movement;
- quiet beats create contrast before action;
- child POV and curiosity drive discovery.

AI translation: allocate 2–4 seconds to observation; do not fill every frame with dialogue or effects.

### Edgar Wright — rhythmic transitions and visual echoes

Use for montage, comedy, and setup/payoff.

- match cuts on shape, motion, color, or sound;
- repeated visual motif with a changed meaning;
- sound-sync edits;
- whip pan or object wipe used at a beat change;
- visual setup must pay off within the scene or episode.

AI translation: use one match cut per montage, not five; plan the outgoing and incoming frame shapes.

### Hitchcock — suspense through audience knowledge

Use for threat, secrets, and cliffhangers.

- audience knows more than the character;
- POV and reaction shots create anticipation;
- delay the reveal;
- confined space and a ticking clock;
- ordinary object becomes threatening.

AI translation: use insert and reaction shots; keep the threat visible or audible before the character notices it.

### Akira Kurosawa — weather, movement, and group staging

Use for action, period drama, and ensemble movement.

- weather increases pressure;
- deep staging and group movement create scale;
- motion continuity across cuts;
- foreground/midground/background layers;
- stillness before a burst of movement.

AI translation: specify rain, wind, dust, crowd direction, and motion continuity in the shot manifest.

### Wong Kar-wai — memory, framing, and repetition

Use for romance, regret, and emotional montage.

- foreground obstruction and framing through objects;
- repetition of a gesture or location with changed emotion;
- time and memory as structure;
- color and music carry subtext;
- incomplete conversations and glances.

AI translation: use close framing, slow motion, texture, and repeated visual motifs; avoid overloading the plot.

### Steven Spielberg — wonder and reaction

Use for adventure, family, and spectacle.

- reaction shot proves the scale of the event;
- oner or moving master reveals geography;
- wonder is timed before danger;
- blocking keeps the family/team readable inside spectacle.

AI translation: show the event, then the character’s reaction, then the consequence. Do not show spectacle without a human response.

## 4. Transition cookbook

| Transition | Best use | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| cut on action | action continuity | cutting before the action completes |
| match cut | visual rhyme, montage | forced similarity |
| whip pan | comic shock, scene change | more than once per short sequence |
| sound bridge | time/space jump | unexplained voice perspective |
| J-cut | lead into next scene | spoiling a reveal |
| L-cut | carry emotion across cut | audio continuity errors |
| object wipe | prop-driven transition | object changing shape |
| foreground wipe | crowd/location change | blocking the subject’s face |
| shape match | theme or visual echo | random graphic similarity |
| color match | emotional continuity | ignoring scene palette |
| hard cut | confrontation, punchline | confusing geography |
| smash cut | tonal shock | overuse |
| speed ramp | impact, realization | hiding a weak action |
| freeze frame | chapter end, punchline | pretending a broken shot is intentional |

## 5. Effects grammar

Use one hero effect per 10–15 seconds, plus secondary atmosphere.

- fire/ink/water/smoke: use as transition or emotional punctuation;
- particles: reveal, impact, memory, or magic, not wallpaper;
- speed ramp: impact or realization;
- light flash: power shift;
- shadow wipe: threat or reveal;
- freeze frame: end card or comic punctuation;
- split screen: parallel action only when both lines matter;
- match dissolve: memory or transformation;
- practical-style lens flare: wonder, not every shot.

Do not stack particles, speed ramps, camera shake, lens flares, and color grading in the same shot unless the story demands a single overwhelming event.

## 6. Sequence recipes

### Comedy reversal — 6 shots

1. wide: protagonist attempts something with confidence;
2. medium: obstacle appears;
3. close-up: confidence cracks;
4. insert: prop fails;
5. reaction: crowd reacts;
6. wide: dignity restored through a new tactic or sincere beat.

### Ensemble standoff — 5 shots

1. static wide: geography and factions;
2. medium A: leader states demand;
3. medium B: opposing leader answers;
4. insert: object or hand reveals leverage;
5. wide: power balance changes; one side moves.

### Prop-driven action — 7 shots

1. wide: establish prop and space;
2. medium: protagonist grabs prop;
3. insert: prop detail;
4. wide: action begins;
5. medium: prop changes function;
6. reaction: consequence;
7. wide: new geography and exit.

### Emotional realization — 4 shots

1. medium: character looks at evidence;
2. insert: evidence;
3. close-up: realization;
4. wide or slow pull-out: emotional isolation.

### World reveal — 5 shots

1. detail: anomaly;
2. reaction: character notices;
3. wide: scale of world;
4. movement: enter the world;
5. detail: new rule established.

## 7. Technique budget

Per episode select:

- one primary storytelling technique;
- one primary staging technique;
- one primary transition family;
- one hero VFX motif;
- one recurring visual or sound gag.

More technique variety is not more quality. Repeat a motif until it changes meaning.

## 8. AI prompt translation

A transition prompt must state:

- outgoing shot final frame;
- incoming shot first frame;
- transition mechanism;
- camera motion;
- light/color match;
- sound bridge;
- continuity constraints.

A VFX prompt must state:

- source and direction of effect;
- what it interacts with;
- what remains unchanged;
- beginning, peak, and end state;
- whether the effect is magical, practical, or environmental.

## 9. Red flags

- named-director imitation without an original story reason;
- copying a recognizable scene or character design;
- more than two transition types in one short scene;
- effects used to hide unclear action;
- no reaction shot after a spectacle;
- camera movement with no motivation;
- comedy that humiliates without a dignity/payoff beat;
- standoff coverage that breaks screen direction;
- montage without a visual or sound rhyme;
- “爆款” promised instead of retention tested.
