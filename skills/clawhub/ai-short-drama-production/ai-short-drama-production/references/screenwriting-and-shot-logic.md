# Screenwriting, Scene Logic, Coverage, and Continuity

Use this reference when writing or reviewing a short-drama script that must survive production, not merely read well on the page.

## 1. Story spine

Lock these before episode writing:

- **promise:** what the audience is promised in the first 10 seconds;
- **protagonist want:** the concrete external goal;
- **protagonist need:** the internal truth they resist;
- **false belief/flaw:** the behavior that creates avoidable trouble;
- **pressure:** who or what prevents the goal;
- **price:** what each victory costs;
- **payoff:** the irreversible choice or change that resolves the promise.

A short drama needs a visible goal, visible resistance, and a cost. A theme without a concrete problem is not a plot.

## 2. Character logic

For each principal character record:

- public goal and private need;
- fear, shame, secret, and contradiction;
- status inside the group;
- relationship map and power imbalance;
- speech rhythm, vocabulary, sentence length, verbal tic;
- what they will never say directly;
- what action reveals their true feeling;
- start-state and end-state for the season.

Dialogue should sound like a specific person under pressure, not like a theme statement.

## 3. Episode architecture for 90–120 seconds

Use this as a diagnostic, not a rigid template:

| Time | Function |
|---|---|
| 0–5s | visual anomaly, danger, decision, or impossible image |
| 5–20s | establish goal and pressure with minimal exposition |
| 20–55s | escalate through action and opposition |
| 55–80s | turn: new information, betrayal, cost, or reversal |
| 80–105s | choice and payoff |
| 105–120s | cliffhanger, new question, or irreversible consequence |

Every episode must change at least one of these:

- knowledge;
- power;
- relationship;
- physical safety;
- ownership;
- identity;
- moral position.

If nothing changes, the scene is filler.

## 4. Scene-level logic

For every scene define:

- **POV:** whose experience organizes the scene;
- **objective:** what the POV character wants now;
- **obstacle:** why they cannot get it;
- **tactic:** what they do to overcome it;
- **turn:** what changes by the end;
- **exit:** a decision, discovery, threat, or emotional rupture.

A scene should contain a change. A scene that only explains the plot is usually dialogue coverage begging to be cut.

### Scene test

- Does the scene start late and end early?
- Is the objective playable as action?
- Is the obstacle visible or audible?
- Does the tactic change at least once?
- Is the turn caused by character choice, not coincidence?
- Is the exit stronger than the entry?
- Can any line be replaced by a look, object, or action?
- Does the scene advance plot, character, or theme in at least two ways?

## 5. Dialogue craft

- Write for subtext: characters negotiate, evade, threaten, bargain, or test each other.
- Use interruptions, unfinished sentences, and silences where they create pressure.
- Avoid exposition characters who explain what everyone already knows.
- Avoid repeating the same information in dialogue and visual action unless the repetition creates irony.
- Let power determine sentence length: powerful characters can speak slowly; cornered characters rush.
- Give each principal character a distinct rhythm.
- Put the most important line at the end of the beat, not the middle.
- Do not use classical quotations, laws, medical claims, or statistics unless source-checked.

### Dialogue compaction pass

Convert:

- “I am very angry because you lied to me” → action + shorter line;
- “As you know, the rule is…” → conflict that reveals the rule;
- “I will definitely get revenge” → a precise threat or irreversible action.

## 6. Season and episode continuity

Maintain a continuity ledger for:

- timeline and duration;
- locations and geography;
- character knowledge;
- injuries, costumes, props, money, and evidence;
- relationship changes;
- promises, debts, threats, and secrets;
- setups and payoffs;
- open loops and resolved loops;
- episode-ending cliffhanger type.

Each episode should inherit at least one unresolved pressure and leave one new consequence.

### Cliffhanger types

Rotate among:

- decision;
- discovery;
- danger;
- reversal;
- deadline;
- emotional rupture;
- moral compromise.

Do not end every episode with the same “someone appears” or “phone rings” device.

## 7. Coverage design

Coverage is not “more shots.” It is the minimum set that preserves geography, emotion, and edit flexibility.

### Dialogue coverage

Typical pattern:

1. master or two-shot for geography;
2. over-the-shoulder A;
3. over-the-shoulder B;
4. close-up on the character making the decision;
5. reaction close-up on the listener;
6. insert for evidence, object, hand, or screen;
7. cutaway or establishing reorientation when geography changes.

Maintain:

- 180-degree rule;
- consistent eyelines;
- screen direction;
- body orientation;
- prop hand;
- light direction;
- background continuity.

### Action coverage

Typical pattern:

1. wide/geography shot;
2. medium action shot;
3. close impact/emotion shot;
4. insert detail;
5. reaction shot;
6. end-state shot.

Do not place the camera where it hides the cause-and-effect of the action.

### Montage or transition coverage

- establish a visual rhyme;
- use match cuts on shape, motion, color, or sound;
- keep one continuous idea per montage;
- avoid changing location, time, and emotion simultaneously without a bridge.

## 8. Shot language

### Shot sizes

- EWS: geography and isolation;
- WS: full body and spatial relation;
- MWS: action plus environment;
- MS: waist-up dialogue and gesture;
- MCU: emotional pressure;
- CU: decision or reaction;
- ECU: detail, trigger, or sensory emphasis;
- insert: evidence or prop;
- POV: subjective access.

### Camera moves

Use a move only when it changes meaning:

- static: observation, pressure, deadlock;
- pan/tilt: reveal or connect;
- push-in: realization, threat, intimacy;
- pull-out: isolation or consequence;
- tracking: pursuit or commitment;
- orbit: unstable power relation or spectacle;
- handheld: urgency, chaos, subjectivity;
- crane/jib: scale or fate;
- rack focus: shift of attention or power;
- whip pan: violent transition or comic shock.

One 4–8 second shot should usually carry one dominant action and one dominant camera intention.

## 9. Multi-shot prompt design

For a 15–30 second generation block:

- list shot boundaries in order;
- give each shot a purpose;
- keep character and prop IDs explicit;
- repeat the scene geography once;
- state what must remain continuous: costume, light direction, prop hand, screen direction, weather;
- specify the end frame or transition;
- do not stack more than 4 major actions in 30 seconds.

If a prompt asks for a push-in, orbit, whip pan, and action beat in the same 5 seconds, simplify it.

## 10. Prompt layers

Use this order:

1. identity anchor;
2. outfit/prop IDs;
3. scene and time;
4. first-frame composition;
5. action sequence;
6. camera intention;
7. lighting/color;
8. emotion/performance;
9. VFX/particles;
10. continuity constraints;
11. end state;
12. technical format.

Separate performance direction from camera direction. “He decides” is performance; “slow push-in” is camera.

## 11. Consistency dimensions

Check every accepted shot for:

- identity: face, eyes, ears, hair/forehead mark, body ratio;
- costume: garment, badge, accessory, damage state;
- props: type, hand, position, condition;
- geography: entrances, exits, furniture, crowd;
- eyeline and screen direction;
- light direction, color temperature, shadow;
- time of day and weather;
- motion speed and weight;
- lens/scale and depth of field;
- VFX state.

## 12. Script and shot QC

### Script QC

- promise is visible by 10 seconds;
- protagonist has a concrete goal;
- obstacle escalates;
- each scene has a turn;
- dialogue is speakable and character-specific;
- setups are paid off;
- cliffhanger type rotates;
- facts, laws, quotations, and titles are verified;
- runtime is plausible when read aloud.

### Shot QC

- shot has a story function;
- coverage preserves geography and eyelines;
- camera move has motivation;
- action is physically possible;
- prompt has one dominant intention;
- character/scene/prop references are explicit;
- end state can connect to the next shot;
- text is planned for post;
- voice and lip-sync are feasible.

## 13. Red flags

- characters explain the theme instead of pursuing a goal;
- scenes end where they began;
- every episode uses the same cliffhanger;
- camera movement replaces dramatic staging;
- too many characters in one short scene;
- important information exists only in generated text;
- a power-up or reversal has no cost;
- a prop appears without setup;
- dialogue is longer than the action can support;
- the shot cannot be edited into the neighboring shot.
