# Milton Erickson: Ericksonian Hypnotherapy

## CHAPTER 1: WHO MILTON ERICKSON WAS

Milton Hyland Erickson (December 5, 1901 — March 25, 1980) was an American psychiatrist and psychologist, regarded as the "father of modern hypnotherapy." His revolutionary approach changed not only hypnosis, but all of psychotherapy.

**Why he is called a genius:**
- Created Ericksonian (indirect, permissive) hypnosis
- Founded the American Society of Clinical Hypnosis (1957)
- Influenced the development of: brief therapy, strategic family therapy, NLP, solution-focused therapy
- Worked with "hopeless" cases that others refused
- Turned hypnosis from a fairground trick into a recognized therapeutic method

**Education and career:**
- 1928: MA in psychology and MD (medicine), University of Wisconsin
- Director of psychiatric research at Wayne County Hospital, Michigan
- 1948: Clinical director of Arizona State Hospital
- From 1949: Private practice in Phoenix, Arizona (30 years)
- First president of the American Society of Clinical Hypnosis
- Founder and editor of the American Journal of Clinical Hypnosis

**Unique characteristics:**
- Color-blind (could not distinguish colors)
- Tone-deaf (could not hear music)
- Dyslexia
- Survived polio twice (at 17 and at 51)
- Spent the last 30 years of his life in a wheelchair
- Managed chronic pain using self-hypnosis

## CHAPTER 2: DID ERICKSON LIVE HIS OWN TEACHING?

**One of the most dramatic stories in the history of psychotherapy.**

### The first polio (1919)

At 17, Milton Erickson fell ill with a severe form of polio. The doctor told his parents: "Your son will not live until morning."

Young Milton lay in bed, completely paralyzed, unable to speak or move. He could only move his eyes and hear.

**What he did:**

Instead of giving up, Erickson began to observe. He noticed the tiniest details of nonverbal communication — how his sisters' gestures contradicted their words, how his parents' voices changed, how the people around him moved.

He also began recalling "body memory" — the sensations of how his muscles used to move. Concentrating on these memories, he slowly, micro-movement by micro-movement, began to restore control over his body.

**The first movement:**

One day, looking out the window at a rocking chair, he wanted so intensely to look at it that the chair shifted slightly — his body moved almost imperceptibly. That was the first step.

After nearly a year he could speak and use his arms. Against the doctors' advice to rest, he set off on a solo 1,200-mile canoe trip down the Mississippi. He returned with his physical strength restored.

### The second polio (1953)

At 51, Erickson fell ill again. Post-polio syndrome left him even more paralyzed. He was confined to a wheelchair, partially paralyzed on one side of his face, suffering from chronic pain.

**How he coped:**

He used self-hypnosis to manage the pain — the same method he taught his patients. He continued seeing patients, teaching, writing. He worked until his death in 1980.

### What this means:

Erickson did not merely "live his teaching." He *created* his teaching out of his own suffering:

- **Observational skill:** learned to read nonverbal signals while paralyzed
- **Utilization:** used his limitations as tools
- **Unconscious resources:** discovered "body memory" from his own experience
- **The individual approach:** understood that standard methods don't work for non-standard situations
- **Self-hypnosis:** managed his own pain for decades

The man who taught the world that the unconscious is a source of healing healed himself using exactly that.

## CHAPTER 3: THE BASIC PRINCIPLES OF THE ERICKSONIAN APPROACH

### 1. The unconscious as a resource

**Traditional psychoanalysis (Freud):** The unconscious is a storehouse of repressed conflicts, dark desires, a source of pathology.

**Erickson:** The unconscious is a vast reservoir of wisdom, creativity, and resources for healing.

"The unconscious mind is always listening. And it is capable of solving problems creatively, better than the conscious mind."

### 2. Utilization

Instead of fighting what the patient brings (resistance, symptoms, beliefs, habits), Erickson **used** it as an entry point for change.

If the patient resists — use the resistance.
If the patient believes in magic — use metaphors of magic.
If the patient is silent — use the silence.

### 3. The individual approach

"Each person is unique. Hence, psychotherapy should be formulated to meet the uniqueness of the individual's needs, rather than tailoring the person to fit the Procrustean bed of a hypothetical theory of human behavior."

Erickson never used the same approach twice. He created a new therapy for each patient.

### 4. Indirect suggestion

**Traditional hypnosis:** "Close your eyes. You are falling asleep. When I count to three, you will stop smoking."

**Erickson:** "I wonder whether you'll notice how your eyelids are growing a little heavier... and perhaps you'll want to let them close... when your unconscious is ready..."

Indirect suggestions:
- Bypass conscious resistance
- Give the patient the illusion of control
- Activate the patient's own resources
- Respect autonomy

### 5. Orientation toward solutions, not problems

In an era when therapy lasted for years and dug into the past, Erickson was a revolutionary:

- Focus on the future, not the past
- Searching for what works, not what is broken
- A single session could create change
- "Insight" into the cause of the problem is not needed for healing

## CHAPTER 4: KEY TECHNIQUES

### 1. Therapeutic metaphors and stories

Erickson was a great storyteller. Instead of direct instructions he told stories — about his patients, about his farm, about his children.

**Why stories work:**
- They bypass conscious criticism
- They speak the language of the unconscious (images, emotions)
- They let the patient find the meaning themselves
- They are remembered better than instructions

**Example:** Instead of telling a child with enuresis "control your bladder," Erickson spent hours telling him about baseball — about fine muscular coordination, about how important it is to release the ball *at the right moment*, not too early and not too late. The boy stopped wetting the bed without ever realizing it had been therapy.

### 2. Confusion and overload (the Confusion Technique)

Erickson used long, convoluted sentences, grammatically incorrect constructions, paradoxical statements — to "overload" the conscious mind and open access to the unconscious.

While consciousness tries to figure out what is happening, the unconscious becomes open to suggestions.

### 3. Double binds

The illusion of choice between two options, both of which lead to the desired outcome.

**Examples:**
- "Do you want to feed the chickens first or the pigs?" (the work will be done)
- "Will you stop smoking before Christmas or after?" (you will stop)
- "Will your bed be dry on Wednesday or on Thursday?" (it will be dry)

### 4. Reframing

Changing the frame within which a person perceives a situation.

**The "African Violet Queen" example:** To a depressed woman who grew African violets, Erickson said: "Your problem is not depression. Your problem is that you are a poor Christian — you have a gift, and you keep it to yourself."

This was a reframe: from "I am sick" to "I am not giving enough."

### 5. Prescribing the symptom

Instead of fighting the symptom — prescribe it.

**Patient:** "I can't stop biting my nails."
**Erickson:** "Fine. Bite them for exactly 15 minutes every hour. Set a timer."

Turning involuntary behavior into voluntary behavior often leads to its disappearance.

### 6. Shock therapy and ordeals

Sometimes Erickson used shock to break a pattern.

**Example:** A man paralyzed after a stroke could not speak. Erickson insulted him so harshly that the man got up, walked out of the room, and told Erickson everything he thought of him — using muscles that "didn't work."

### 7. The handshake as trance induction

The famous technique: Erickson would begin a handshake normally, then unexpectedly interrupt or modify it (for example, touching the patient's wrist or watch). This interruption of the expected pattern created a moment of bewilderment — and opened a window for suggestion.

## CHAPTER 5: FAMOUS CASES

### Case 1: "The African Violet Queen"

**The patient:** A 53-year-old wealthy woman, deeply depressed, no friends, no family. She lived alone in a huge dark house. Possibly suicidal.

**What Erickson did:**

Entering her home, he noticed only one bright spot — a greenhouse with magnificent African violets. The woman came alive showing them to him.

Erickson told her:
"I see your problem. You are not a very good Christian. You have this gift — and you keep it to yourself. Get the church bulletin. Every time someone is born — send a violet. A christening — a violet. An illness — a violet. An engagement — a violet. A wedding — a violet."

**The result:**

Twenty years later the local newspaper ran the headline: "The African Violet Queen of Milwaukee has died... Mourned by thousands."

**Analysis:**

When a student asked, "Why didn't you treat her depression?", Erickson answered:

"I decided it was easier to grow the part of her life with the violets than to weed out the depression."

### Case 2: The boy with enuresis and baseball

**The patient:** A 12-year-old boy with nocturnal enuresis.

**What Erickson did:**

He sent the parents out and never once mentioned the problem. Instead, he spent hours talking with the boy about baseball — about how finely you need to coordinate the muscles of your arm when catching the ball, how important it is to open and close the glove correctly, how when throwing you must release the ball *at exactly the right moment*...

**The result:**

The enuresis stopped. The boy didn't even realize it had been therapy.

**Analysis:**

The baseball story was a metaphor for muscle control. The unconscious heard the message about proper "timing" and applied it to the bladder.

### Case 3: The nurse and suicide

**The patient:** A nurse at a Boston hospital who had announced to everyone that she was going to end her life. Two years in psychoanalysis; everyone was "prepared" for her death.

**What Erickson did:**

During a hypnosis demonstration he chose her as a subject — because he saw how responsive she was. When colleagues warned him about her suicidality, he answered:

"If I refuse to use her, that could be the final rejection that pushes her to suicide."

He conducted a session, using her responsiveness to hypnosis as a resource.

**The result:**

She did not take her life.

## CHAPTER 6: UTILIZATION — THE HEART OF THE APPROACH

Utilization is the principle by which the therapist uses *everything* the patient brings as a resource for change.

### What can be utilized:

| What the patient brings | How to utilize it |
|------------------------|-------------------|
| Resistance | "You can resist — and, in resisting, you will discover..." |
| Negative beliefs | Use their structure for positive suggestions |
| Habits | Turn them into a resource |
| Cultural background | Use familiar metaphors |
| Bodily characteristics | Work with what is there |
| Symptoms | Prescribe or reframe them |
| The patient's language | Use their words, not yours |

### An example of utilizing resistance:

**Patient:** "I can't be hypnotized."
**Traditional approach:** "Let's try; maybe it will work."
**Erickson:** "True, you cannot be hypnotized — at least, not the way you imagine it. But perhaps you will notice something else..."

## CHAPTER 7: TRANCE — NOT WHAT YOU THINK

### Everyday trance

Erickson showed that trance is not some exotic state. We enter and leave trance constantly:

- Daydreamed and missed your stop
- Absorbed in a book and didn't hear a question
- Driving a car on autopilot
- Dreaming while gazing out the window

Therapeutic trance is the deliberate use of this natural state.

### The therapist is in trance too

Erickson entered trance together with the patient:

"I go into trance so that I will be more sensitive to the intonations of the patient's speech. To hear better, to see better."

### Trance as learning

For Erickson, trance was not a state of passivity but a state of heightened learnability. In trance the unconscious is open to new patterns.

## CHAPTER 8: INFLUENCE ON MODERN PSYCHOTHERAPY

### Neuro-Linguistic Programming (NLP)

Richard Bandler and John Grinder studied Erickson in the 1970s, trying to understand "what makes some therapists more effective."

The result: the "Milton Model" — a set of Erickson's language patterns that became the foundation of NLP.

### Brief Therapy

Before Erickson, therapy lasted for years. He showed that change could happen in a single session.

Influence on:
- The Mental Research Institute (Palo Alto)
- Strategic therapy
- Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT)

### Strategic family therapy

Jay Haley, who worked with Erickson, carried his ideas into family therapy. The book "Uncommon Therapy" (1973) is a catalogue of Erickson's techniques.

### Solution-Focused Brief Therapy

Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg developed Ericksonian principles into a separate approach:
- Focus on solutions, not problems
- Using the client's resources
- A minimal number of sessions

### Narrative therapy

Erickson's idea of therapeutic stories influenced the development of the narrative approach.

## CHAPTER 10: PRACTICAL DIALOGUE EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Indirect suggestion

**Traditional hypnosis:**
"Close your eyes. Relax. You are falling asleep, deeply and calmly."

**The Ericksonian approach:**
"And as you sit there... and perhaps notice how your breathing is slowing down all by itself... you can allow yourself to wonder... which hand will become more relaxed first — the right or the left... and while you decide... your eyes may want to close... or stay open... until your unconscious decides it is ready..."

### Example 2: Double bind

**Patient:** A teenager who doesn't do homework.

**Parent:** "Do your homework!"

**The Ericksonian approach:**
"I wonder — will you start with math, because it's the hardest, or with English, because it's quicker? Which subject will you choose first?"

(The choice is between subjects, not between "doing" and "not doing")

### Example 3: Therapeutic metaphor

**Patient:** A woman stuck in a toxic relationship.

**The Ericksonian approach:**
"You know, I once had a patient... her grandmother kept an old withered bush in the garden. Every day she watered it. Every spring she waited for it to bloom. Years went by. One day the granddaughter asked: 'Grandma, why do you keep watering a dead bush?' The grandmother answered: 'Because I've already invested so much in it.' I wonder... what would you advise that grandmother?"

## CHAPTER 11: KEY QUOTES FROM ERICKSON

**On the unconscious:**
"The unconscious mind is the storehouse of all our lifetime learning. Much of it works automatically, helping us function."

**On individuality:**
"Each person is unique. There has never been and never will be a copy of them. From birth to death."

**On therapy:**
"Psychotherapy should be formulated to meet the uniqueness of the individual's needs, rather than tailoring the person to fit the Procrustean bed of a hypothetical theory."

**On resources:**
"Patients are patients because they have lost touch with their resources. They know how to solve their problems — but they don't know that they know."

**On problems:**
"The problem is not the problem. The problem is the way you relate to it."

**On himself:**
"I am not a former polio victim. I am a doctor who had polio, who uses that experience to better understand his patients."

## CHAPTER 12: WHEN TO USE THE ERICKSONIAN APPROACH

**Especially effective for:**
- Anxiety disorders and phobias
- Chronic pain
- Psychosomatic symptoms
- Bad habits (smoking, overeating)
- Being stuck in problem patterns
- Resistance to therapy
- When standard methods haven't worked

**Works well for:**
- Depression (especially mild and moderate)
- Difficulties making decisions
- Self-esteem problems
- Interpersonal difficulties
- Motivational problems

**Requires caution:**
- Acute psychotic states
- Severe trauma (preparation needed)
- When the patient needs structured work with beliefs (CBT)

## CHAPTER 14: INTEGRATION WITH THE OTHER AUTHORS OF THE PROJECT

Erickson occupies a unique position in the project as the master of **indirect influence**.

**The architecture of use:**

1. **Rogers:** Creates safety (HOW to relate)
2. **Frankl/Yalom:** Provides depth and meaning (WHY)
3. **Beck/Ellis:** Works with cognitions (WHAT to change in thinking)
4. **Linehan:** Provides concrete skills (WHAT to do)
5. **Erickson:** Bypasses resistance, activates unconscious resources (HOW to get past barriers)

**When to add Erickson:**
- When direct methods meet resistance
- When the patient "understands everything but cannot change"
- When a creative, non-standard approach is needed
- When other methods don't work

**Example of integration:**

A patient understands their cognitive distortions (Beck), understands their irrationality (Ellis), feels accepted (Rogers), but... doesn't change.

The Ericksonian approach: instead of one more explanation — a therapeutic story, an indirect suggestion, utilizing their "stubbornness" as a resource.
