# Steven Hayes: Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

## CHAPTER 1: WHO IS STEVEN HAYES

Steven C. Hayes (born August 12, 1948) is an American clinical psychologist, professor at the University of Nevada, and the creator of Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT — pronounced as the word "act," not as an acronym).

**Why he matters:**
- Created ACT — one of the most researched modern psychotherapies
- Developed Relational Frame Theory (RFT) — a scientific model of language and cognition
- Author of over 650 scientific articles and 47 books
- More than 1,000 randomized trials support the effectiveness of ACT
- Ranked among the 30 most cited psychologists in the world
- President of several major psychological associations

**Education and career:**
- PhD in clinical psychology, West Virginia University
- Professor of psychology, University of Nevada, Reno
- Founder of the Association for Contextual Behavioral Science (ACBS)
- Past president of the Association for Behavioral and Cognitive Therapies
- Recipient of the ABCT Lifetime Achievement Award

**Key publications:**
- "Get Out of Your Mind and Into Your Life" (2005) — bestseller
- "A Liberated Mind: How to Pivot Toward What Matters" (2019)
- "Acceptance and Commitment Therapy: The Process and Practice of Mindful Change" (2012)
- "Relational Frame Theory: A Post-Skinnerian Account of Human Language and Cognition" (2001)

## CHAPTER 2: DID HAYES LIVE HIS OWN TEACHING?

**ACT was born from a personal hell. Steven Hayes created the method that saved his life — and then proved it scientifically.**

### The night on the carpet

In 1978 Hayes — a young professor of psychology — was sitting in a department meeting. When he tried to say something, his mouth opened and closed, but no words came out. His heart was pounding so hard he thought it was a heart attack.

It was his first panic attack. He was 29 years old.

From his TED talk:
> "My journey did not begin with research — it began in the middle of the night 34 years ago, when my panic disorder threatened to take everything."

### Three years of hell

Over the next three years, the panic attacks claimed more and more territory of his life:
- He could not lecture without terror
- He could not step into an elevator
- He could not go to a movie theater
- He could not eat in a restaurant

He was a **trained behavioral and cognitive therapist** — and the methods he taught others did not work for him.

> "When I applied to myself the methods I would have used with panic patients, they did not give me what I needed."

### Childhood trauma

Something deeper lay behind the panic. Hayes grew up in a family where his parents fought viciously. At eight years old, he would hide under the bed, wondering whether he would see blood on the floor when he came out.

> "I would sit under the bed at eight years old, listening to my parents scream and throw things at each other."

This childhood trauma erupted decades later as panic in a department meeting.

### The moment of insight

One night, at two o'clock, on a beige-and-gold carpet, Hayes hit bottom. He saw no way out. He let out a strange, hoarse cry.

And then — in the silence — came a revelation:

**"I will not run from myself."**

This was not a decision to get rid of anxiety. It was a decision to **accept** it — and to move toward what matters, **along with it**.

> "I made the choice that changed my life: I will not run from myself."

### The science of acceptance

After that night, Hayes did not simply "recover" — he devoted his career to understanding what had happened. Why did **accepting** pain help more than **fighting** it?

Over the next 35+ years he:
- Developed the theoretical model (Relational Frame Theory)
- Created the therapeutic protocol (ACT)
- Conducted hundreds of studies
- Trained thousands of therapists around the world

Today, more than 40 years later, he says:
> "I haven't had a panic attack in over ten years."

### How trauma became method

| Hayes's experience | ACT principle |
|--------------------|---------------|
| Fighting anxiety made it worse | Acceptance, not control |
| Thoughts of "I'm broken" took over | Cognitive defusion |
| Losing himself in fear | The observing self (self-as-context) |
| Life stopped because of panic | Values-directed action |
| Meditation and Eastern practices helped | Mindfulness |
| The decision "not to run from myself" | Acceptance |

**Hayes is a rare case in psychotherapy: a scientist who created a method from personal experience and then proved its effectiveness through rigorous scientific research.**

## CHAPTER 3: THE CORE IDEA — ACT AND PSYCHOLOGICAL FLEXIBILITY

### The heart of the theory in one sentence:

**Suffering arises not from painful thoughts and feelings themselves, but from our struggle with them; healing comes through accepting inner experience and moving toward what truly matters.**

### ACT — what is it?

ACT (pronounced as the word "act," not as an acronym) stands for:
- **A**cceptance
- **C**ommitment
- **T**herapy

A therapy of **accepting** inner experience and **committing** to action based on values.

### The central concept: Psychological flexibility

**Psychological flexibility** is the ability to:
- Be open to your own thoughts and feelings
- Be present in the current moment
- Act in accordance with your values

> "Psychological flexibility is the ability to feel and think openly, to voluntarily direct attention to the present moment, and to move one's life in directions that matter to you."

### The opposite: Psychological rigidity

Psychological **rigidity** (inflexibility) is when:
- We "fuse" with thoughts and believe them literally
- We avoid unpleasant feelings
- We live on autopilot, not in the present
- We lose contact with our values
- We get stuck in ineffective patterns

> "Psychological rigidity is, at its core, an attempt to avoid the negative thoughts and feelings evoked by difficult experience."

### Why doesn't fighting work?

**The paradox of control:**
The more we try to control or get rid of unpleasant thoughts and feelings, the stronger they become.

Daniel Wegner's experiment: "Don't think of a white bear" — and you will think of nothing else.

> "Attempts to suppress a thought may briefly reduce its frequency, but soon it appears more often than ever."

**The Chinese finger trap metaphor:**
These toy traps: the harder you pull your fingers, the tighter they grip. To get free, you must relax and move toward the center, not away from it.

So it is with anxiety: fighting strengthens it. Acceptance sets you free.

## CHAPTER 4: THE SIX PROCESSES OF THE HEXAFLEX

ACT works through **six interconnected processes** that together create psychological flexibility. They are depicted as a hexagon — the "hexaflex."

### 1. Acceptance

**What it is:** Willingness to experience unpleasant thoughts, feelings, and sensations without trying to control, change, or get rid of them.

**It is NOT:**
- Passivity or resignation
- Approving of the situation
- Wanting to suffer

**It is:**
- Active openness to experience
- Making room for feelings
- Ending the war with inner experience

**Example:**
Instead of "I must not be anxious" — "Here is anxiety. I can make room for it and still act."

### 2. Cognitive Defusion

**What it is:** The ability to step back from your thoughts and see them as just thoughts — not facts or commands.

"Fusion" = merging. "Defusion" = separation.

**Fusion:** "I'm a failure" → this is the truth about me
**Defusion:** "I'm having the thought that I'm a failure" → this is just a thought

**Defusion techniques:**
- "I'm having the thought that..."
- Repeat the thought in a silly voice
- Sing the thought to the tune of "Happy Birthday"
- Thank your mind: "Thank you, mind, for that thought"

> "Thoughts are like lenses through which we look at the world."

### 3. Contact with the Present Moment

**What it is:** Conscious attention to what is happening here and now — inside and outside.

This is the foundation of mindfulness.

**Practice:**
- Notice what you see, hear, and feel right now
- Bring attention back when the mind drifts into the past or future
- Be fully present, without judgment

### 4. The Observing Self (Self-as-Context)

**What it is:** The ability to distinguish "self as content" (my thoughts, roles, stories) from "self as context" (the observer who is aware of all of it).

**"Self as content":** "I'm an anxious person," "I'm a failure," "I'm a victim"
**"Self as context":** "I am the one noticing these thoughts and feelings"

> "The stories my analytical mind tells are not me; they are products of the thinking processes within me."

This "self" is a safe place from which any experience can be observed. It is not damaged by pain.

### 5. Values

**What they are:** Chosen qualities of being that give life meaning and direction.

Values are **not goals**:
- A goal can be achieved and "checked off"
- A value is a direction you follow your whole life

**Examples:**
- Goal: get a promotion
- Value: professional growth, contribution, mastery

- Goal: get married
- Value: love, intimacy, partnership

**Values are a compass**, not a destination.

> "Pain and purpose are two sides of the same thing. A person suffering from depression is very likely a person yearning to feel fully. A socially anxious person is a person yearning for connection with others. You hurt where you care."

### 6. Committed Action

**What it is:** Concrete steps in the direction of values — even in the presence of unpleasant thoughts and feelings.

This is the **behavioral** part of ACT. It draws on:
- Goal setting
- Exposure (meeting what is avoided)
- Skill development
- Action planning

**The key:** Act **despite** the discomfort, rather than **waiting** for the discomfort to pass.

## CHAPTER 5: THE TWO WINGS OF THE HEXAFLEX

The six processes combine into **two groups**:

### Wing 1: Openness (Mindfulness & Acceptance)

- Acceptance
- Defusion
- Contact with the present
- The observing self

**Answers the question:** How do I relate to inner experience?

### Wing 2: Engagement (Commitment & Behavior Change)

- Values
- Committed action
- (Contact with the present and the observing self belong here too)

**Answers the question:** Where do I direct my life?

## CHAPTER 6: KEY TECHNIQUES

### 1. "I'm having the thought that..."

**Goal:** Cognitive defusion — separating yourself from the thought.

**How:**
When a painful thought arrives, preface it with: "I'm having the thought that..."

**Example:**
- "I'll never cope" → "I'm having the thought that I'll never cope"

This simple shift creates distance between you and the thought.

### 2. "Thank you, mind"

**Goal:** Gentle defusion; acknowledging the mind's "helpful intentions."

**How:**
When the mind generates catastrophic scenarios or criticism:
- "Thank you, mind, for trying to protect me"
- "I see you're trying to help, but I don't need that right now"

### 3. Singing the thought

**Goal:** Break the literalness of the thought through absurdity.

**How:**
Take a painful thought and sing it to the tune of "Happy Birthday" or another familiar melody.

"I'm-a-fail-ure, I'm-a-fail-ure..." — to the tune of the birthday song.

The thought remains, but it loses its power.

### 4. The Passengers on the Bus metaphor

**Goal:** Show that we can drive toward our values even when the "passengers" (thoughts, fears) are making noise.

**The metaphor:**
You are the driver of a bus. The passengers are your thoughts, feelings, and memories. Some passengers are loud and threatening: "Turn around!", "Stop!", "You're going the wrong way!"

You can:
1. Stop the bus and argue with the passengers (get stuck)
2. Drive where they tell you (live by their rules)
3. **Let them shout and drive where you decided to go**

They cannot physically grab the wheel. They can only yell.

### 5. The "Leaves on a Stream" exercise

**Goal:** Practice defusion and mindfulness.

**How:**
1. Close your eyes and imagine a stream with leaves floating on its surface
2. Place each thought that comes on a leaf and watch it drift away
3. Don't try to stop the stream or change the thoughts — just observe

### 6. Choosing from the menu

**Goal:** Clarifying values.

**How:**
Imagine you are in a restaurant and you are handed a menu of life values. You cannot take everything — you must choose.

Areas to explore:
- Family
- Romantic relationships
- Friendship
- Career/work
- Learning/growth
- Health
- Spirituality
- Community/contribution
- Leisure/pleasure

## CHAPTER 7: PRACTICAL DIALOGUE EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Working with anxiety (acceptance)

**Client:** I can't cope with this anxiety. It's killing me.

**Therapist:** Tell me, what do you do when the anxiety comes?

**Client:** I try to distract myself. Sometimes it works for a minute, then it all comes back. I'm so tired of fighting.

**Therapist:** What if the fighting is part of the problem?

**Client:** What do you mean?

**Therapist:** Have you ever seen Chinese finger traps — those woven tubes? The harder you pull your fingers, the tighter they grip. To get free, you have to move toward the center, not away from it.

**Client:** Are you saying I should *want* the anxiety?

**Therapist:** Not want it. But perhaps give it room. Imagine: instead of "Go away, anxiety!" you say, "Hello, anxiety. I see you. You can be here while I do what matters."

### Example 2: Cognitive defusion

**Client:** I constantly think I'm a failure. And it's true — look at my life.

**Therapist:** I hear that this thought comes often. Let's try something. Say: "I'm a failure."

**Client:** I'm a failure.

**Therapist:** Now say: "I'm having the thought that I'm a failure."

**Client:** I'm having the thought that I'm a failure.

**Therapist:** Did you notice the difference?

**Client:** Yes... in the second version it's as if it's not so... heavy.

**Therapist:** The thought stayed the same. But your relationship to it changed. It became just a thought — not a verdict.

### Example 3: Values and action

**Client:** I don't know why I should do anything at all. The anxiety will come back anyway.

**Therapist:** Let's set the anxiety aside for a second. If the anxiety magically disappeared tonight — what would you do tomorrow?

**Client:** I would... probably call my brother. We haven't talked in a long time.

**Therapist:** What matters to you about that relationship?

**Client:** He's my brother. Family matters. I just... I'm afraid he's disappointed in me.

**Therapist:** So family is a value for you. And anxiety says: "Don't call, he's disappointed." The question is not whether the anxiety will go away. The question is: are you willing to take the anxiety with you and make the call, because family matters?

**Client:** That's scary.

**Therapist:** Of course. Values always stand next to vulnerability. We hurt where we care.

## CHAPTER 8: WHEN TO USE ACT

**Especially effective for:**
- Anxiety disorders (including panic disorder)
- Depression
- Chronic pain
- Obsessive-compulsive disorder
- Post-traumatic stress
- Eating disorders
- Addictions
- Psychosis (as a complementary approach)

**Works well for:**
- Life crises and transitions
- Work problems (burnout, stress)
- Chronic illness (diabetes, cancer)
- Low self-esteem
- Procrastination
- Perfectionism

**Special features:**
- Works across a wide range of problems through a single mechanism (psychological flexibility)
- Suitable for short-term therapy
- Integrates well with other approaches
- Rich in self-help resources (books, apps)

## CHAPTER 10: COMPARISON WITH OTHER AUTHORS OF THE PROJECT

### ACT and CBT (Beck/Ellis)

| Aspect | CBT | ACT |
|--------|-----|-----|
| Attitude toward thoughts | Change the content | Change the relationship |
| Goal | Rational thoughts | Psychological flexibility |
| Negative feelings | Reduce them | Accept them |
| Similarity | Both structured and evidence-based |

ACT is sometimes called the "third wave" of CBT — a development, not a replacement.

### ACT and Frankl

| Aspect | Frankl | Hayes |
|--------|--------|-------|
| Focus | Meaning | Values |
| Suffering | Find meaning IN suffering | Act ON values despite suffering |
| Similarity | Both: suffering is inevitable, but how we relate to it is a choice |

### ACT and Perls

| Aspect | Perls | Hayes |
|--------|-------|-------|
| Focus | Here and now | Present moment + values |
| Method | Experiential | Experiential + scientific |
| Similarity | Both: awareness, acceptance, responsibility |

### ACT and Linehan (DBT)

| Aspect | DBT | ACT |
|--------|-----|-----|
| Focus | Emotion regulation + skills | Psychological flexibility |
| Dialectic | Acceptance AND change | Acceptance AND action |
| Similarity | Both use mindfulness; both are "third wave" |

### ACT and Rogers

| Aspect | Rogers | Hayes |
|--------|--------|-------|
| Style | Following the client | Actively teaching skills |
| Acceptance | Unconditional (from the therapist) | Self-acceptance (the client learns) |
| Similarity | Both: acceptance as the path to change |

## CHAPTER 11: INTEGRATION INTO THE PROJECT

### Hayes's unique role

Hayes is the only author in the project focused on the **relationship with inner experience** and on **values-directed action**.

**He contributes:**
- An understanding of why fighting feelings intensifies suffering
- Techniques for changing one's relationship to thoughts (defusion)
- Methods for clarifying values
- A structure for acting despite discomfort

### The architecture of use

1. **Rogers:** Creates safety
2. **Bowlby:** Understands the roots in attachment
3. **Beck/Ellis:** Works with the content of thoughts
4. **Frankl/Yalom:** Provides meaning and direction
5. **Linehan:** Teaches regulation skills
6. **Erickson:** Bypasses resistance
7. **Perls:** Returns to the present
8. **Hayes:** Changes the **relationship** to thoughts and feelings, directs toward values

### When to bring in Hayes

- When the client is stuck in fighting their own feelings
- When avoidance is making the problem worse
- When the client is "fused" with negative thoughts about themselves
- When there is a need to clarify what truly matters
- When there is a need to move despite fear

## CHAPTER 14: HAYES — THE SCIENTIST WHO HEALED HIMSELF

Steven Hayes is a rare bridge between personal suffering and rigorous science.

He did not merely survive panic — he understood its mechanism. He did not merely recover — he created a method. He did not merely create a method — he proved it through thousands of studies.

And he is still here — writing, teaching, developing the approach.

> "Life is not a problem to be solved."

ACT does not promise to free you from pain. It promises something more valuable: **a life filled with meaning, together with the pain when pain is present**.

That is an honest promise. And it can be kept.
