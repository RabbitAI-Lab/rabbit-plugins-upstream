# Marsha Linehan: Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)

## CHAPTER 1: WHO MARSHA LINEHAN IS

Marsha M. Linehan (born May 5, 1943) is an American psychologist, professor emeritus of psychology at the University of Washington in Seattle, and the creator of Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT). One of the most influential figures in modern clinical psychology.

**Achievements:**
- Created DBT — the only therapy with proven effectiveness for chronically suicidal patients
- DBT is recognized as the "gold standard" treatment for borderline personality disorder (BPD)
- Published 240+ scientific papers and numerous books
- In 2018, included in Time Magazine's special issue "Great Scientists"
- Awards: the Louis Israel Dublin Award for contributions to suicidology, the Grawemeyer Award in Psychology (2017), and many others

**Education and career:**
- 1971: PhD in clinical psychology, Loyola University, Chicago
- 1972: Postdoctoral fellowship, Stony Brook University (behavioral therapy)
- Since 1977: Professor at the University of Washington in Seattle
- Founder and director of Behavioral Research and Therapy Clinics

**Spiritual path:**
Linehan is a certified Zen master. She studied meditation with the Catholic priest and Zen teacher Willigis Jäger, then Zen Buddhism under Roshi Jiyu-Kennett at Shasta Abbey. Her spiritual practice deeply influenced the creation of DBT, especially the mindfulness and acceptance components.

**Key publications:**
- "Cognitive-Behavioral Treatment of Borderline Personality Disorder" (1993)
- "Skills Training Manual for Treating Borderline Personality Disorder" (1993)
- "DBT Skills Training Manual" (2nd ed., 2015)
- "Building a Life Worth Living: A Memoir" (2020)

## CHAPTER 2: DID LINEHAN LIVE HER OWN TEACHING?

**The most powerful evidence in the history of psychotherapy.**

On June 17, 2011, at the age of 68, Marsha Linehan returned to the Institute of Living in Hartford, Connecticut — the psychiatric clinic where she had spent more than two years as a teenager. Before an audience of 300 friends, colleagues, and relatives, she told her story publicly for the first time.

**The hospitalization story:**

In 1961, at the age of 17, Marsha was hospitalized at the Institute of Living. She had been a popular, cheerful high school senior — and suddenly fell into an abyss.

From the discharge summary (May 31, 1963):
"During 26 months of hospitalization, Miss Linehan was, for a considerable part of this time, one of the most disturbed patients in the hospital."

**What happened:**
- Constant self-harm: cutting her arms, legs, stomach; burning her wrists with cigarettes
- Placement in the seclusion room for severely disturbed patients — because of the unceasing wish to end her life
- Banging her head against walls and the floor
- Diagnosis: schizophrenia (BPD did not yet exist as a diagnosis)
- Treatment: Thorazine, Librium, electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)
- Result of ECT: significant loss of autobiographical memory

**Nothing helped.**

After discharge she lived for a time at the YWCA, worked part-time, lived on pennies. She was forcibly hospitalized at Cook County Insane Asylum — an experience she describes as a "Kafkaesque nightmare."

**The turning point:**

One night, praying in a chapel, Linehan made a vow:
"I will get out of hell — and when I do, I will find a way to help others get out too."

This became the mission of her entire life.

**The epiphany:**

At some point — Linehan does not give the exact date — an inner shift occurred. She describes it as a spiritual experience filled with golden light:

"God loves me, and I love myself... God is in everyone and everything, and loves everyone and everything."

Later she formulated this as **accepting herself as she is**. Not trying to become someone else, not a war with herself — but acceptance.

But acceptance alone was not enough. She also needed to **change**. These two seeming contradictions — acceptance and change — became the core of the dialectic in DBT.

**The scars:**

Linehan does not hide the scars on her arms. Patients sometimes asked her: "Are you one of us?" For decades she did not answer that question publicly.

In 2011 she finally answered: "Yes."

**Quote from the memoir:**
"I think I can say: I have fulfilled the vow I made to God at the Institute of Living many years ago. But I didn't stop there... I want to find ways to bring DBT and DBT skills to every person in the world who needs them."

## CHAPTER 3: THE CORE IDEA — DIALECTICS

**What is dialectics?**

Dialectics is the philosophical idea that reality consists of opposing forces that can be true at the same time. The synthesis of these opposites leads to truth and growth.

**The central dialectic of DBT:**

**Acceptance + Change = A Life Worth Living**

- On one side: You are okay as you are. Your feelings make sense. The ways you coped were the best you were capable of.
- On the other side: You need to change. Your ways of coping cause harm. There are more effective paths.

**Both statements are true at the same time.**

This is not "either-or," but "both-and":
- "I'm doing the best I can" AND "I need to try harder"
- "I accept myself" AND "I want to change"
- "This is unbearable" AND "I can survive this"

**Why does this matter?**

Many therapies emphasize either acceptance (humanistic) or change (cognitive-behavioral). DBT is the first therapy to systematically integrate both poles.

For chronically suicidal patients this is critically important:
- Pure pressure to change = "You're not good enough" → deterioration
- Pure acceptance without change = stagnation → hopelessness

The dialectical approach: "I understand why you cut yourself — and let's find another way to cope with the pain."

## CHAPTER 4: THE BIOSOCIAL THEORY

DBT is based on the biosocial theory of emotional dysregulation — a model of how borderline personality disorder develops.

**The formula:**

**Emotional vulnerability + Invalidating environment = Emotional dysregulation**

### The biological component: Emotional vulnerability

Some people are born with a nervous system that is:
- **Highly sensitive:** reacts to lower thresholds of stimulation
- **Highly reactive:** reactions are more intense than others'
- **Slow to return to baseline:** emotions persist longer

This is not "weakness" and not "character" — it is biology, probably with a genetic component.

### The social component: The invalidating environment

An invalidating environment is one that systematically:
- Rejects or ignores the person's emotional experience
- Communicates that their feelings are wrong, exaggerated, or inappropriate
- Punishes emotional expression
- Demands that the person "cope" without teaching them how

**Examples of invalidation:**
- "Don't cry, it's nothing"
- "You're too sensitive"
- "Others have it worse, and they don't complain"
- "Pull yourself together"
- Ignoring a child's pain
- The extreme form: physical, sexual, emotional abuse

**Important:** An invalidating environment is not necessarily abusive. It can be a loving family that simply does not understand how to respond properly to an emotionally vulnerable child.

### The transactional cycle

Biology and environment mutually reinforce each other:

1. The emotionally vulnerable child reacts intensely
2. The environment, not understanding, invalidates the reaction
3. The child does not learn to regulate emotions
4. The child reacts even more intensely
5. The environment intensifies the invalidation
6. The vicious circle escalates

The result: the person does not develop emotion regulation skills and resorts to dysfunctional ways of coping (self-harm, impulsivity, unstable relationships).

### The "blameless" model

The biosocial theory is a model without blame:
- It does not blame the patient ("you're just manipulating")
- It does not blame the parents ("it's all because of your mother")
- It explains how biology and environment together created the problem

This is liberating: "You didn't create these problems. But you will have to solve them."

## CHAPTER 5: THE FOUR DBT SKILLS MODULES

DBT teaches concrete skills in four modules. Two are aimed at **acceptance**, two at **change**.

### MODULE 1: MINDFULNESS — Acceptance

**The foundational module.** Mindfulness is the basis of all other skills.

**The essence:** The ability to be present in the current moment, observing one's experience without judgment.

**Three states of mind:**

| State | Description | Problem |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| Emotion mind | Ruled by emotions, impulses | Reactivity, impulsive decisions |
| Reasonable mind | Ruled by logic, facts | Disconnection from emotions, "coldness" |
| **Wise mind** | Integration of emotion and logic | **The goal of DBT** |

**The "WHAT" skills:**
- **Observe:** Notice the experience without being immersed in it
- **Describe:** Put the experience into words ("I notice sadness")
- **Participate:** Fully engage in the current moment

**The "HOW" skills:**
- **Non-judgmentally:** Without categories of "good/bad"
- **One-mindfully:** One thing at a time, with full attention
- **Effectively:** Do what works, not what is "fair"

### MODULE 2: DISTRESS TOLERANCE — Acceptance

**The essence:** Surviving a crisis without making the situation worse.

**When to use:** In an acute moment, when emotions overflow and there is no way to solve the problem right now.

**Crisis survival skills:**

**TIPP — Rapidly changing physiology:**
- **T**emperature: Cold water on the face activates the "dive reflex," slowing the heartbeat
- **I**ntense exercise: A discharge of energy
- **P**aced breathing: A slow exhale longer than the inhale
- **P**aired muscle relaxation: Progressive muscle relaxation

**ACCEPTS — Distraction:**
- **A**ctivities
- **C**ontributing (helping others)
- **C**omparisons (with harder situations)
- **E**motions (opposite emotions)
- **P**ushing away (temporarily setting aside)
- **T**houghts (distracting thoughts)
- **S**ensations (physical sensations)

**Self-soothing through the 5 senses:** Pleasant smells, sounds, tastes, touches, visual images.

**IMPROVE — Improving the moment:**
- **I**magery
- **M**eaning
- **P**rayer (prayer/meditation)
- **R**elaxation
- **O**ne thing (one thing at a time)
- **V**acation (a brief break)
- **E**ncouragement (cheering yourself on)

**Radical acceptance:**
Complete and total acceptance of reality as it is — with mind, body, and spirit.

**Important:** Acceptance ≠ approval. Acceptance means stopping fighting the facts of reality.

"It is what it is" — does not mean "I like it" or "This is fair."

Refusing to accept reality does not change reality. It turns pain into suffering.

### MODULE 3: EMOTION REGULATION — Change

**The essence:** Understanding emotions and managing them to reduce suffering.

**Key skills:**

**Understanding emotions:**
- Identifying and naming emotions
- Understanding the function of emotions
- Recognizing what increases vulnerability

**Check the Facts:**
Before reacting, check:
- What event triggered the emotion?
- What are my interpretations?
- Do they match the facts?
- What is most likely? (not the worst-case scenario)

**Opposite Action:**
Acting opposite to what the emotion urges, when the emotion does not fit the facts or does not help.

| Emotion | Urge | Opposite action |
|---------|------|-----------------|
| Fear | Avoid | Approach |
| Anger | Attack | Gently step back, do something kind |
| Sadness | Withdraw | Become active, reach out to people |
| Shame | Hide | Tell someone |

**PLEASE — Reducing vulnerability:**
- **PL**: Treating physical illnesses
- **E**: Balanced eating
- **A**: Avoiding mood-altering substances
- **S**: Balanced sleep
- **E**: Exercise

**Accumulating positive emotions:**
- Short term: Do pleasant things every day
- Long term: Live in accordance with your values

### MODULE 4: INTERPERSONAL EFFECTIVENESS — Change

**The essence:** Skills for healthy relationships — asking, refusing, keeping self-respect.

**DEAR MAN — Getting what you want:**

- **D**escribe (describe the situation with facts)
- **E**xpress (express feelings and opinions)
- **A**ssert (state the request or refusal clearly)
- **R**einforce (reinforce — explain the benefit for the other)
- **M**indful (stay mindful — don't get distracted from the goal)
- **A**ppear confident
- **N**egotiate (be ready to compromise)

**GIVE — Keeping the relationship:**

- **G**entle (no attacks or threats)
- **I**nterested (listen)
- **V**alidate (acknowledge the other's feelings)
- **E**asy manner (humor, softness)

**FAST — Keeping self-respect:**

- **F**air (to yourself and others)
- **A**pologies (no excessive apologizing)
- **S**tick to values
- **T**ruthful

## CHAPTER 6: THE STRUCTURE OF A DBT PROGRAM

A full DBT program includes four components:

### 1. Individual therapy (once a week, 50–90 min)

Focus:
- Working through the hierarchy of targets (see below)
- Applying skills to concrete situations
- Behavioral analysis of problem behavior
- Motivation for change

### 2. Group skills training (once a week, ~2.5 hours)

Focus:
- Teaching the four skills modules
- Practicing in the group
- Homework
- Sharing experience

**Format:** Not group therapy in the traditional sense. It is skills training, like a class.

### 3. Telephone coaching (as needed)

Focus:
- Help applying skills in real time
- Crisis situations
- The call comes before the problem behavior, not after

**Rule:** If the patient has self-harmed, the therapist does not discuss it by phone for 24 hours (so as not to reinforce the behavior).

### 4. Consultation team (for therapists)

Focus:
- Supporting therapists
- Preventing burnout
- Supervision
- Maintaining fidelity to DBT

**Philosophy:** DBT is therapy for therapists as much as for patients. Working with suicidal patients is exhausting. The team is a necessity, not a luxury.

## CHAPTER 7: THE HIERARCHY OF TARGETS IN DBT

DBT has a clear hierarchy of priorities:

### Stage 1 (primary):

**Priority 1:** Life-threatening behavior
- Suicidal thoughts and actions
- Self-harm
- Suicidal communications

**Priority 2:** Therapy-interfering behavior
- Missing sessions
- Breaking agreements
- Behavior that causes therapist burnout

**Priority 3:** Quality-of-life-interfering behavior
- Substance abuse
- Eating disorders
- Financial problems
- Housing instability

**Priority 4:** Developing behavioral skills

### Stage 2:

Working with trauma and PTSD (only after stabilization in Stage 1)

### Stage 3:

Ordinary life problems, self-respect

### Stage 4:

Spiritual development, a sense of wholeness

## CHAPTER 8: VALIDATION — THE HEART OF DBT

Validation is one of the therapist's key skills in DBT.

**What is validation?**
Communicating that a person's experience, feelings, thoughts, or actions are understandable and make sense (even if they are not approved of).

**What validation is NOT:**
- It is not agreement
- It is not approval
- It is not encouraging problem behavior

**The six levels of validation (according to Linehan):**

| Level | Description | Example |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| 1 | Presence and listening | Full attention, eye contact |
| 2 | Accurate reflection | "I hear that you feel anger" |
| 3 | Articulating the unspoken | "Maybe there's hurt behind the anger?" |
| 4 | Validation through history | "Given your past, it's understandable why you react this way" |
| 5 | Validation through current circumstances | "In this situation anyone would feel anxious" |
| 6 | Radical genuineness | Treating the person as an equal, as capable |

**Why is validation so important in DBT?**

Patients with BPD grew up in invalidating environments. Therapy must become a **corrective emotional experience** — an environment where their experience is finally validated.

The paradox: Only when a person feels accepted are they capable of changing.

## CHAPTER 9: REAL CASES AND STUDIES

**Study 1: Linehan's first RCT (1991)**

44 women with BPD and parasuicidal behavior. Randomly assigned to DBT or treatment as usual (TAU) for 1 year.

Results of DBT vs TAU:
- Fewer episodes of self-harm
- Fewer hospitalizations
- Fewer inpatient days
- Better retention in therapy

This was the first time *any* therapy had shown effectiveness for this population.

**Study 2: Linehan et al. (2006)**

101 women with BPD and high suicide risk. DBT vs "Community Treatment by Experts" (CTBE).

Results of DBT:
- Half as many suicide attempts
- Fewer hospitalizations for suicidality
- Fewer emergency room visits
- Higher likelihood of staying in therapy

**Meta-analysis (DeCou et al., 2019):**

18 controlled studies of DBT.

Results:
- Significant reduction in self-harm and suicide attempts (d = -0.32)
- Significant reduction in psychiatric crisis service use (d = -0.38)
- The effect persists up to 24 months after the end of therapy

**Study with adolescents (Mehlum et al., 2014):**

DBT-A (the adaptation for adolescents) showed a significant reduction in self-harm and suicidal ideation in adolescents with BPD symptoms.

**Expanding applications:**

DBT has also shown effectiveness for:
- Eating disorders (bulimia, binge eating)
- Substance abuse
- Depression
- PTSD
- Bipolar disorder
- ADHD with emotional dysregulation

## CHAPTER 10: PRACTICAL DIALOGUE EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Validation + prompting change**

Patient: I cut myself yesterday. I know I shouldn't have, but I just couldn't help it.

Therapist: Tell me what happened. (Level 1: presence)

P: I had a fight with my mom. She said I'd never change, that I'm "the same as always." I felt such rage and pain that I couldn't breathe.

T: The pain was unbearable. (Level 2: reflection) Given how much criticism you've received from your mom all your life, it's understandable that her words hit so hard. (Level 4: through history)

P: Yes... I didn't know what to do with those feelings.

T: And you did the only thing you knew to cope. The cutting worked — it reduced the intensity of the pain. (Level 5: validating the function)

P: (crying) Yes, it works. At least something works.

T: And at the same time — it creates new problems. Scars, shame, your health. Do you want to find other ways that work but don't cost so much?

P: Yes... but I don't know how.

T: That's exactly why we're here. Let's look at the chain of events and find where a skill could have been used.

---

**Example 2: Working with radical acceptance**

Patient: It's unfair. Why should I suffer? Why did I get these parents? Why is it easier for others?

Therapist: You're fighting the reality of what is.

P: Because it's wrong!

T: I understand. And what happens inside when you fight what has already happened?

P: I get angrier and angrier.

T: Does fighting reality change reality?

P: (pause) No.

T: What does it do?

P: Makes it worse. Adds suffering to the pain.

T: Radical acceptance is not agreeing with what happened. It's not "I like it" or "It's fair." It's — "It is what it is."

P: But how can I accept something that shouldn't have happened?

T: Imagine you're standing in the rain, screaming at the sky to make it stop. Does the rain stop?

P: No.

T: You can keep screaming. Or you can open an umbrella. Acceptance is the umbrella. You can't change the rain. But you can change what you do in the rain.

---

**Example 3: DEAR MAN**

A patient wants to ask her husband to help with cleaning but fears conflict.

Therapist: Let's practice DEAR MAN. Start with "Describe."

Patient: "Over the past two weeks I've cleaned every day after work. You come home later and relax."

T: Good — facts without accusations. Now "Express."

P: "I feel exhausted and hurt. It feels like I'm carrying the whole load alone."

T: Excellent — an "I" statement. Now "Assert."

P: "I'd like us to split the cleaning. For example, you could do the dishes on even days."

T: Clear and specific. Now "Reinforce."

P: "If we split it, I'll have more energy, and we can spend evenings together instead of me collapsing into bed."

T: Wonderful — you've shown him the benefit. Now remember — "Mindful": if he starts steering the conversation away, return to the request. "Appear confident" — steady voice, eye contact. "Negotiate" — if he offers an alternative, consider it.

## CHAPTER 11: WHEN TO USE DBT

**High effectiveness (evidence base):**
- Borderline personality disorder (BPD)
- Chronic suicidality
- Self-harm (NSSI)
- Emotional dysregulation

**Indicated (growing evidence base):**
- Eating disorders (bulimia, binge eating)
- Substance abuse
- Depression (resistant to standard treatment)
- PTSD (in combination with Prolonged Exposure)
- Bipolar disorder (as an adjunct)

**Potentially useful:**
- Anxiety disorders
- ADHD with emotional dysregulation
- Anger and aggression
- Interpersonal difficulties

**When DBT is especially indicated:**
- Multiple diagnoses
- Ineffectiveness of other therapies
- High suicide risk
- Difficulties staying in therapy

**When caution is required:**
- Acute psychosis (stabilization first)
- Severe intellectual disability (adaptation needed)
- Refusal of the structured format

Linehan complements the authors already collected beautifully:

**Rogers + Linehan:**

- Linehan: Validation + Skills training
- Integration: Acceptance (Rogers) as the foundation for change (Linehan)

**Beck/Ellis + Linehan:**
- Beck/Ellis: Changing cognitions and beliefs
- Linehan: Cognitive techniques + Mindfulness + Acceptance
- Integration: Emotionally dysregulated clients need stabilization first (Linehan), then cognitive work (Beck/Ellis)

**Yalom + Linehan:**
- Yalom: Existential themes, depth
- Linehan: Concrete skills, structure
- Integration: DBT for stabilization → Existential work for depth

**Example of integration for a suicidal client:**

1. **Linehan:** Stabilization, safety, crisis survival skills
2. **Rogers:** Empathic presence, validation
3. **Beck/Ellis:** Working with cognitive distortions, catastrophizing
4. **Frankl/Yalom:** Existential work on meaning, once the client is stable

## CHAPTER 16: SUMMARY — THE KEY PRINCIPLES OF DBT

1. **Dialectics:** Acceptance AND Change. Both poles are necessary.

2. **Biosocial theory:** Emotional dysregulation = Biology + Invalidating environment

3. **Four skills modules:**
   - Mindfulness (the foundation)
   - Distress tolerance (surviving a crisis)
   - Emotion regulation (managing feelings)
   - Interpersonal effectiveness (healthy relationships)

5. **Validation:** A therapeutic tool for creating a corrective emotional experience

6. **The ultimate goal:** Building a life worth living
