# Albert Ellis: Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT)

## CHAPTER 1: WHO ALBERT ELLIS WAS

Albert Ellis (September 27, 1913 — July 24, 2007) was an American psychologist, the founder of Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT), which is considered the first form of cognitive behavioral therapy.

In a 1982 survey of American and Canadian psychologists, Ellis was ranked the second most influential psychotherapist in history (after Carl Rogers, ahead of Sigmund Freud). Psychology Today noted: "No individual — not even Freud himself — has had a greater impact on modern psychotherapy."

**Childhood and youth:**
Ellis was born in Pittsburgh to a Jewish family and grew up in the Bronx, New York. The eldest of three children. His father was a traveling salesman, often absent. His mother suffered from bipolar disorder and paid little attention to the children. Ellis effectively raised his younger brother and sister himself.

In childhood he suffered from kidney problems and spent a great deal of time in hospitals. He turned to books and philosophy as a way to cope with difficulties.

**Overcoming his own fears:**
In his youth Ellis suffered from severe shyness — especially with women — and a fear of public speaking.

At 19 he conducted an experiment on himself that became the prototype of his therapy: every day for a month he went to the Bronx Botanical Garden and forced himself to start a conversation with every woman sitting alone on a bench. He found 130 women. Thirty of them got up and walked away as he approached. With the remaining 100 he talked — about birds, flowers, books, anything at all.

The result: not a single date (one agreed but didn't show up). But — the complete disappearance of the fear of rejection.

Ellis told himself before each approach: "If I die — I die. To hell with it, let me die." He did not die. And he understood: rejection is unpleasant, but not unbearable.

This experience became the foundation of the exposure technique and of the entire REBT philosophy.

**Education and career:**
- 1934: Bachelor's degree in business, City College of New York
- Worked in business during the Great Depression, without success
- Tried to become a writer; could not publish fiction
- Began writing about sexuality; friends started coming to him for advice
- 1942: Enrolled in the clinical psychology program at Columbia University
- 1943: Master's degree in psychology
- 1947: Doctorate (PhD)

**From psychoanalysis to REBT:**
Like most psychologists of his time, Ellis began as a psychoanalyst. He underwent personal analysis and practiced classical psychoanalysis.

But he grew disillusioned: patients gained insights into their childhood but "did almost nothing to change."

By 1953 he had broken with psychoanalysis completely. In 1955 he presented "Rational Therapy" (RT). In 1961 he renamed it "Rational Emotive Therapy" (RET) to emphasize the work with emotions. In 1993 he added "Behavior" — REBT.

**Philosophical roots:**
Ellis drew inspiration from Stoic philosophy, especially from Epictetus: "People are disturbed not by things, but by their views of things."

Other influences: Alfred Adler (beliefs determine behavior), Karen Horney ("the tyranny of the shoulds").

**Final years:**
Ellis worked until the last days of his life. He wrote more than 80 books and 600+ articles. He held his famous "Friday Night Workshops" at the Albert Ellis Institute in New York well into old age.

He died on July 24, 2007, at the age of 93.

## CHAPTER 2: THE CORE IDEA OF REBT

**The central principle:**
It is not events that cause our emotions and behavior — but our beliefs about those events.

This is an ancient Stoic idea that Ellis turned into a practical system of therapy.

**Ellis' formula:**
"People disturb themselves — with their rigid and extreme beliefs about things."

**Comparison with Beck:**
Both Beck and Ellis work with thoughts. But there are differences:

| Aspect | Beck (CT) | Ellis (REBT) |
|--------|-----------|--------------|
| Focus | Automatic thoughts, cognitive distortions | Irrational beliefs, "must/should" |
| Style | Socratic questions, gentle exploration | Direct confrontation, disputing |
| Origin | Empirical observations of depression | Philosophy (Stoicism) |
| Tone | Empathic, collaborative | Directive, sometimes provocative |

Beck himself acknowledged: "Ellis was first. He helped me get started."

## CHAPTER 3: THE ABC (ABCDE) MODEL

This is the core of REBT — a simple and powerful model for understanding emotional reactions.

**A — Activating Event**
What happened. The situation, the trigger.

**B — Beliefs**
What the person thinks/believes about the event. They can be rational or irrational.

**C — Consequences**
Emotional and behavioral reactions. The result of the beliefs (B), not of the event (A).

**The key insight:**
A does not cause C directly. B is the intermediary.

**Example:**
- A: A friend didn't reply to a message
- B (irrational): "He SHOULD have replied! He's ignoring me! This is awful! I can't stand it!"
- C: Intense hurt, anger, avoiding the friend

- B (rational): "I would have preferred him to reply, but he doesn't have to. He's probably busy. It's unpleasant, but not a catastrophe."
- C: Mild disappointment, calm waiting

**Extending the model — D and E:**

**D — Disputing**
Actively disputing irrational beliefs. Questions:
- "Where is the evidence that he MUST reply?"
- "Why is this AWFUL, and not simply unpleasant?"
- "Is it really true that I CAN'T STAND it?"

**E — Effective new belief / Effect**
The result of disputing — a more rational belief and a healthier emotional reaction.

## CHAPTER 4: FOUR TYPES OF IRRATIONAL BELIEFS

Ellis originally described 11 irrational ideas, but later researchers identified four main categories:

**1. Demandingness**
The core of all irrational beliefs. Absolutist demands on oneself, others, the world.

Markers: must, should, have to, ought to, need to

Examples:
- "I must be successful"
- "People must treat me fairly"
- "Life must be easy"

Ellis called this "musturbation" — from the word must.

**2. Awfulizing (Catastrophizing)**
Exaggerating the negativity of an event to the maximum.

Markers: awful, terrible, catastrophe, the end of the world

Examples:
- "If I fail — it will be awful!"
- "Her rejection is a catastrophe!"

Ellis proposed a scale from 0 to 100: "How bad is this really? 100 is the death of all your loved ones. And failing an exam — where does that land?"

**3. Low Frustration Tolerance**
The belief that discomfort cannot be borne.

Markers: I can't stand it, I won't survive it, unbearable, I can't take it

Examples:
- "I can't stand this uncertainty!"
- "I couldn't bear it if he rejected me!"

Reality: people endure incredible hardships. "I can't stand it" is almost always untrue.

**4. Global Evaluation / Self-Downing**
Evaluating an entire person (oneself or another) on the basis of individual actions.

Markers: I'm a failure, I'm worthless, he's a bastard, life is garbage

Examples:
- "I failed the project — I'm a total failure"
- "He lied — he's a terrible person"

Ellis: "You can evaluate actions, but not the whole person. A person is too complex a system for a global rating."

**The hierarchy of beliefs:**
Demandingness (MUST) → generates → Awfulizing + Low frustration tolerance + Global evaluation

## CHAPTER 5: RATIONAL ALTERNATIVES

For each type of irrational belief there is a rational alternative:

| Irrational | Rational |
|------------|----------|
| Demandingness: "Must" | Preference: "I'd like to, but I don't have to" |
| Awfulizing: "Terrible" | Evaluation: "Bad/unpleasant, but not the end of the world" |
| Low tolerance: "I can't stand it" | Tolerance: "Hard, but I can cope" |
| Global evaluation: "I'm a failure" | Unconditional acceptance: "I made a mistake, but I am not my mistakes" |

**Examples of transformation:**

Irrational: "I must get this job. If I don't — it's a catastrophe, I won't survive it, I'm a complete failure."

Rational: "I very much want this job and will do everything I can. If I don't get it — it will be very disappointing, but not a catastrophe. I will survive it. Not getting one job doesn't make me a failure — it's just one setback."

## CHAPTER 6: THREE TYPES OF UNCONDITIONAL ACCEPTANCE

Ellis particularly emphasized three forms of acceptance as the foundation of psychological health:

**1. Unconditional Self-Acceptance (USA)**
Accepting yourself as a worthwhile person simply because you are alive and unique — regardless of achievements, others' approval, or mistakes.

Not to be confused with self-esteem! Ellis criticized the concept of self-esteem: it makes a person's worth dependent on achievements.

"You cannot rate your whole self. You can rate your actions, skills, traits — but not your entire personhood."

**2. Unconditional Other-Acceptance (UOA)**
Accepting other people as complex, imperfect beings — even when their behavior is unpleasant or harmful.

You can condemn actions without putting global labels on people.

"He did a bad thing" ≠ "He is a bad person"

**3. Unconditional Life-Acceptance (ULA)**
Accepting life as it is — with its injustices, difficulties, and uncertainty.

Not demanding that life be easy, fair, or match our expectations.

"Life is often hard and unfair. That is reality, not a catastrophe."

## CHAPTER 7: THE DISPUTING TECHNIQUE

The central technique of REBT is the active, energetic disputing of irrational beliefs.

**Three types of disputing:**

**1. Cognitive (logical)**
Questions to test the logic of a belief:

- "Where's the evidence?" — "What facts confirm that you MUST be successful?"
- "Is it logical?" — "Does it follow from wanting success that you are obligated to achieve it?"
- "Is this belief useful?" — "Does the thought 'I'm a failure' help you become more successful?"
- "Is it realistic?" — "Is there a law of the universe that people must be fair?"

**2. Emotive (empirical)**
Techniques for working with emotions:

Rational Emotive Imagery:
1. Imagine the worst-case scenario
2. Allow yourself to feel the unhealthy emotion (depression, panic)
3. Work on changing the emotion to a healthy one (disappointment, concern)
4. Track which beliefs you changed

Shame-attacking exercises:
Deliberately doing "shameful" things (singing on the subway, wearing ridiculous clothing) and noticing that nothing terrible happens.

**3. Behavioral**
Actions that disprove irrational beliefs:

- Exposure to fears (like Ellis' experiment in the Botanical Garden)
- Postponing avoidance
- Homework involving risky behavior

**Ellis' disputing style:**
Ellis was known for a directive, sometimes confrontational style. He could tell a client: "That's horseshit!" — but he always disputed beliefs, never the person.

Modern REBT therapists often use a softer tone while retaining the activity and directiveness.

## CHAPTER 8: HEALTHY VS UNHEALTHY NEGATIVE EMOTIONS

REBT distinguishes healthy from unhealthy negative emotions. Negative emotions are normal and adaptive. The problem lies in their unhealthy forms.

| Unhealthy emotion | Healthy alternative |
|-------------------|---------------------|
| Anxiety | Concern |
| Depression | Sadness, sorrow |
| Unhealthy anger, rage | Healthy anger, annoyance |
| Guilt (self-condemnation) | Regret, remorse |
| Shame (I'm bad) | Disappointment in oneself |
| Hurt | Disappointment in others |
| Envy (destructive) | Healthy envy (motivating) |
| Jealousy (destructive) | Healthy jealousy (caring for the relationship) |

**The difference is not in intensity, but in quality.**

Example:
- Unhealthy anger: "He SHOULD NOT have done that! It's AWFUL! He's a BASTARD!" → Rage, aggression, destruction of the relationship.
- Healthy anger: "I really don't like what he did. It was wrong of him." → Annoyance, assertive expression of displeasure, constructive resolution.

## CHAPTER 9: ELLIS' 12 IRRATIONAL IDEAS

Ellis' classic list — 12 common irrational beliefs:

**1. The need for approval**
Irrational: "I must be loved and approved of by everyone significant to me."
Rational: "It's pleasant to be loved, but it's not a necessity. It's impossible to please everyone."

**2. The demand for perfection**
Irrational: "I must be competent and successful at everything to be considered worthy."
Rational: "It's good to be successful, but no one can be good at everything. My worth does not depend on achievements."

**3. Condemning others**
Irrational: "Bad people must be punished."
Rational: "People make mistakes out of ignorance, lack of skill, or disturbance. Condemnation doesn't help."

**4. Catastrophizing**
Irrational: "It's awful when things don't go the way I want."
Rational: "Unpleasant, but not the end of the world. One can try to change the situation or accept it."

**5. External locus of control**
Irrational: "My unhappiness is caused by external events; I cannot control my emotions."
Rational: "My emotions are the result of my thoughts. I can change my reactions."

**6. Worrying about the future**
Irrational: "If something is dangerous — one must constantly worry about it."
Rational: "It's better to calmly assess the risks and take measures than to torment oneself with anxiety."

**7. Avoiding difficulties**
Irrational: "It's easier to avoid problems than to solve them."
Rational: "Avoidance makes problems worse. It's better to face difficulties head-on."

**8. Dependence on the strong**
Irrational: "I need someone stronger than me to rely on."
Rational: "It's better to develop one's own strength and independence, taking risks and learning."

**9. The influence of the past**
Irrational: "The past determines the present. What affected me before will affect me forever."
Rational: "The past influences, but does not determine. I can change now."

**10. Getting upset over others' problems**
Irrational: "I must get upset over other people's problems."
Rational: "I can sympathize without destroying myself. My being upset won't help them."

**11. The existence of a perfect solution**
Irrational: "For every problem there is a perfect solution. It's awful if it isn't found."
Rational: "Perfect solutions usually don't exist. Better to look for good ones, not perfect ones."

**12. The impossibility of controlling emotions**
Irrational: "I cannot control my feelings."
Rational: "Emotions are hard to control directly, but I can change the thoughts that cause them."

## CHAPTER 10: ELLIS' REAL CASES

**Case 1: Ellis and his own shyness (1932)**

This is an autobiographical case — Ellis was the first patient of his future therapy.

The problem: At 19 — a paralyzing fear of rejection by women. He could not start a conversation with an unfamiliar girl.

The intervention (self-administered):
1. He identified the irrational belief: "I MUST be accepted. Rejection is AWFUL."
2. He gave himself an assignment: for a month, approach every woman sitting alone in the Botanical Garden.
3. He told himself before each approach: "If I die — I die. To hell with it."

The result:
- 130 women found
- 30 walked away as he approached
- 100 conversations took place
- 1 agreed to a date (didn't show up)
- The fear of rejection — disappeared completely

Ellis' conclusion: "Rejection is unpleasant, but not unbearable. I proved this to myself experimentally."

This experience became the prototype of the exposure technique in REBT.

---

**Case 2: Roxanne — trauma from her husband's suicide (session video)**

There is a video recording of a real session of Ellis with a client named Roxanne.

The problem: Her husband had taken his own life in front of Roxanne and their little daughter 10 years earlier. Roxanne continued to be tormented by guilt and grief.

Irrational beliefs (identified by Ellis):
1. "Condemnation": "I did something terrible — therefore I'm a terrible person"
2. "Demandingness": "I should have prevented it"

Ellis' intervention:
He pointed directly to the logical error: "You did a bad thing" ≠ "You are a bad person."

He helped her reframe: "I did something I regret, but that doesn't define me as a person."

The result: In a single session — noticeable relief. Roxanne began to separate her actions from her identity.

---

**Case 3: Orville — addiction (session video)**

Another documented session of Ellis.

The problem: A man addicted to marijuana, alcohol, and hallucinogens. He wants to quit but "doesn't know how."

Irrational beliefs (identified by Ellis):
1. Low frustration tolerance: "I can't stand discomfort without substances"
2. Self-downing: "If I don't always behave well — I'm a bad person"

Ellis' style in the session:
- Directive, persistent
- Used strong language
- Kept bringing the client back to the topic (Orville kept digressing into side stories)
- Debunked the myth: "You're not a bad person — you're a person who sometimes does foolish things. Like all of us."

The result: In a single session — an understanding of the connection between beliefs and addictive behavior.

**Case 4: Effectiveness study of REBT at the Ellis Institute (2007–2016)**

A large-scale study in real clinical practice.

Participants: 349 patients of the Albert Ellis Institute
Method: REBT in a standard format (up to 20 sessions)
Measurement: Outcome Questionnaire-45 (patient functioning)

Results:
- Significant improvement in functioning after just 3 sessions (early response)
- Medium effect size after 20 sessions
- The effectiveness of REBT confirmed in real clinical practice (not only in controlled studies)

## CHAPTER 11: WHEN TO USE REBT

**High effectiveness:**
- Depression
- Anxiety disorders (generalized anxiety, social anxiety, phobias)
- Anger and aggression
- Procrastination
- Self-esteem problems
- Perfectionism
- Adjustment disorders

**Good effectiveness:**
- Addictions (alcohol, drugs, behavioral)
- Eating disorders
- Relationship problems
- Sexual dysfunctions (an area where Ellis worked extensively)
- Work stress, burnout

**Specific applications:**
- Sports psychology (working with athletes — a popular area)
- Coaching and performance development
- Education (Rational Emotive Education)
- Pastoral counseling

**When REBT may be less effective:**
- Acute psychotic states
- Severe cognitive impairments (dementia)
- When the client is not ready for a directive style
- When predominantly emotional support is needed (though modern REBT therapists know how to be warm)

**Features of REBT:**
- Directive, active approach
- Focus on philosophical change (not just symptoms)
- Mandatory homework
- Short-term (usually 5–20 sessions)
- Can be intense and "tough"

## CHAPTER 12: KEY QUOTES FROM ELLIS

**On the nature of disturbance:**
"People are disturbed not by things, but by their view of things" (Epictetus, whom Ellis quoted)

"People upset themselves. They contribute to their own disturbance."

"Much of what we call emotion is nothing more than a certain kind of thinking: biased, prejudiced, or strongly evaluative."

**On shoulds:**
"Must, should, have to — the three words that create the most suffering."

"There are three main musts: I must do well, you must treat me well, the world must be easy."

**On acceptance:**
"Accept yourself unconditionally — simply because you are alive, unique, and human."

"You cannot rate your whole self. You can rate your actions — but not all of you."

**On life:**
"Life is indeed hard — partly because of the real difficulties that must be overcome, partly because of our desire to always do better, to overcome new challenges. Happiness is experienced mainly in striving toward a goal, not in possession."

"The best years of my life are the ones I'm living now."

**On therapy:**
"People gained insights into what bothered them but did almost nothing to change" (on his disillusionment with psychoanalysis).

"I hope I'm not a fanatical REBTer, because I don't think it's a universal cure for everyone, and I acknowledge its obvious limitations."

## CHAPTER 13: PRACTICAL DIALOGUE EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Working with demandingness and catastrophizing**

Client: I'm in shock. My project at work failed. My boss will be furious. It's a catastrophe.

Therapist: Okay, let's look at this. The project failed — that's a fact. But how do you know it's a "catastrophe"?

C: Well... the boss will be unhappy, maybe I'll get fired...

T: Suppose the boss is unhappy. That's unpleasant, agreed. But what is a "catastrophe"? An earthquake, a war, the death of loved ones. Is a failed project a catastrophe of the same level?

C: No, but it's very bad for my career.

T: "Very bad" is an honest assessment. "Catastrophe" is an exaggeration. Notice the difference in how it feels when you say "very bad" instead of "catastrophe"?

C: Yes... somehow lighter.

T: Now: you said "the boss will be furious." How do you know that?

C: Well, he's always unhappy when something goes wrong.

T: Are "unhappy" and "furious" the same thing?

C: No, probably not...

T: And one more question: did the project absolutely HAVE to succeed? Is there a law of the universe that your projects MUST be successful?

C: No, of course not. But I wanted it to succeed.

T: There! "I wanted" — that's rational. "It HAD to" — that's a demand on reality, which creates suffering.

---

**Example 2: Working with global self-evaluation**

Client: I'm a total failure. Everything I take on — fails.

Therapist: A strong statement. You say "total failure" — that's a description of your entire personality as a whole. Is that even possible — to rate a whole person with a single word?

C: Well... maybe not entirely. But I have a lot of failures.

T: Okay, you have failures. Do you also have successes?

C: Well, some...

T: Name three.

C: (thinking) Graduated from university. Married a good woman. Raised two kids who seem all right.

T: Not bad for a "total failure," huh?

C: (smiling) Yes, that sounds strange...

T: Look: you have failures — that's a fact. You have successes — also a fact. You're a person who sometimes fails and sometimes succeeds. Like all people. Calling yourself a "total failure" means taking a part of the truth and making it the whole truth. That's logically incorrect.

C: But I feel like a failure.

T: Emotional reasoning! "I feel like a failure — therefore I am a failure." But a feeling is not proof. You could feel like an elephant — that wouldn't make you an elephant.

---

**Example 3: Working with low frustration tolerance**

Client: I can't stand this job. My colleagues are annoying, my boss is unfair. I just can't take it anymore!

Therapist: "Can't stand it" literally means you'll die or go insane. Are you dying?

C: No, but it's really hard.

T: "Really hard" — that's honest. "Can't stand it" — that's untrue. You're standing it every day. You come to work, do your tasks, go home. Technically — you're standing it.

C: But I hate it!

T: Hating it is your right. Question: does the belief "I can't stand it" help you?

C: No... it only makes it worse.

T: What if you replaced it with: "I really dislike this job, it's hard for me, but I'm capable of coping while I look for other options"?

C: Sounds... more manageable.

T: And notice: that doesn't mean "endure and stay silent." You can look for another job, set boundaries, talk to HR. But from the position "I'm coping" you'll act more effectively than from the position "I can't."

## CHAPTER 14: COMPARING REBT WITH OTHER APPROACHES

**REBT and Beck's cognitive therapy:**

| Aspect | REBT (Ellis) | CT (Beck) |
|--------|--------------|-----------|
| Key focus | Irrational beliefs (MUST) | Automatic thoughts, distortions |
| Therapist's style | Active, directive, confrontational | Socratic, exploratory |
| Philosophical base | Stoicism, Adler | Empirical observations |
| View of emotions | Healthy vs unhealthy negative emotions | Normalizing negative emotions |
| Work with the past | Minimal, focus on the present | May include schemas from the past |

**REBT and Rogers' humanistic therapy:**

| Aspect | REBT (Ellis) | Client-centered (Rogers) |
|--------|--------------|--------------------------|
| Therapist's role | Teacher, coach | Facilitator |
| Directiveness | High | Low |
| Focus | Changing beliefs | Acceptance, congruence |
| Unconditional acceptance | Yes (three types) | Yes (core of the approach) |
| Attitude toward emotions | Disputing unhealthy ones | Reflecting and validating all |

**What all approaches share:**
- Respect for the client
- Faith in the person's ability to change
- The importance of the therapeutic relationship
- The client's responsibility for their own life

## CHAPTER 15: HOMEWORK IN REBT

Ellis was a pioneer in the use of homework in therapy.

**Typical homework assignments:**

**1. The ABCDE form**
The client records:
- A: What happened
- B: What I thought/believed
- C: What I felt and did
- D: How the belief can be disputed
- E: The new rational belief and its effect

**2. Bibliotherapy**
Reading Ellis' books:
- "How to Stubbornly Refuse to Make Yourself Miserable About Anything — Yes, Anything!"
- "A Guide to Rational Living" (with Robert Harper)

**3. Behavioral experiments**
- Exposure exercises for fears
- "Shame attacks" — deliberately doing "shameful" things
- Practicing refusals and requests

**4. Rational self-statements**
Repeating rational alternatives:
- "I want success, but I don't need it"
- "Rejection is unpleasant, but not awful"
- "I'm a person who makes mistakes — like all people"

**5. Audio recordings**
Ellis often recorded his sessions and gave them to clients to listen to. He also recommended audio courses.

## CHAPTER 16: INTEGRATION WITH OTHER AUTHORS

How REBT combines with Rogers, Beck, and Frankl in our system:

**Rogers sets the tone:**
- Unconditional acceptance (which Ellis also values)
- Empathy and warmth
- Reflecting feelings

**Frankl provides meaning:**
- Once irrational beliefs are disputed — what next?
- Meaning as the foundation for a rational life
- Suffering as an opportunity for growth

**Beck provides the tools:**
- The thought record (similar to the ABCDE form)
- Socratic questions
- Behavioral experiments

**Ellis provides the philosophy:**
- Deep work with shoulds
- Three types of unconditional acceptance
- Distinguishing healthy from unhealthy emotions
- Energetic disputing

**Example of integration for a client with anxiety:**

1. **Rogers:** Listen, reflect feelings, create a safe space
2. **Beck:** Identify automatic thoughts ("I'll fail," "Everyone will laugh")
3. **Ellis:** Find the deep should ("I MUST succeed, otherwise I'm worthless") and dispute it
4. **Frankl:** Help find meaning in acting despite the anxiety ("Why is this important to you?")
