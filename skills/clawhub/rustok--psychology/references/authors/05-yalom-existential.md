# Irvin Yalom: Existential Psychotherapy

## CHAPTER 1: WHO IRVIN YALOM IS

Irvin David Yalom (born June 13, 1931) is an American psychiatrist, psychotherapist, writer, and professor emeritus of psychiatry at Stanford University. One of the founders of modern existential psychotherapy and the world's leading expert on group therapy.

In the 1982 survey — among the three most influential psychotherapists in the world (alongside Rogers and Freud). Psychology Today called his works "among the most significant in the history of psychotherapy."

**Childhood and origins:**
Born in Washington, D.C., to a family of Jewish immigrants from Belarus (the village of Telz near the Polish border). His parents came to the US after World War I and opened a grocery store. The family lived in an apartment above the store in a poor neighborhood.

His childhood was spent among books — little Irvin spent all his free time in the local library, reading. This shaped him as a writer and thinker.

**Education:**
- 1952: Bachelor's degree, George Washington University
- 1956: MD, Boston University School of Medicine
- Internship: Mount Sinai Hospital, New York
- Residency: Phipps Clinic, Johns Hopkins Hospital
- 1960–1962: Military service, Tripler General Hospital, Honolulu

**Career at Stanford:**
From 1963 — faculty member at Stanford University. Received tenure in 1968. Quickly became the leading expert on group therapy. His textbook "The Theory and Practice of Group Psychotherapy" (1970) became a classic — over 700,000 copies sold, translated into 12+ languages, now in its 6th edition.

**Influences:**
Yalom studied with Rollo May (one of the founders of existential psychology in the US), who was for a time his personal therapist. Other influences: Karen Horney, Erich Fromm, Carl Rogers, and the philosophers Kierkegaard, Heidegger, Sartre, Camus.

**The writer:**
Yalom is unique in writing both scientific works and fiction. His novels — "When Nietzsche Wept" (1992, won the Commonwealth Gold Medal), "Lying on the Couch" (1996), "The Schopenhauer Cure" (2005), "The Spinoza Problem" (2012) — became bestsellers and have been translated into 20+ languages.

"Love's Executioner" (1989) — a collection of 10 therapeutic tales — became a classic and showed that psychotherapy can be as gripping as good literature.

## CHAPTER 2: DID YALOM LIVE HIS OWN TEACHING?

**The fear of death — the central theme of Yalom's life:**

Yalom openly admitted that he struggled with death anxiety all his life. In the book "Staring at the Sun" (2008), written at 70, he devoted an entire chapter to his personal fear of death.

"I have worked with patients on the fear of death my entire career. But that doesn't mean I have conquered this fear myself. It is always with me."

**The death of his wife Marilyn:**
In 2019, Marilyn Yalom died — Irvin's wife of 65 years (they had met as teenagers). Marilyn was a writer and scholar, the author of books on cultural history.

When Marilyn was diagnosed with cancer (multiple myeloma), the couple decided to write a book together — "A Matter of Death and Life" (2021). The chapters alternate: Marilyn writes about preparing for death; Irvin — about preparing to live without her.

It is a stunning document — a man who spent his whole life helping others cope with the fear of death now going through the greatest loss himself.

**An unexpected healing:**
After Marilyn's death, Yalom reported that his own fear of death had vanished. The loss of the beloved person — the one who knew him best of all — proved stronger than the fear of his own death.

"In all my life, all the books, all the therapy — nothing helped me as much as losing Marilyn. Now I am not afraid of death. I am ready."

**Practicing into deep old age:**
At 93 (2024), Yalom continues to write and give interviews. His latest works — on grief, loss, and aging — are written with the same honesty as his early ones.

## CHAPTER 3: THE CORE IDEA — FOUR EXISTENTIAL GIVENS

The core of Yalom's existential therapy is four "ultimate concerns" — four inescapable givens of human existence:

### 1. DEATH

**The essence:** We exist now, but one day we will cease to exist. This is inevitable.

**The existential conflict:** The tension between awareness of the inevitability of death and the wish to go on living.

**How it manifests:**
- Death anxiety (overt or hidden)
- Avoidance of thoughts about death
- Hypochondria, panic attacks
- Compulsive behavior (an attempt to "outrun" the awareness)
- Narcissism ("I'm special, the rules don't apply to me")
- The search for a "rescuer" (religion, a guru, a partner)

**Yalom quote:**
"Although the physical reality of death destroys us, the idea of death saves us."

**The therapeutic paradox:**
Fully facing death does not paralyze — it liberates. When a person truly accepts their mortality — they begin to live more fully, to value each moment, to make choices in accordance with their values.

### 2. FREEDOM

**The essence:** We are wholly responsible for our lives, our choices, our fate. Beneath us — a void. There is no external structure to guide us.

**The existential conflict:** The tension between awareness of freedom/responsibility and the wish for support, structure, certainty.

**Yalom on freedom:**
"Responsibility means authorship — authorship of one's own self, destiny, life situation, feelings, and, if such be the case, one's own suffering."

"To be responsible is a frightening insight."

**How it manifests:**
- Avoiding decisions
- Shifting responsibility onto others
- The victim position ("life is unfair to me")
- Dependence on authorities
- Compulsiveness (the illusion of control)

**Existential anxiety:**
"To experience existence in this way is a dizzying sensation. The ground disappears from under one's feet. 'Groundlessness' is a common term for the subjective experience of becoming aware of responsibility."

### 3. ISOLATION

**The essence:** We are fundamentally alone. We are born alone and we die alone. No one can fully share our experience.

**Three types of isolation:**
1. **Interpersonal** — loneliness from the absence of relationships
2. **Intrapersonal** — alienation from parts of oneself, dissociation
3. **Existential** — fundamental aloneness, separateness from the world and other people, which persists even within good relationships

**Yalom:**
"No matter how close we get to another person, there always remains a final, unbridgeable gap. Each of us enters existence alone and must depart from it alone."

**The existential conflict:** The tension between awareness of our absolute aloneness and the wish for closeness, protection, belonging.

### 4. MEANINGLESSNESS

**The essence:** The universe has no built-in meaning. We must create meaning ourselves.

**The existential conflict:** The tension between the need for meaning and the apparent meaninglessness of the world.

**Yalom on meaning:**
The search for meaning is paradoxical (as with Frankl and pleasure): it cannot be attained directly. Meaning arises as a by-product of engagement — in relationships, work, creativity, helping others.

**The therapeutic response:**
If a patient reports an absence of meaning, the therapist should:
1. Check for other problems (depression, isolation, fear of death)
2. Help remove obstacles to engagement
3. Use their own engagement with the patient as a model

"The wish to engage with life is always present within the patient."

## CHAPTER 4: DEFENSES AGAINST EXISTENTIAL ANXIETY

Yalom describes typical defense mechanisms against each of the four givens:

**Defenses against the fear of death:**

| Defense | Description | Problem |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| Belief in one's specialness | "It won't happen to me" | Narcissism, risky behavior |
| Belief in a rescuer | Someone/something will protect me | Dependency, religious fanaticism |
| Denial | "I won't think about it" | Anxiety breaks through in other forms |
| Compulsiveness | Constant busyness | Avoidance, burnout |

**Defenses against freedom:**

| Defense | Description | Problem |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| Compulsiveness | "I had no choice" | Refusal of responsibility |
| Shifting responsibility | "It's because of them/circumstances" | Victim position |
| Denial of responsibility | "I didn't choose this" | Helplessness |
| Avoiding decisions | Procrastination, postponement | Stagnation |

**Defenses against isolation:**

| Defense | Description | Problem |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| Fusion with another | Dissolving into the relationship | Loss of self |
| Sexual compulsiveness | The illusion of closeness | Emptiness afterward |
| Idealization | "He/she is my everything" | Disappointment |

**Defenses against meaninglessness:**

| Defense | Description | Problem |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| Compulsive activity | Constant busyness | Avoiding the question |
| Crusading | Fighting for "the right cause" | Fanaticism |
| Nihilism | "Nothing has meaning" | Depression, apathy |

## CHAPTER 5: THE HERE-AND-NOW

Yalom's central technique is working in the "here-and-now" of the therapeutic relationship.

**The principle:**
How the patient behaves with the therapist is a microcosm of how they behave in life. The therapeutic relationship becomes a laboratory for exploring and changing patterns.

**Yalom's mantra:**
"It's the relationship that heals. It's the relationship that heals. It's the relationship that heals."

**How to use the here-and-now:**

1. **Observation:** How the patient enters, sits down, begins talking, expresses emotions
2. **Reflection:** How the patient's words and actions affect the therapist
3. **Discussion:** Open exploration of what is happening between them

**Example questions:**
- "What is happening between us right now?"
- "How are you feeling with me in this moment?"
- "Do you notice that with me you behave the same way you describe behaving with other people?"

**Yalom:**
"Therapy must be relationship-driven, not theory-driven."

## CHAPTER 6: THE THERAPIST AS A "FELLOW TRAVELER"

Yalom insists on equal, authentic relationships between therapist and patient.

**The "fellow travelers" metaphor:**
Therapist and patient are two people traveling together along the mysterious and difficult road of life. The therapist is not higher, not wiser — they have simply walked a bit further and can share their experience.

**The difference from the classical approach:**
The Freudian model — the therapist as a "blank screen," detached, never revealing themselves.
Yalom — the therapist as a living, authentic person, willing to be vulnerable.

**Therapist self-disclosure:**
Yalom actively uses self-disclosure — sharing his feelings, reactions, sometimes personal experience. But he does this in the patient's interest, not for his own relief.

**Principles of self-disclosure:**
- Share feelings that arise in the relationship with the patient
- Acknowledge your mistakes openly
- Do not hide that the patient matters to you
- Avoid disclosure that would burden the patient

**Yalom:**
"Let the patient matter to you. Let them enter your mind, change you — and do not conceal this from them."

## CHAPTER 7: THE 11 THERAPEUTIC FACTORS OF GROUP THERAPY

Yalom created the most influential model of group therapy. He identified 11 "curative factors" explaining why a group works:

### 1. Instillation of Hope
Seeing how others cope and change instills hope for one's own healing.

### 2. Universality
"I'm not the only one." Realizing that others struggle with similar problems lifts shame and isolation.

### 3. Imparting Information
Teaching, advice, psychoeducation from the therapist and the participants.

### 4. Altruism
By helping others, a person feels valuable and useful. This raises self-esteem.

### 5. The Corrective Recapitulation of the Primary Family Group
The group reproduces family dynamics. An opportunity appears to work through family patterns in a safe environment.

### 6. Development of Socializing Techniques
The group is a safe place to practice new ways of relating.

### 7. Imitative Behavior
Participants learn from one another, modeling the behavior of the therapist and other group members.

### 8. Interpersonal Learning
Receiving feedback on how one is perceived by others. Understanding one's impact on people.

### 9. Group Cohesiveness
A sense of belonging, acceptance, and value within the group. The analogue of the therapeutic alliance in individual therapy.

### 10. Catharsis
Expressing strong emotions in a safe environment. Release.

### 11. Existential Factors
Accepting the realities of life — its limits, responsibility, aloneness, mortality. The group helps one face these together.

**Yalom on cohesiveness:**
"Cohesiveness is the primary curative factor in group therapy. Without cohesiveness, the other factors do not work."

## CHAPTER 8: YALOM'S REAL CASES

**Case 1: Thelma — "Love's Executioner" (from the collection of the same name)**

The problem: A 70-year-old woman, chronic depression for 20 years. She came in crisis after a suicide attempt. It turned out that for 8 years she had been obsessed with a "romance" with her previous therapist Matthew — 27 days of intimacy, after which he broke off all contact.

Those 27 days had become the only thing giving her life meaning. For 8 years she had lived in the past.

Yalom's intervention:
Yalom understood that Thelma could not live while clinging to this illusion of mutual love. He decided to become the "love's executioner" — to help her see the truth.

With Thelma's permission, Yalom met with Matthew. It turned out: Matthew had never loved Thelma. His "feelings" had been a collapse of professional boundaries, not love.

Yalom delicately but honestly conveyed this to Thelma: "He never loved you. His boundaries collapsed for a time — personal and professional — but it was not love on his part."

The result: Thelma terminated therapy immediately afterward — exactly when Yalom believed the real work was just beginning. She chose to leave.

Yalom's lesson: "The therapist does not control the process. The patient can choose anything — even what seems against their interests. A wounded soul often chooses more wounding... until it stops."

---

**Case 2: Betty — "The Fat Lady" (from "Love's Executioner")**

The problem: A woman of 250 pounds (113 kg) at 5'2" (157 cm), with an eating disorder.

The uniqueness: Yalom honestly acknowledges his own feelings — he felt repelled by obese people. This became part of the therapy.

The intervention:
Yalom worked with his own reactions, using them as data. He did not hide his difficulties from himself, but did not impose them on Betty.

Therapy revealed: behind the obesity lay deep loneliness, depression, fear of intimacy. The weight was a defense.

The result: As therapy progressed, Yalom began to see the person behind the weight. His repulsion gave way to empathy. Betty began to lose weight — not because it was the goal, but because the need for protection had fallen away.

The lesson: The therapist is no saint. They have prejudices, reactions, blind spots. Honesty with oneself about these reactions is the key to effective work.

---

**Case 3: Group therapy with oncology patients**

The study: Yalom with colleagues (Spiegel, Bloom, 1981) conducted a study of group therapy with women with metastatic breast cancer.

The method: Weekly group sessions focused on existential themes — fear of death, meaning of life, relationships.

The results:
- Significant improvement in quality of life
- Reduced anxiety and depression
- An unexpected result: an average increase in survival of 18 months

This result generated enormous interest and debate. Although later studies did not always reproduce the survival effect, the effect on quality of life is consistently confirmed.

---

**Case 4: Yalom's own grief (from "A Matter of Death and Life")**

The context: After the death of his wife Marilyn in 2019.

The process:
Yalom reread his own book "Staring at the Sun" — and discovered that, due to declining memory, he had forgotten much of what he had written. He benefited from the advice of his own younger self — an unexpected "time-travel therapy."

The insight:
"In a lifetime of working on the fear of death — my own and my patients' — I have found nothing more effective than losing the beloved person. Since Marilyn's death, I no longer fear death."

## CHAPTER 9: KEY TECHNIQUES AND INTERVENTIONS

**1. The "Disidentification" exercise:**
The patient writes down answers to the question "Who am I?" (roles, qualities, relationships). Then they meditate, letting go of each identity one by one.

The goal: To realize that there is a "self" behind all the roles. Preparation for accepting mortality.

**2. Working with dreams:**
Yalom uses dreams pragmatically — not for full interpretation, but for the "here-and-now" of the therapeutic relationship.

"Don't try to interpret the dream fully. Plunder it — take what is useful for the dynamics of the relationship."

**3. "What would you want written on your tombstone?"**
A question that helps clarify values and see the gap between the desired and the actual life.

**4. The "awakening experience":**
Using crises, illnesses, losses as opportunities for existential awakening. Yalom: "Patients who have faced death become wiser."

**5. "Rippling":**
A concept for working with the fear of death: we live on in the consequences of our actions, in the people we have touched, in the ideas we have passed on.

**6. The question "Is there a question to me in that?":**
When a patient makes a statement about the therapist: "You must be tired of me," Yalom asks: "Is there a question to me in that?" — inviting open dialogue.

## CHAPTER 10: "THE GIFT OF THERAPY" — YALOM'S 85 TIPS

In the book "The Gift of Therapy" (2001), Yalom collected 85 "tips for beginning therapists." Here are the key ones:

**On relationships:**
- "Therapy must be relationship-driven, not theory-driven"
- "Let the patient matter to you"
- "Acknowledge your errors openly and non-defensively"
- "Create a new therapy for each patient"

**On the here-and-now:**
- "Use it, use it, use it" (the title of the chapter on the here-and-now)
- "Observe how the patient enters, sits down, begins talking"
- "Your feelings are data about the patient"

**On self-disclosure:**
- "Be real. Use self-disclosure"
- "But disclose in the patient's interest, not for your own relief"

**On death:**
- "Talk about death. Patients who have faced death become wiser"
- "Two modes of existence: the everyday (absorbed in trivia) and the ontological (awareness of being)"

**On dreams:**
- "Don't try to interpret a dream fully. Plunder it pragmatically"

**On oneself:**
- "Take care of yourself. Seeing patients one-on-one all day is isolating"
- "Your personal therapy is the cornerstone of good work with patients"

## CHAPTER 11: WHEN TO USE EXISTENTIAL THERAPY

**High effectiveness:**
- Existential crises (loss of meaning, midlife crisis)
- Fear of death (overt or hidden)
- Severe illness, palliative care
- Grief and loss
- Life transitions (retirement, divorce, "empty nest syndrome")
- Problems with decision-making and responsibility
- Loneliness and isolation

**Group therapy is especially effective for:**
- Relationship problems
- Social anxiety
- Depression
- Addictions
- Cancer (support groups)

**When caution is required:**
- Acute psychotic states
- Severe depression (stabilization first)
- When the patient is not ready for deep work
- When concrete techniques are needed (e.g., for phobias, CBT may be more useful)

**Features of the approach:**
- No fixed protocol
- Duration varies (from short-term to many years)
- Can be integrated with other approaches
- Requires the therapist to have worked through existential themes themselves

## CHAPTER 13: PRACTICAL DIALOGUE EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Working with the fear of death**

Patient: I can't stop thinking about the fact that I'll die. It paralyzes me.

Therapist: Tell me more about these thoughts. When do they come?

P: At night, especially. I lie there thinking: one day I simply won't exist. And I panic.

T: What are you feeling right now, talking about this?

P: Anxiety. A tightness in my chest.

T: I notice this is the first time you've spoken about this in our meetings. What helped you share it today?

P: I don't know... Maybe I got tired of carrying it alone.

T: "Carrying it alone" — do you feel alone with this fear?

P: Yes. No one wants to talk about it. Everyone pretends death doesn't exist.

T: And yet here we are — two people talking about death. What is that like for you?

P: Strange... but somehow easier. Not as frightening.

T: You know, many philosophers have said that fully facing death does not paralyze — it liberates. If you know your time is limited — how would you want to live it?

P: (pause) I'd want to stop spending it on things that don't matter to me.

T: Which things, exactly?

---

**Example 2: Working with responsibility**

Patient: My life is a complete failure. My parents didn't give me a proper education, my wife doesn't support me, my boss doesn't value me...

Therapist: I hear a lot of people who are to blame for your situation. And where are you in this picture?

P: What do you mean?

T: You're describing a life that "happened" to you. As if you're a passenger in your own life.

P: What could I have done? The circumstances were against me.

T: Perhaps. And yet — were there moments when you made a choice? Chose this job, this wife, this reaction to your parents?

P: (defensively) I didn't choose my parents!

T: True. But you chose how to relate to what they gave you. You are choosing right now — to tell this story as a victim or as its author.

P: (long pause) That's... frightening.

T: Yes. Freedom is frightening. Because it means responsibility. But it also means you can change something.

---

**Example 3: The here-and-now in the relationship**

Patient: (looking away, speaking monotonously about their week)

Therapist: May I interrupt for a second? I notice you're not looking at me and are speaking as if into empty space. What is happening between us right now?

P: (embarrassed) I don't know... I feel like you're bored listening to my problems.

T: Is there a question to me in that?

P: (pause) Are you bored?

T: Honestly — I feel a distance between us right now. As if you're here, but not quite with me. And I wonder — is this how you feel with other people?

P: (eyes moistening) Yes... I always feel like no one is truly interested in me.

T: And here, with me, you expect the same — that I'll lose interest too?

P: Yes.

T: What if we use this room as a laboratory? What if here you try to be truly real — look me in the eye, say something that matters — and we see what happens?

## CHAPTER 14: COMPARISON WITH OTHER AUTHORS

**Yalom and Frankl:**

| Aspect | Yalom | Frankl |
|--------|-------|--------|
| Focus | Four givens (death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness) | Meaning as the central motivation |
| Approach to meaning | Meaning is created, arises from engagement | Meaning is found, discovered |
| Suffering | Part of the human lot | An opportunity for growth |
| Style | Interpersonal, here-and-now | Philosophical, didactic |

**In common:** Both are existentialists; both work with meaning and death. Yalom held Frankl in high regard.

**Yalom and Rogers:**

| Aspect | Yalom | Rogers |
|--------|-------|--------|
| Directiveness | Moderate, interprets | Low, follows the client |
| Relationship | Fellow travelers, mutual influence | Facilitator of the client's growth |
| Self-disclosure | Active | Limited |
| Focus | Existential themes | The client's experience |

**In common:** Both value the relationship as the foundation of therapy; both are humanists.

**Yalom and Beck/Ellis:**

| Aspect | Yalom | Beck/Ellis |
|--------|-------|------------|
| Focus | Existential givens | Cognitive distortions, beliefs |
| Techniques | Here-and-now, interpretation | Disputing, behavioral experiments |
| Structure | Flexible, without protocol | Structured, manualized |
| Goal | Accepting the existential givens | Changing thinking and behavior |

**Yalom on CBT:**
"CBT is not what it's taken to be. Don't be afraid of the existential 'bogeyman.'" Yalom criticizes excessive manualization and calls for flexibility.

## CHAPTER 15: INTEGRATION WITH THE OTHER AUTHORS OF THE PROJECT

Yalom complements the authors already collected beautifully:

**Rogers + Yalom:**
Rogers creates safety and acceptance → Yalom adds existential depth and the here-and-now.

**Frankl + Yalom:**
Both work with meaning and death. Frankl — "meaning can be found in suffering." Yalom — "meaning arises from engagement and accepting the givens."

**Beck/Ellis + Yalom:**
Beck/Ellis provide the tools for cognitive work. Yalom — the existential context: "Why change one's thinking? Because life is finite and choices matter."

**Example of integration for a client in an existential crisis:**

1. **Rogers:** Unconditional acceptance, empathy, safety
2. **Yalom:** Exploring the four givens (what exactly is troubling — death? freedom? isolation? meaninglessness?)
3. **Frankl:** Helping find meaning through values, creativity, suffering
4. **Beck/Ellis:** Working with specific cognitive distortions, if present
