# AARON BECK — COGNITIVE THERAPY

---

## CHAPTER 1: WHO AARON BECK WAS

Aaron Temkin Beck (1921–2021) was an American psychiatrist, the founder of cognitive therapy. He lived to 100. He worked until the last days of his life. He died peacefully at home in Philadelphia, surrounded by family.

Beck began as a psychoanalyst but became disillusioned with Freud's approach. Observing depressed patients, he noticed that their suffering was connected not to unconscious conflicts, but to distorted thoughts — negative interpretations of themselves, the world, and the future.

In 1977, cognitive therapy became the first talk-therapy method to outperform antidepressants in clinical trials. Since then, more than 2,000 studies have confirmed the effectiveness of CBT.

Beck founded the Beck Institute for Cognitive Behavior Therapy together with his daughter Judith Beck. He also developed scales for measuring depression and anxiety (Beck Depression Inventory, Beck Anxiety Inventory) that are used worldwide.

Beck: "People have tremendous resources for self-understanding and for changing their attitudes and behavior — these resources can be tapped if a definable climate is created."

## CHAPTER 2: THE CORE IDEA — THOUGHTS DETERMINE FEELINGS

Beck's cognitive therapy is based on a simple but powerful idea: it is not events that cause our emotions, but our interpretation of those events.

**The basic CBT model:**

Situation → Thought → Emotion → Behavior

Example:
- Situation: A friend didn't reply to a message
- Thought: "He's ignoring me, he doesn't need me"
- Emotion: Sadness, hurt
- Behavior: I withdraw, stop writing

Alternative interpretation:
- Situation: A friend didn't reply to a message
- Thought: "He's probably busy. He'll reply later"
- Emotion: Calm
- Behavior: I carry on with my day

The same situation — different thoughts — different emotions. By changing thinking, you can change your emotional state.

## CHAPTER 3: THE COGNITIVE TRIAD OF DEPRESSION

Beck discovered that depressed people have a stable pattern of negative thinking — the cognitive triad.

**The three components of the triad:**

**1. Negative view of the self**
"I'm worthless," "I'm a failure," "Something is wrong with me," "I don't deserve love"

**2. Negative view of the world**
"The world is cruel," "People are selfish," "No one can be trusted," "Everything is against me"

**3. Negative view of the future**
"Nothing will change," "It will only get worse," "There's no hope," "I'll never be happy"

These three negative beliefs reinforce one another and create a vicious circle of depression. The task of therapy is to identify and challenge these beliefs.

**Questions for identifying the triad:**

About the self:
- How would you describe yourself?
- What do you think of yourself as a person?

About the world:
- How do you perceive the people around you?
- What do you think about how life works?

About the future:
- How do you see your future?
- What do you think lies ahead for you?

## CHAPTER 4: AUTOMATIC THOUGHTS

Automatic thoughts are quick, involuntary thoughts that arise in response to a situation. They appear at the edge of awareness — we often don't notice them, but they strongly influence emotions.

**Characteristics of automatic thoughts:**
- They arise instantly, without effort
- They seem like the truth; they go unquestioned
- They are often exaggerated or distorted
- They are linked to negative emotions
- They repeat in similar situations

**Examples of automatic thoughts:**

With anxiety:
- "Something bad is going to happen"
- "I won't cope"
- "Everyone will be staring at me"
- "I'll disgrace myself"

With depression:
- "I'm good for nothing"
- "I'll never get better"
- "I'm in everyone's way"
- "What's the point of trying"

With anger:
- "He did it on purpose"
- "I'm not respected"
- "This is unfair"
- "You can't treat me like this"

**How to identify automatic thoughts:**

Questions for the client:
- What flashed through your mind in that moment?
- What did you think right before you felt this?
- What images or words came up?
- What does this situation mean to you?

## CHAPTER 5: COGNITIVE DISTORTIONS

Cognitive distortions are systematic errors in thinking that distort the perception of reality. Beck identified them by observing depressed patients. Later, his student David Burns expanded and popularized the list.

**The main cognitive distortions:**

---

**1. Black-and-white thinking (all or nothing)**

Seeing only extremes, with no shades in between.

Examples:
- "If I'm not perfect — I'm a complete failure"
- "Either he loves me, or he hates me"
- "Either I'll do this excellently, or I won't do it at all"

Question to challenge it: "Is there anything between these extremes?"

---

**2. Catastrophizing**

Expecting the worst outcome, exaggerating danger.

Examples:
- "If I fail the exam — my life is ruined"
- "This headache is definitely a tumor"
- "If he leaves — I'll never find anyone else"

Question to challenge it: "What is the realistically likely outcome? What's the worst that can happen, and could I cope with it?"

---

**3. Mind reading**

Being sure you know what others think, without evidence.

Examples:
- "He thinks I'm stupid"
- "They consider me a loser"
- "She's definitely angry at me"

Question to challenge it: "How do I know this? Is there any evidence?"

---

**4. Fortune-telling**

Being sure of a negative outcome without grounds.

Examples:
- "The interview will go terribly"
- "The relationship is doomed"
- "I'll definitely fail"

Question to challenge it: "Can I know the future? Have there been times when my predictions didn't come true?"

---

**5. Overgeneralization**

Drawing global conclusions from a single incident.

Examples:
- "I was rejected — I'm always rejected"
- "I made a mistake — I always ruin everything"
- "This didn't work out — nothing ever works out for me"

Markers: the words "always," "never," "everyone," "no one"

Question to challenge it: "Does this REALLY happen ALWAYS? Are there exceptions?"

---

**6. Discounting the positive**

Ignoring or downplaying positive experience.

Examples:
- "I just got lucky"
- "That doesn't count"
- "Anyone could have done it"
- "He paid the compliment out of politeness"

Question to challenge it: "Why doesn't the positive count, while the negative does?"

---

**7. Personalization**

Taking personally things that are not connected to you.

Examples:
- "He's gloomy — I must have offended him"
- "The project failed because of me"
- "If I were better — this wouldn't have happened"

Question to challenge it: "What other factors could have played a role? Is this really about me?"

---

**8. Emotional reasoning**

Taking feelings as proof of reality.

Examples:
- "I feel like a failure — therefore I am a failure"
- "I'm scared — therefore it's dangerous"
- "I feel guilty — therefore I am guilty"

Question to challenge it: "Is a feeling a fact or an interpretation?"

---

**9. Should statements**

Rigid rules about how things must be.

Examples:
- "I must be perfect"
- "People must be fair"
- "He should have understood"

Markers: "must," "should," "have to," "ought to"

Question to challenge it: "Where does this rule come from? What will happen if it's broken?"

---

**10. Labeling**

A global negative evaluation of yourself or others based on behavior.

Examples:
- "I'm a failure" (instead of "I made a mistake in this")
- "He's selfish" (instead of "he acted selfishly")
- "I'm stupid" (instead of "I didn't understand this topic")

Question to challenge it: "Does one action define the whole person?"

## CHAPTER 6: TECHNIQUE — THE THOUGHT RECORD

The thought record is the central technique of cognitive therapy. It is a structured way to identify and challenge automatic thoughts.

**The five columns of a thought record:**

1. **Situation**: What happened? Where, when, with whom?
2. **Emotions**: What did you feel? Rate the intensity (0–100%)
3. **Automatic thoughts**: What flashed through your mind?
4. **Evidence for and against**: Facts supporting and contradicting the thought
5. **Alternative thought**: A more balanced view

**Example of a completed record:**

Situation: A colleague didn't say hello in the morning

Emotions: Hurt (70%), anxiety (50%)

Automatic thought: "He's angry at me. I must have done something wrong"

Evidence FOR:
- He usually says hello
- We had a tense conversation last week

Evidence AGAINST:
- He looked tired and preoccupied
- He didn't greet others either
- I didn't do anything bad
- He may have his own problems

Alternative thought: "Most likely he was simply absorbed in his own thoughts or slept badly. It's not necessarily connected to me"

Emotions afterward: Hurt (20%), anxiety (10%)

## CHAPTER 7: TECHNIQUE — SOCRATIC QUESTIONS

Socratic questions are a method of gently challenging thoughts through questions rather than through argument. The therapist does not tell the client they are wrong — they help the client see the limitations of their thinking for themselves.

**Categories of Socratic questions:**

**Examining the evidence:**
- What evidence supports this thought?
- What evidence contradicts it?
- Is this a fact or an interpretation?
- How do I know this for certain?

**Examining alternatives:**
- Is there another explanation?
- How would another person look at this?
- How would I look at this if I were in a good mood?
- What would I tell a friend in this situation?

**Examining consequences:**
- What's the worst that can happen?
- What's the best that can happen?
- What is most likely?
- Could I cope if the worst happened?

**Examining usefulness:**
- Does this thought help me?
- What do I gain by thinking this way?
- How would my life change if I thought differently?

**Example of a Socratic dialogue:**

Client: "I'm a complete failure"

Therapist: "What does it mean to be a complete failure?"

Client: "Well, I can't do anything right"

Therapist: "Nothing at all? Has there been even one occasion when you did something right?"

Client: "Well... at work yesterday I gave a good presentation"

Therapist: "How does that fit with the thought that you can't do anything right?"

Client: "I guess it's an exaggeration..."

Therapist: "How would you rephrase that thought more accurately?"

Client: "I sometimes make mistakes, but sometimes things go well for me"

## CHAPTER 8: TECHNIQUE — BEHAVIORAL EXPERIMENTS

A behavioral experiment is a way to test negative beliefs in practice. Instead of merely reasoning about thoughts, the client tests them in real life.

**Structure of a behavioral experiment:**

1. **Identify the belief**: Which thought are we testing?
2. **Formulate the prediction**: What does the client expect will happen?
3. **Plan the experiment**: What exactly will be done?
4. **Run the experiment**: Carry out the plan
5. **Evaluate the result**: What actually happened?
6. **Draw conclusions**: Was the belief confirmed?

**Examples of behavioral experiments:**

Belief: "If I ask for help, people will think I'm weak"

Experiment: Ask a colleague to help with a small task

Prediction: He'll look at me with contempt or refuse

Result: The colleague gladly helped and said, "Feel free to ask again"

Conclusion: My prediction was not confirmed. People don't see asking for help as weakness

---

Belief: "If I voice my opinion, I'll be judged"

Experiment: Voice an opinion at a meeting

Prediction: Everyone will criticize me; I'll feel humiliated

Result: Some agreed, some didn't; the discussion went normally

Conclusion: Voicing an opinion is not as dangerous as I thought

## CHAPTER 9: TECHNIQUE — BEHAVIORAL ACTIVATION

Behavioral activation is a technique for working with depression. When a person is depressed, they stop doing things that used to bring pleasure. This intensifies the depression. Behavioral activation breaks this vicious circle.

**The principle:**
Don't wait for motivation — act. Motivation comes after action, not before.

**The vicious circle of depression:**
Low mood → Reduced activity → Less positive experience → Even lower mood → Even less activity

**The recovery cycle:**
A small action → A small positive experience → Slightly better mood → The next action becomes easier

**Steps of behavioral activation:**

1. **Activity monitoring**: Record what you do each hour and rate your mood (0–10)

2. **Identifying patterns**: Which activities improve your mood? Which worsen it?

3. **Scheduling activities**: Include in the schedule activities that bring:
   - Pleasure (pleasant things)
   - Mastery (a sense of achievement)

4. **Gradual increase**: Start small, add gradually

**Examples of activities:**

Pleasure: A walk, music, meeting a friend, good food, a film

Mastery: Tidying up, cooking, completing a work task, physical exercise

**The 5-minute rule:**
If you don't feel like doing anything — agree with yourself to do it for 5 minutes. Often, after 5 minutes, the impulse to continue appears.

## CHAPTER 10: CORE BELIEFS (SCHEMAS)

Core beliefs (schemas) are fundamental beliefs about oneself, others, and the world, formed in childhood. They lie deeper than automatic thoughts and determine them.

**The three levels of thinking:**

1. **Automatic thoughts** (the surface): "I botched the presentation"
2. **Intermediate beliefs** (rules): "If I'm not perfect, I'll be rejected"
3. **Core beliefs** (the core): "I don't deserve love"

**Typical negative core beliefs:**

About oneself:
- "I'm not good enough"
- "I don't deserve love"
- "I'm helpless"
- "I'm a bad person"
- "Something is wrong with me"

About others:
- "People can't be trusted"
- "People will use me"
- "No one will understand me"

About the world:
- "The world is dangerous"
- "Life is unfair"
- "Everything is meaningless"

**How to identify core beliefs:**

The "downward arrow" technique:

Automatic thought: "I performed poorly"
↓ What does that mean to you?
"People will think I'm incompetent"
↓ And then what?
"They won't respect me"
↓ And what does that mean?
"I don't deserve respect"
↓ Why?
"Because I'm not good enough" ← Core belief

## CHAPTER 11: BECK'S REAL CASES

**Case 1: The patient who revealed automatic thoughts (late 1950s)**

This case became a turning point in the history of psychotherapy — it is what led Beck to the discovery of cognitive therapy.

The patient: A woman whom Beck had been treating psychoanalytically for about a year. In sessions she talked about her sexual adventures with men — dramatically, in detail.

The session: At the end of one session, Beck asked how she was feeling. She answered: "Very anxious."

Beck, as a psychoanalyst, interpreted: "That makes sense — you have sexual impulses, they are forbidden, they break through your defenses, and that causes anxiety."

The patient agreed: "You're right, that's brilliant." But there was hesitation in her voice.

Beck noticed and asked. She admitted: "Actually, I was afraid I was boring you."

Beck: "You've been here all these months, and this is the first time you've told me that."

She: "It never occurred to me to say it out loud."

Beck: "How often do you think this?"

She: "Constantly. I think it when I'm with you, and when I'm with other people."

**Beck's discovery:**
- Her anxiety was not connected to repressed sexual impulses
- It was connected to the automatic thought "I'm boring"
- She slept with men because she thought she had nothing else to offer
- She told dramatic stories in therapy to seem less boring

Beck began asking other patients about their thoughts and discovered that everyone had a similar "stream" of negative automatic thoughts that they usually did not mention. He moved patients from the couch to a chair, to talk face to face and explore these thoughts together.

That is how cognitive therapy was born.

---

**Case 2: The first successful case with schizophrenia (1952)**

Publication title: "Successful outpatient psychotherapy of a chronic schizophrenic with a delusion based on borrowed guilt" (Psychiatry, 1952).

This was Beck's very first scientific publication — a full decade before the creation of cognitive therapy. The case shows that even then Beck was intuitively working with the patient's beliefs.

The patient had a chronic delusion of guilt — he felt guilty for something that had actually been done by someone else ("borrowed guilt").

Beck worked with the patient's beliefs, helping him see the illogic of the delusion. The therapy was successful — the delusion was eliminated.

Fifty years later (2002), this case was republished as a precursor of cognitive therapy for schizophrenia.

---

**Case 3: Suicide prevention — the University of Pennsylvania study**

Beck and his team developed a cognitive therapy protocol for people who had attempted suicide. Results of the randomized controlled trial (Brown et al., 2005, JAMA):

- Participants: people who had recently attempted suicide
- Method: 10 sessions of cognitive therapy aimed specifically at suicidal behavior (rather than depression as such)
- Result: **a 50% reduction in repeat suicide attempts**

A key element: working with the "suicide mode" — the complex of thoughts, emotions, and behaviors that activates in a crisis. Patients were taught to:
- Recognize triggers and early signs of crisis
- Create a safety plan
- Challenge hopelessness
- Use coping skills

This protocol (CT-SP — Cognitive Therapy for Suicide Prevention) is now used in the US Army, where it showed a 60% reduction in repeat attempts.

---

**Case 4: Working with veterans and severe schizophrenia (2000s–2021)**

In the last 20 years of his life, Beck focused on the most severe cases — patients with schizophrenia whom traditional therapy had not helped.

He developed Recovery-Oriented Cognitive Therapy (CT-R) — an approach that focuses not on symptoms, but on the patient's strengths and goals.

Beck in an interview: "Perhaps I was wrong all this time, focusing on the negative. Maybe the focus should always have been on people's strengths."

CT-R helps patients with schizophrenia to:
- Find periods of "adaptive mode" when they are receptive to change
- Activate hidden goals and motivations
- Restore their connection to life

The program was implemented in psychiatric hospitals in Philadelphia and Pennsylvania, then spread to other states.

Beck worked on a paper about CT-R ("Keys to Their Kingdom") literally until the last days of his life — at 100 years old, confined to bed.

## CHAPTER 12: WHEN TO USE COGNITIVE THERAPY

Beck's cognitive therapy has an extensive evidence base — more than 2,000 clinical studies.

**Indications — high effectiveness:**
- Depression (Beck's primary indication)
- Generalized anxiety disorder
- Panic disorder
- Social anxiety
- Specific phobias
- OCD (obsessive-compulsive disorder)
- PTSD
- Insomnia
- Eating disorders
- Addictions

**Indications — moderate effectiveness (as a complement):**
- Bipolar disorder
- Schizophrenia (Recovery-Oriented Cognitive Therapy)
- Chronic pain
- Somatic illnesses with a psychological component

**Features of application:**
- Structured approach — sessions have an agenda
- Focus on the present — not digging into the past
- Homework — practice between sessions
- Short-term — usually 12–20 sessions
- Measurable results — scales are used

**Combining with other approaches:**

Cognitive therapy combines well with:
- The Rogerian tone (empathy, acceptance)
- Frankl's logotherapy (meaning)
- Behavioral techniques (exposure, activation)

## CHAPTER 14: PRACTICAL DIALOGUE EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Identifying automatic thoughts**

Client: "I'm so anxious about tomorrow's interview that I can't sleep"

Therapist: "What exactly runs through your mind when you think about the interview?"

Client: "That I'll forget everything, I'll stumble over my words, and they'll think I'm incompetent"

Therapist: "These are automatic thoughts — they arise quickly and seem true. Let's write them down and look at them more closely..."

---

**Example 2: Challenging catastrophizing**

Client: "If I don't get this job — my career is over"

Therapist: "That sounds very categorical. Let's check: how many vacancies are there in your field?"

Client: "A lot, probably..."

Therapist: "If this one doesn't work out — what other options do you have?"

Client: "Well, there are several more companies I could apply to..."

Therapist: "How does that fit with the thought that your career is over?"

---

**Example 3: Working with black-and-white thinking**

Client: "I completely failed the project"

Therapist: "Completely — meaning there was nothing good in it?"

Client: "Well... the client liked the first part..."

Therapist: "What else went well?"

Client: "We met the deadlines... The team worked well together..."

Therapist: "How would you rate the project on a scale from 0 to 100, where 0 is a total failure and 100 is perfect?"

Client: "Probably 60–70..."

Therapist: "Is that different from a 'total failure'?"
