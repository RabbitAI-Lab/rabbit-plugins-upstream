# The Host Playbook

The optimizer handles the combinatorics; these are the human moves that make
an engineered guest list feel effortless rather than clinical.

## 1. The two rules of engineered mixing

1. **Every guest needs an anchor**: one person they already know OR a
   shared-interest bridge sitting/standing next to them. A guest with
   neither is your job all night — and they usually leave early.
2. **Spark comes from structured strangers; comfort comes from clusters.**
   Aim for roughly 40-60% new connections. All-strangers = networking
   event. All-clusters = three separate parties in one room.

## 2. Introduction scripts (the 10-second version)

Bad: "Ana, this is Tom." (silence)

Good: "Ana — Tom. You're both obsessed with bouldering. Tom just did his
first V5, Ana's been at it for years. Fight about beta." Then LEAVE.

Formula: **name + name + shared hook + one detail each + exit**. The exit
is the important part — stuck hosts kill the connection they just made.

## 3. Energy management

- **Talker scale** (1 listener - 5 dominator) matters most per-POD, not
  globally. One 5 and a table of 2s = dinner monologue; two 5s adjacent =
  competing monologues; a 4 with 2s works if a 3 sits between.
- Place your best **connector** (high talker, high empathy — usually you)
  where the weakest cluster sits.
- Quiet guests near the kitchen/host path get swept into logistics talk
  and never surface. Give them the corner seats with one strong comfort
  neighbor.

## 4. Pod design for stand-up parties (12-40 people)

Furniture, not seating charts, does the mixing:

- **Create 3-5 landing pods**: kitchen island, sofa + chairs, balcony,
  record player corner. Pods of 3-6 regenerate conversation naturally.
- **No single center**: one big couch facing one TV makes one conversation
  that guests can't enter. Angle chairs into circles.
- **Host arrows**: for the first hour, your only job is physically walking
  people between pods with an intro script. After that the party compounds.

## 5. Tension pairs (the maya/jon problem)

- Same room is usually survivable; **adjacency is not**. The optimizer
  enforces non-adjacency — but also give each a buffer person they like.
- Brief each side privately beforehand: "Jon's coming. You OK? Great,
  I'll keep you at opposite ends."
- If either asks the other not to come — that's a ranking decision, not a
  seating one. Have it before you send invitations, not after.

## 6. Recovery moves (mid-party)

| Failure | Move |
|---|---|
| Guest pinned in corner with stranger-silence | Re-introduce with NEW hook (not the same one that died) |
| Monologue forming | Redirect: "Sam, you've done this — what would you do?" |
| Two pods won't mix | Host carries one person over: physical migration beats invitation |
| Guest who knows nobody is floundering | Give them a JOB (playlist, photos, cocktail judge) — roles beat small talk |
| Dead table after dinner move | Pre-plan the post-dinner rearrangement; desserts in another room force rematching |

## 7. Recurring parties: the met-graph

For a monthly dinner: keep the JSON, and each run add `met` edges for pairs
who talked 10+ minutes. Next month's optimizer then maximizes NEW
connections (strangers ≠ met). Over a year a 20-person circle becomes a
dense community instead of host-centered spokes — the dinner-party version
of a friendship graph.

## 8. Numbers that matter

- Dinner party sweet spot: 6-10 (one conversation) or exactly 8 (two
  concurrent side-conversations possible at a rectangle).
- Stand-up: invite 1.5-2× the number you want attending; pods of 3-6.
- Introductions per host-hour: ~8-12 in hour one, then taper.
- A guest's first 10 minutes predicts their night — front-load anchors.
