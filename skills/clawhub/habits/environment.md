# Environment and Friction

Changing the room instead of the resolve. Every unit of friction removed is a unit of willpower that is never needed again, and friction changes keep working on the days motivation does not.

**Before proposing an environment change**, read `## What Works` in `~/Clawic/data/habits/memory.md` — friction changes are physical and slightly disruptive, and re-proposing one the user rejected costs credibility.

## The Friction Ledger

For any habit that is failing at the start rather than in the middle, count the steps between the cue firing and the behavior beginning. Then remove one.

| Steps from cue to start | Expected reliability | Typical example |
|---|---|---|
| 0 — the thing is in hand | Very high | Book on the pillow; guitar on a stand in the room |
| 1 — one reach or one door | High | Shoes by the front door; dumbbells under the desk |
| 2-3 — fetch, change, set up | Fragile; fails on bad days | Gym kit in a drawer, gym 15 min away |
| 4+ — travel, booking, coordination | Fails except when motivated | Class that must be booked the night before |

The rule that follows: **for every step you cannot remove, move it out of the cue moment.** Packing the bag the night before does not reduce the total work; it moves three steps from the fragile morning to the reliable evening.

For an avoid-habit the ledger runs backwards: add steps between the trigger and the unwanted behavior (`quitting.md`).

## The 20-Second Rule

A useful working threshold: a behavior that takes about twenty seconds longer to start is markedly less likely to happen, and one that takes twenty seconds less is markedly more likely. The number is a heuristic, not a measurement — its value is that it is small enough to be believable, which is the point. Users overestimate how much friction it takes to stop them.

Applications, both directions:

| Direction | Move |
|---|---|
| Reduce for a wanted habit | Prepare the setup in advance; keep the object in the room where the cue fires; leave the file open; keep the equipment assembled |
| Increase for an unwanted one | Out of the room, out of the building, logged out, unassembled, requiring a trip |

## Cue Placement

The cue and the object should occupy the same place. A cue in one room and the object in another is a two-step habit pretending to be a one-step habit.

- **Put the object in the path of the cue**, not in its proper storage location. A yoga mat unrolled in the living room is ugly and works; a rolled mat in the closet is tidy and does not.
- **One place per habit.** A habit that happens in a different room each day never becomes automatic, because location is half of the retrieval trigger (Rule 3).
- **Visible beats remembered.** A visible object is a standing cue; a hidden one requires the memory the habit is meant to replace.
- **Remove competing objects from the same place.** A phone next to the book is not a neutral object; it is a competing cue with a much stronger reward schedule.
- When the object cannot live in the open (shared space, small flat), the fallback is a container that opens in one move and lives in the room — not a tidy solution in another room.

## Defaults and Pre-Commitment

Anything that must be decided at the moment of action will sometimes be decided the other way. Move the decision earlier, once.

| Decision | Moved to |
|---|---|
| What to train today | A written week template; the day's session is read, not chosen |
| What to eat after training | Bought and in the fridge before the week starts |
| Whether to go this morning | Decided last night when the bag was packed |
| Which route to run | One default route; alternatives are a good-day option |
| When to stop | A timer set at the start, so the end is not a judgment call |

Pre-commitment is strongest when it is physical (the bag is packed) and weakest when it is verbal (the intention was stated). A stated intention with no physical residue is not a pre-commitment.

## The Preparation Habit

When the friction is irreducible, the preparation becomes its own habit with its own cue and its own log column. This is the legitimate case for two rows serving one outcome.

- Preparation habits are easy — they are short, indoors, and unaffected by weather or energy — so they hit high rates quickly and drag the main habit up with them.
- Log both. A month where preparation is 95% and the main habit is 55% is a precise diagnosis: the obstacle is not preparation.
- Do not create a preparation habit for a habit that already runs above 80%. That is scaffolding on a working structure and costs a slot (Rule 7).

## Digital Environments

Screens are the one environment where friction can be changed in seconds and where defaults are engineered against the user.

| Move | Effect |
|---|---|
| Remove the app from the home screen; keep it installed | Reintroduces a search step, which is enough for habitual opens and not for intentional ones |
| Log out of the account | Password entry is a decision point, and it lands before the scroll starts |
| Grayscale display | Reduces pull for image-driven apps; cheap to try, easy to revert |
| Notifications off, except people | Removes the externally-generated cue entirely, which is the largest single change available |
| Charge the phone outside the bedroom | Fixes the first and last thirty minutes of the day, where both morning and evening habits live |
| A separate browser profile or device for the wanted work | Makes the work context free of the leisure cues |
| Blockers and screen-time limits | Work while they are not trivially bypassed; expect a bypass within two weeks and treat them as a supplement to physical friction, not a substitute |

## Shared Spaces

Environment changes affect other people, and a change that annoys a housemate gets reverted.

- **Negotiate the smallest sufficient version.** A shelf rather than a room, one drawer rather than a rule about the kitchen.
- **Never make someone else's habit the constraint.** "My partner buys the snacks" is a supply problem with a conversation attached, not a willpower problem — but the conversation is the user's to have, and the plan should work at a reduced level if it does not happen.
- **Household defaults are the highest-leverage change available** and the hardest to make: what is bought, where it is kept, what the evening routine is. Propose one, once.
- **A child or a housemate is also a cue.** Habits that used to fire alone often fail in company; the fix is a version that works in company, not a request for solitude.

## Temptation Bundling

Pairing something wanted with something avoided — the audiobook only during the walk, the show only on the exercise bike. Milkman's gym field work found bundling increased attendance, with the effect fading as the arrangement loosened.

- The bundle only works while the restriction holds. As soon as the audiobook is also available on the sofa, the bundle is over.
- Best used on habits whose obstacle is boredom, not effort. It does nothing for a habit that is skipped because it is hard or because there is no time.
- Do not bundle with the thing being quit (`quitting.md`), and do not bundle something the user already enjoys freely — that is the overjustification risk (SKILL.md, Where Experts Disagree).

## When Environment Is Not the Answer

Environment fixes start-failures. It does nothing for:

- A habit that starts reliably and is abandoned midway — that is a floor or a Why problem (`troubleshooting.md`).
- A habit missing because there is genuinely no time in the day — that is a capacity or scheduling problem (`capacity.md`, `productivity`).
- A whole roster collapsing at once (`disruptions.md`).
- A habit the user does not want. No amount of friction reduction makes someone read a book they dislike.

**When an environment change is made**, write it in the same turn: one line in `## What Works` of `memory.md` naming the change, its date, and — after two weeks — whether the rate moved. This is the section that stops the same shelf being proposed twice and makes the third habit easier to design than the first (`memory-template.md`).
