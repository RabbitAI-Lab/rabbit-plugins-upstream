# A Habit That Is Not Sticking

The diagnostic ladder for a habit that has been mediocre for weeks. For a streak that broke yesterday, go to `relapse.md`; for everything collapsing at once, `disruptions.md` or `capacity.md`.

**Before diagnosing**, read the last 28 days in `~/Clawic/data/habits/logs/`, the habit's roster row in `memory.md` (the six fields are where most faults are visible), and `## Patterns` for anything already established about this person.

## Read the Shape First

The distribution of misses names the fault before any question is asked. Look at the grid, not at the percentage.

| Shape in the log | Fault class |
|---|---|
| Misses scattered evenly | Usually none — if the rate is ≥80%, this is a working habit (Rule 5) |
| Misses clustered on one or two weekdays | Schedule collision |
| Everything fine, then a cliff on a date | Context change; find what happened that week |
| Sawtooth: good week, bad week, good week | Floor set at the good-week level |
| Front-loaded: strong early in the week, empty later | Depletion or a back-loaded week |
| Complete for 2-3 weeks, then nothing | Novelty exhaustion — the classic week-3 death (`starting.md`) |
| Long blank runs, not `n` | Not a habit fault; the logging stopped, which is its own problem |
| Misses on the same days across several habits | Not this habit's fault (`capacity.md`) |

## The Ladder

Work down. Stop at the first fault found and fix only that.

1. **Is there data?** Under 14 days, or mostly blanks — there is nothing to diagnose. Fix logging first (`tracking.md`).
2. **Is the anchor firing?** Not "did they do the habit" but "did the cue event happen". An anchor that only occurs four days a week cannot support a daily habit.
3. **Is the floor the worst-day version?** Read the roster row aloud. Most floors have quietly become targets.
4. **Is the frequency achievable in this life?** Count the scheduled days against what the week actually contains.
5. **Is there a competing habit or commitment in the same slot?** Two habits at the same anchor is one habit and one intention.
6. **Does the Why still hold?** A habit whose reason expired is being maintained out of obligation, and obligation loses to everything.
7. **Is the environment fighting it?** If the answer to 2-6 is fine, the friction is physical (`environment.md`).
8. **None of the above** — capacity, not design (`capacity.md`).

## Cue Faults

The single most common fault, and the one users misdiagnose as discipline.

| Fault | Sign in the log or conversation | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No cue at all — the habit runs on memory | "I just forget"; the completion time varies widely | Anchor to an existing action with a place (Rule 3) |
| The anchor is unreliable | Misses match the days the anchor did not happen | Pick a different anchor, or define a second cue for the other context |
| The gap between cue and behavior is too long | "I meant to after breakfast, then the day started" | The behavior must begin at the anchor, not later that day |
| The cue is a clock alarm | Missed after ~2 weeks, when the alarm becomes background | Attach to an event; the alarm can stay as a backstop, never as the cue |
| The cue is another new habit | Both fail together | Anchor to something at least three months old (`routines.md`) |
| The cue exists but the environment blocks it | Cue fires, behavior does not start | Environment, not cue (`environment.md`) |

A reminder is never the fix for a cue fault. It patches the memory symptom and leaves the habit dependent on a notification, which habituates.

## Floor Faults

| Fault | Sign | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Floor is the ambition | Sawtooth pattern; done on good days only | Halve it, and say you are halving it so the user does not silently keep the old number |
| Floor crept up | Rate dropped a band with no other change; the user "may as well do the full thing" | Reset to the written floor and log the excess as a note, not as the requirement |
| Floor requires preparation | Missed on days that are otherwise normal | Move the preparation to a different day, or redefine the floor as the preparation |
| Floor is fine, the user disbelieves it | "One page isn't worth logging" | The floor is the insurance policy, not the plan; state the rate as the evidence |

## Schedule Faults

- **Wrong day of week.** Misses cluster. Fix: a smaller variant for that day rather than moving the habit — a 5-minute version on the hard day preserves the chain and the rate.
- **Wrong time of day.** Evening habits fail at a higher rate than morning ones for most people because the evening carries the day's overruns. Before concluding it, check the log: if the misses are all evening habits, move one and observe two weeks.
- **`N×/week` clustered at the end of the window.** The user leaves it to Friday-Sunday and the weekend eats it. Fix: name the days, converting a flexible frequency into a fixed one.
- **Frequency simply too high.** Four sessions demanded, three consistently achieved for eight weeks. The habit is a `3×/week` habit and always was. Lower the frequency to what the log shows and the rate jumps a band with zero behavior change — the number was wrong, not the person.

## Why Faults

Symptoms: the habit is done and resented; the user cannot restate why without reading it; the Why belongs to someone else ("my doctor said", "my partner wants").

- Re-derive it once with the six-month question (`design.md`). A recovered Why usually changes the habit's form — the walk stays, the gym goes.
- If the Why has genuinely expired, retire the habit. A habit maintained past its reason consumes a slot and teaches the user that the system is bureaucracy.
- If the Why is external and the user does not hold it, no design fixes that. Say so plainly rather than optimising a habit nobody wants.

## Competition Between Habits

Two habits at the same anchor, or in the same 30-minute window, are competing. The log shows it as an anticorrelation: the days one is done are the days the other is not.

Check for it whenever two habits both sit in the 50-79% band. Fix by separating the anchors, not by asking for both — and if no separate anchor exists, one of them is not currently possible (Rule 7).

The same pattern appears between a habit and a fixed commitment that is not in the roster at all: standing meetings, school runs, shift patterns. Ask what is in that slot on the missed days before redesigning anything.

## One Change at a Time

The whole file reduces to this at the end. Change one variable, keep everything else fixed, observe 14 days, then read the rate again.

- Two changes in one turn make the next diagnosis impossible: the rate moved, and nobody knows which change did it.
- The order of preference when several faults are plausible: cue → floor → frequency → environment → Why. Cue first because it is the cheapest to change and the most often wrong.
- Write the change and its date in the roster row's note, so the review can attribute the movement (`review.md`).
- If two consecutive single changes produce no movement, the habit is not the problem — check capacity (`capacity.md`) or retire it.

## When the Answer Is Retirement

Retirement is a correct outcome, not a failure, and saying so early saves months.

Retire when: the Why has expired; three honest redesigns have not moved the rate out of <50%; the habit was inherited from someone else's system; or the slot is worth more to a habit the user actually wants (Rule 7).

Move the row to `## Retired` with the final 28-day rate, the date, and one line on what was tried — that line is what stops the same habit being proposed again next quarter (`review.md`).

**After any diagnosis**, write in the same turn: the fault class and the single change in the roster row's note with its date; the condition in `## Patterns` of `memory.md` once it has been observed twice on this person; and the row moved to `## Retired` if the outcome was retirement (`memory-template.md`). A diagnosis that is not written is one that gets rediscovered next month.
