# Editorial workflow

This workflow adapts The Pudding's public pitch, process, and AI-story retrospectives into practical gates.

## 1. Find the story, not the topic

A topic is “AI development.” A story is a defensible statement about AI development that changes how a reader sees it.

Write:

- **Question:** What are we trying to understand?
- **Thesis:** What does the evidence currently suggest?
- **Tension:** What reasonable expectation does the evidence complicate?
- **Human opening:** Which specific case makes the tension visible?
- **Visual reason:** What relationship cannot be understood as well in prose?

The thesis may change after analysis. The production schedule may not force the data to answer a question it cannot answer.

## 2. Test the “soul”

The Pudding asks whether a story has a point of view and feels meaningful. Use these tests:

- Could the pitch apply to almost any dataset by replacing the nouns? If yes, it is generic.
- Does the idea emerge from curiosity, lived experience, domain knowledge, or a concrete anomaly?
- Is there a reader transformation beyond “learn some facts”?
- Can the author explain why this story matters now without relying on hype?
- Is the story respectful of the people represented by the data?

## 3. Build the evidence receipts

For each proposed scene:

1. Write the audience-facing claim.
2. Name the exact rows, fields, and time range supporting it.
3. Record any calculation and denominator.
4. Note uncertainty, missingness, category changes, and coverage limits.
5. Decide whether the caveat changes the claim, the visual, or both.

No source means no scene. No denominator means no percentage.

## 4. Open with a concrete case

The Pudding's AI retrospective identified a strong pattern: begin with a specific example, then pull back to the dataset.

A useful opening:

- is understandable before the methodology;
- contains the central tension in miniature;
- creates a visual object that can persist into the wider view;
- avoids pretending that one example proves the whole claim.

## 5. Draft a reader-facing spine

Write only the sentences that a reader needs. A reliable draft sequence:

1. Here is one thing.
2. Something about it does not behave as expected.
3. It is not an isolated case.
4. The wider pattern looks like this.
5. This mechanism helps explain it.
6. Here is what changes when you test another case or assumption.
7. Here is why the pattern matters.
8. Here is what the evidence cannot tell us.

Do not narrate the production process unless the process is itself the subject.

## 6. Storyboard the visual states

For each beat, draw or describe the exact marks on screen. Record:

- marks and encodings;
- annotations;
- active data subset;
- camera or scale changes;
- reader input;
- prior and next states;
- mobile fallback;
- reduced-motion behavior.

If a visual state cannot be sketched, it is not ready to code.

## 7. Separate methods from momentum

Keep the main narrative readable while making methods easy to inspect:

- concise source note near the first relevant visual;
- expandable or linked methods;
- downloadable data/code when rights allow;
- clear limitations;
- reproducible transformation notes.

## 8. Iterate on the reveal

The Pudding's AI experiment concluded that iteration distinguishes a merely competent result from a strong one. Review at least three times:

- **Editorial pass:** Is the thesis specific and supported?
- **Visual pass:** Does the reader see the claim before reading an explanation?
- **Experience pass:** Does the interaction feel inevitable, or attached after the fact?
