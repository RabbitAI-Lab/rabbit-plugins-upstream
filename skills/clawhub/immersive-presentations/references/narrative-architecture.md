# Narrative Architecture

Immersive presentations are structured as guided experiences. They need a story engine before they need animation.

## Core Questions

Before designing scenes, answer:

- Who is the audience?
- What do they believe before the presentation?
- What should they understand, feel, or decide afterward?
- What is the central tension?
- What object, system, or metaphor can carry the story visually?
- What should be demonstrated rather than explained?

## Narrative Contract

Every presentation needs a promise:

```text
By the end, the audience will see/understand/decide ______ because the experience will reveal ______.
```

Examples:

- By the end, executives will know when to use RAG, agents, or both because the experience will show how information retrieval becomes action orchestration.
- By the end, students will understand photosynthesis because the experience will let them move energy through a living system.
- By the end, operators will understand supply-chain risk because the experience will make disruptions propagate across a map.

## Act Structure

Use acts to create momentum. Do not announce acts as static section dividers unless it helps presentation flow.

### Act 1: Orientation

Make the audience care. Establish context, stakes, and the unresolved question. Use a visual hook immediately.

Typical scene roles:

- World state.
- Problem pressure.
- Tension or contradiction.
- Baseline mental model.

### Act 2: Transformation

Teach by changing the system in front of the audience. Concepts should enter as visible forces, constraints, flows, or agents.

Typical scene roles:

- Mechanism reveal.
- Comparison.
- Failure mode.
- Tradeoff.
- Interactive exploration.

### Act 3: Synthesis

Resolve the tension and give the audience a usable model. Show the decision framework, future state, or action path.

Typical scene roles:

- Integration.
- Decision map.
- Recommendation.
- Final memory image.

## Scene Beats

Each scene should contain a beat:

```text
setup -> reveal -> transformation -> implication
```

Not every beat must be long. A fast scene can be a single visual punchline. A complex scene may need interaction and presenter narration.

## Pacing

Design the experience for live delivery:

- 15-30 seconds: visual punctuation or transition scene.
- 45-90 seconds: single concept scene.
- 2-3 minutes: interactive or complex mechanism scene.
- 5+ minutes: usually too long unless it is a live demo or exploratory canvas.

Alternate density:

- Dense mechanism scene.
- Breath scene.
- Comparison.
- Interaction.
- Synthesis.

## Continuity

Do not reset the world on every scene. A presentation feels cinematic when objects persist and transform.

Continuity tools:

- A document cloud becomes a vector field, then a retrieval beam, then an agent workspace.
- A city map becomes a climate-risk heatmap, then a budget allocation map.
- A molecule becomes a process diagram, then a data trace.
- A customer journey line becomes a funnel, then a system architecture.

## Presenter Narrative

Presenter notes should say what the speaker should do, not restate on-screen text.

Good notes:

```text
Ask the audience where they think the bottleneck is before revealing the retrieval layer.
```

Weak notes:

```text
This slide explains the retrieval layer.
```
