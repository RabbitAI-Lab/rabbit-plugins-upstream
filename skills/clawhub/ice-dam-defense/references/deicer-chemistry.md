# De-Icer Chemistry Reference

## Why "Salt Stops Working" Below Certain Temperatures

A de-icer works by dissolving into brine; brine freezes at a lower temperature than pure water
(freezing-point depression). Two numbers matter:

- **Eutectic temperature** — the theoretical lowest freezing point of the brine (NaCl: −21 °C).
- **Practical effective floor** — below this, dissolution is too slow to matter before traffic/
  sun/refreeze change the situation. For NaCl that's about **−9 °C pavement**; lab eutectic is
  irrelevant to a sidewalk.

Always reason from PAVEMENT temperature, which at night runs several degrees colder than air
(radiative sky cooling) and by afternoon can run warmer than air (sun).

## The Chemicals

| Product | Active | Eutectic | Practical floor | Heat on dissolving | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rock salt / halite | NaCl | −21 °C | −9 °C | slight absorb | cheap, ubiquitous, slow when cold |
| Magnesium chloride | MgCl₂ | −33 °C | −15 °C | exothermic | flakes/pellets, less plant damage |
| Calcium chloride | CaCl₂ | −30 °C (30% soln) | −25 °C | strongly exothermic | fastest; attracts moisture, gets slick/slimy; harms concrete via rebar corrosion less than NaCl but still chloride |
| Blends (NaCl+CaCl₂, pre-wetted) | mixed | ~−31 °C | ~−18 °C | mixed | CaCl₂ "kick-starts", NaCl carries bulk |
| Potassium acetate | KAc | −60 °C | −25 °C | — | chloride-free, airport standard, 5-10× cost |
| Calcium magnesium acetate | CMA | −27 °C (lab) | −9 °C | — | chloride-free, slow, concrete-friendly, needs higher rate |
| Sand / cinders / ash | inert | — | any | — | traction only; sweeps into drains |

## Application Rates (per 1000 sq ft; script converts to kg per your area)

| Condition | Rate |
|---|---|
| Anti-icing pre-treat (brine) | ~0.5-1 gal brine (≈ 0.3-0.8 lb salt equivalent) |
| Light ice / frost | 8-15 lb granular |
| Compact snow / black ice | 25-45 lb granular |

Coffee-mug calibration: a 350 g mug of granular salt covers ≈ 10 m² at light rate. The
kitchen-glass "just throw some" technique typically triples the needed rate.

## Brine Anti-Icing (the pro move)

- 23% NaCl brine: ~1 kg salt per 4 L water (dissolves fully around 15-20 °C water).
- Spray the evening BEFORE the storm; it prevents the ice-pavement bond so a shovel/plow
  clears to bare pavement.
- Uses ~⅓ the chloride of post-storm granular — cheaper and much better for waterways.
- MgCl₂/CaCl₂ brines work colder and residual-stick to pavement (hygroscopic), giving some
  carry-over protection between storms.

## Damage Profiles

- **Concrete:** chlorides don't chemically attack concrete directly — they increase
  freeze-thaw cycles in saturated surface pores and corrode rebar in reinforced slabs.
  Concrete < 1 year old (still curing, high porosity) should get NO chlorides: use CMA or sand.
  Sealed, cured concrete tolerates moderate chloride use.
- **Pets:** NaCl stings cracked paws and GI-upsets when licked off. CaCl₂'s exothermic
  dissolution can burn wet skin. Wipe paws after walks; booties for long exposure; "pet-safe"
  products are usually MgCl₂ or urea/blends — safer, not risk-free.
- **Plants/soil:** Na⁺ destroys soil structure and accumulates for years (roadside grass die-off
    is sodium, not cold). Ca/Mg salts are plant-nutrient cations and far gentler. Flush planting
  beds near treated pavement in spring if salt heavy.
- **Waterways/metals:** chloride runs off to streams (no removal process) and attacks
  galvanized/steel (guardrails, bike frames, door thresholds). Track bikes indoors and rinse.

## What NOT to Use

- **Hot water** on a walkway: melts, then flash-refreezes into mirror ice (and thermal-shocks
  concrete).
- **Fertilizers (urea, KCl, ammonium nitrate)** as de-icers: marginal melt, serious runoff
  algae problems; ammonium nitrate also attacks concrete.
- **Homemade "safe" mixes** of salt+vinegar+soap: the soap does nothing; you've made brine with
  extra steps and suds in the drain.
