# Edge Geometry & Sharpening Reference

## Why angles differ by knife class

Edge angle is a trade between sharpness (thin wedge, less force, cleaner
cut) and stability (thicker wedge, more metal supporting the apex). The
right choice depends on what the blade hits:

| Class | Angle/side | Why |
|-------|-----------|-----|
| Japanese double-bevel (gyuto, santoku, nakiri) | 12–15° | hard steel (60+ HRC) holds a thin apex; push-cutting vegetables |
| German/Western chef's | 17–22° (20 typical) | softer steel must be thicker not to fold; rocking chopping, bones-adjacent work |
| Paring/petty | 15–17° | fine control work on a small blade |
| Boning/fillet | 18–22° | lateral flex would fold a thin edge |
| Deba | 20–25°, mostly one-sided | hits fish bones |
| Cleaver | 25–35° | pure chopping shock |
| Pocket/EDC | 20–25° | cutting unknown materials on hard surfaces |

## The pass-height math

Hand-sharpening "20 degrees" is hard because 20° is just a slope. The
physical fact you CAN hold: spine height above the stone.

    height = blade_width × sin(angle)

45 mm German blade at 20° → 15.4 mm (about two thumb widths). A 40 mm
santoku at 13° → 9.0 mm. The Sharpie/marker trick calibrates you in two
passes: color the bevel, take two strokes; ink removed at the shoulder =
too steep, ink left at the apex = too shallow.

## Grit numbers: what they actually do

Grit = abrasive particle size (JIS for Japanese stones). Coarse removes
steel fast but leaves deep scratches; each successive stage exists to
remove the previous stage's scratches, and the edge is only as good as the
last fully-removed scratch pattern.

- 220–400: reprofile, chip repair, tip rebuilds
- 400–800: establishing the bevel on a dull knife
- 800–1200: the working zone where the edge is truly formed
- 1200–3000: refinement; a 1000→3000 finish is the kitchen sweet spot
- 3000–6000: polish, push-cut refinement, micro-bevels
- 6000–8000+: mirror polish; diminishing returns for food, loved by
  sharpening hobbyists (and valid for straight razors)

Jump rule: ≤ ~3× between stages (1000 → 3000 fine; 400 → 3000 wastes time
and stone).

## The burr: proof an edge exists

A burr is the thin metal fold that forms on the OPPOSITE face when you've
abraded one face all the way to the apex. Feeling it (thumb stroked AWAY
from the edge, never into it) along the entire length is the only
beginner-verifiable signal that the bevels meet. No burr = the two bevel
planes don't intersect yet = not sharp, no matter how it looks.

Sequence: burr on B (from A passes) → mirror passes on A (from B) →
alternating lightest passes to shed the burr → strop. A wire edge (burr
never removed) is the classic "sharp for one day" failure.

## Steel decoder — behavior at the stone

| Steel | Hardness | Stone behavior |
|-------|----------|----------------|
| X50CrMoV15 (Wüsthof/Henckels class) | 54–56 HRC | fast burr formation, forgiving, dulls quicker |
| VG-10 | ~60 HRC | small brittle burrs — lighten pressure or micro-chip the apex |
| SG2/R2 powder | 62–64 HRC | very abrasion resistant; diamond/CBN saves time |
| Aogami/Shirogami carbon | 61–63 HRC | fast, takes the finest edges, rusts instantly — dry it |
| S35VN class | 58–61 HRC | slow; diamond plates; stable tiny burr |
| 1095 | 56–58 HRC | the learner steel: cheap, easy, honest feedback |

Hardness rule of thumb: above ~60 HRC, thin low angles (12–15°) hold;
below ~56 HRC, give the apex 18–22° or it folds on the first hard item.

## Maintenance doctrine

- **Honing rod**: realigns a rolled edge; removes nothing. Postpones stone
  sessions; cannot rescue a dull edge.
- **Stropping** (leather/cardboard, optional 1–4 µm compound): cleans the
  apex micro-serrations. Weekly stropping extends stone cycles 5–10×.
- **Pull-through sharpeners**: material-scraping V-grooves. Acceptable for
  soft-steel beaters; destructive on hard Japanese steel (carbide tear-out)
  and they can't hold a consistent angle.
- **Dishwasher**: chemistry + basket rattling = the fastest edge-killer in
  a home. Hand-wash, dry immediately (also prevents carbon-steel rust).

## Session checklist

1. Stone flattened (a dished stone rounds your bevel).
2. Angle chosen by class; Sharpie-calibrated.
3. Burr full length side A → mirror side B → shed burr → strop.
4. Paper-slice test; tomato-skin test for kitchen knives.
5. Carbon steel: wiped dry + oiled if stored.

## Safety

Sharpening is safe; testing is where blood happens. Never test with a
lateral thumb draw along the edge. Paper slice away from body, or the
tomato test. If you slip at the stone, the knife moves away from your
hand, not toward it — build that habit from pass one.
