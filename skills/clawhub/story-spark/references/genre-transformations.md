# Genre Transformations: Detailed Lens Breakdown

Each genre lens in Story Spark transforms a real moment into a fiction prompt using specific narrative techniques. This document explains the patterns behind each transformation.

## Literary Fiction

### Core Question
*What is the internal conflict?*

### Transformation Pattern
Literary fiction takes the emotional weight of a moment and makes it the story's engine. The external plot is minimal; the internal landscape is everything.

**Input**: A photo of a half-eaten meal at a restaurant, taken alone.
**Transformation**:
- **Premise**: A person eats alone at a restaurant they used to visit with someone now gone.
- **Character**: Someone processing loss through mundane ritual.
- **Conflict**: The waiter asks, "Just one tonight?" — a question that breaks through careful composure.
- **Twist**: The person isn't grieving a death — they're grieving their own decision to leave.

### Key Techniques
- Focus on sensory detail (the taste of food, the quality of light)
- Subtext over explicit statement
- Character revelation through small actions
- Quiet but devastating endings

## Mystery / Thriller

### Core Question
*What is hidden?*

### Transformation Pattern
Mystery introduces a secret, a discrepancy, or an unanswered question into the moment. The familiar becomes suspicious.

**Input**: A photo of a quiet suburban street at dusk.
**Transformation**:
- **Premise**: A new neighbor has moved in, but their house has no furniture, no mail, no signs of life except a light that turns on exactly at 11 PM every night.
- **Character**: A bored retiree who notices patterns.
- **Conflict**: Nobody believes them — until another neighbor disappears.
- **Twist**: The house isn't occupied. It's being watched *from* somewhere else.

### Key Techniques
- Withhold information strategically
- Create suspicion through incongruous detail
- Build tension through repetition of small anomalies
- The reveal recontextualizes everything

## Science Fiction

### Core Question
*What if this were different?*

### Transformation Pattern
Sci-Fi introduces a speculative element — technology, time shift, alternate reality — that changes one fundamental rule of the moment.

**Input**: A photo of a crowded subway car.
**Transformation**:
- **Premise**: In a near-future city, the subway doesn't just transport bodies — it transports memories. Each passenger carries one memory they've agreed to swap by the end of the ride.
- **Character**: A passenger carrying a memory they don't want to lose but must exchange to reach their destination.
- **Conflict**: The memory they receive in trade is their own — from someone they've never met.
- **Twist**: The city's memory transit system isn't for convenience. It's how the government erases dissent.

### Key Techniques
- Change one rule and explore consequences
- Use familiar settings as anchors for speculative elements
- The technology should reveal something human
- Ground speculation in sensory detail

## Horror

### Core Question
*What is wrong here?*

### Transformation Pattern
Horror finds the uncanny in the mundane — something is not as it should be, and the wrongness grows.

**Input**: A photo of a child's bedroom, tidy, with a nightlight.
**Transformation**:
- **Premise**: Every morning, the child's toys are arranged differently than the night before. The parents assume the child is playing. Then they set up a camera.
- **Character**: A parent who needs to know the truth.
- **Conflict**: The footage shows the toys moving on their own — but only when no one is in the room.
- **Twist**: The camera also shows something standing behind the child's bed, just out of frame. It's been there every night. It waves.

### Key Techniques
- Build dread through escalation of small wrongness
- The unseen is scarier than the seen
- Violate safety (home, bedroom, daylight)
- End on the moment of realization, not the aftermath

## Romance

### Core Question
*Who could meet here?*

### Transformation Pattern
Romance introduces a potential connection — an encounter that could change two lives.

**Input**: A photo of a bookstore cafe.
**Transformation**:
- **Premise**: Two strangers reach for the same used book and discover notes in the margins — left by different previous owners, having a conversation through annotations spanning years.
- **Character**: Someone lonely who finds connection through the unexpected.
- **Conflict**: They disagree about what to do with the book — one wants to find the annotators, the other wants to preserve the mystery.
- **Twist**: They realize they've each written in the margins of different copies of the same book years ago. They've been part of the same conversation all along.

### Key Techniques
- Meet-cute with substance (not just bumping into each other)
- Obstacles that reveal character
- Emotional stakes beyond "will they get together"
- The romance should change both people

## Historical Fiction

### Core Question
*What if this were another era?*

### Transformation Pattern
Historical fiction takes the moment and shifts it to a different time period, using the same emotional core but period-appropriate detail.

**Input**: A photo of a farmer's market.
**Transformation**:
- **Premise**: Same market square, but in 1348 during the Black Death. A merchant continues to sell spices while the town dies around him.
- **Character**: A spice merchant who believes his wares protect him — or knows they don't.
- **Conflict**: A doctor asks him to stop selling. People are crowding the market and spreading the plague. But the merchant's family will starve.
- **Twist**: The merchant has been selling burial spices — not cooking spices — and knew the plague was coming months ago.

### Key Techniques
- Research period detail (clothing, speech, customs, technology)
- Use historical constraints to heighten conflict
- Show how universal emotions play out in specific eras
- The historical setting should feel like a character
