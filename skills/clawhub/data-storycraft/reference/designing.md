# Designing

## Affordances

Design so the correct reading is the obvious one.

- Bold is the cleanest emphasis; italics are weaker and underline adds noise. Uppercase works for short titles and labels. Do not switch fonts to highlight. Color works best combined with bold; use inversion and size sparingly, and never blink.
- Drop noncritical data and summarize when detail is not needed. If removing an element would change nothing, remove it, however much work went into it. Push necessary but non-message items to light grey, and move possibly-needed detail to an appendix.
- Super-categories give structure. Group twenty demographic rows under age, income and region, or label scatterplot quadrants ("High satisfaction, many issues") so readers skip decoding the axes.

## Accessibility

When readers cannot understand a chart, the design failed, not the reader. Hard-to-read looks hard-to-do (Song and Schwarz, 2008), so complicated-looking visuals get skipped. Use one legible font and plain, few words; define jargon and spell out acronyms on first use.

Leaving an axis untitled can be a deliberate exception, for example to keep a contested metric from derailing a chart where only the relative peaks matter.

## Aesthetics

Attractive designs are perceived as easier to use and earn more patience; visible care invites the audience to invest time. Keep a collection of good examples and borrow what works.

## Gaining acceptance

When stakeholders resist a new format, explain what the new view reveals, show old and new side by side, offer a few options, and win over one influential audience member first. If resistance persists, ask someone with no stake to think aloud through both versions, because the design itself may be the problem.
