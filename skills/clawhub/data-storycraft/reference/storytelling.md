# Storytelling

## Why story

Bullets persuade only intellectually, while the audience argues back in their heads. A story joins an idea to an emotion, so people remember it and retell it. A story shows how and why things change: balance, a disruption, struggle, resolution. Tension holds attention. To test a story, borrow screenwriter Robert McKee's questions: what does the protagonist want, what stands in the way, and is the telling honest, neither exaggerated nor softened?

## Three acts

| Act | Content |
|---|---|
| Beginning | The setting; the audience as the main character; the imbalance (what changed and why it matters); the desired balance; the proposed solution |
| Middle | Background, external comparisons, examples, data that shows the problem, the cost of inaction, the options considered, the benefits of the recommendation, and why this audience is the one that can act. Frame it around what motivates them: money, competition, market share, efficiency. |
| End | The exact ask, tied back to the opening tension |

When there is no obvious problem, frame the tension between what is and what could be (Duarte). A purely rosy update moves no one.

Write the headlines first, reorder them until the flow is logical, then use them as the slide titles.

## Delivery

- Whichever order you choose, announce it at the start ("I'll start with what we're asking of you, then show how we got there").
- In a live talk, the voice carries each so-what and the slides only reinforce it; slides are never a teleprompter. Set expectations for questions with audiences that tend to derail.
- In a sent document, each page carries its own so-what, and the structure lets readers jump to what interests them.

## Clarity tactics

| Tactic | Check |
|---|---|
| Horizontal logic | Read in sequence, the slide titles tell the whole story |
| Vertical logic | Everything on a slide supports its title; anything unrelated moves to an appendix |
| Reverse storyboarding | A list of each finished page's main point matches the intended storyboard |
| Fresh perspective | Someone without context says what they notice, what seems important and what they would ask |

Repetition helps the message stick: summary, body, recap (the book calls it "Bing, Bang, Bongo").

## Writing style

The book borrows Kurt Vonnegut's advice, paraphrased here: care about the subject, keep it simple, cut sentences that add nothing, sound like yourself, and be patient with the reader.
