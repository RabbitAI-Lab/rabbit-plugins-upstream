# Measurement Traps and Methodology Index

> Self-check material to use before a report is released. The **trap catalogue** is for checking, one entry at a
> time, whether a number in the report has fallen into a known measurement trap. The **methodology index** is for
> choosing the right analytical tool and for naming the framework you used in the report.
>
> Verify these as well: the methodology entries are public industry frameworks. When you cite one, check the
> original source and the conditions under which it applies.

---

## Part 1. Measurement trap catalogue

Entries are grouped by analysis layer. **When a draft is finished, compare it against this catalogue entry by
entry.** For every entry that applies, state the trap's effect explicitly under "Known limitations" or in the
basis-note box.

**IDs.** Macro M-01 to M-29 · industry chain L3-01 to L3-14 · market L4-01 to L4-12 · single stock L5-01 to L5-32 ·
general G-01 to G-02, 89 entries in all. An ID is a stable handle. Append new entries at the end of a group; never
renumber and never reuse an ID. A withdrawn entry keeps its ID and is marked "withdrawn". Cite IDs only in working
files: research notes, audit records and the audit disposition table (for example, "L5-20 checked"). **The report
body and the basis-note box carry no IDs and never name this file.** An internal file name in the body is a
blocking audit finding.

**Entry format.** ID, a bracketed tag, what the trap is, and **How to check**: the concrete action, such as which
note to read, which XBRL tag to pull or which reconciliation formula to run. Where a catalogue entry says "tag it
[C]", it uses the evidence grades [A]-[D] from SKILL.md.

### Macro layer (L0 / L1 / L2)

- **M-01** [L0] The UN World Population Prospects is a projection, not a forecast. The medium variant is only the median path of a probability distribution. The model also carries a prior that very-low-fertility countries drift back toward replacement-level fertility, so it systematically underestimates how fast East Asian fertility is falling. Compare the successive downward revisions for China and South Korea across the 2019, 2022 and 2024 editions and the direction of the bias is clear. When you use it as a long-horizon anchor, read the low variant and the country's own birth-registration record alongside it.
- **M-02** [L0] TFP is the Solow residual, not a measurable "efficiency". It is output growth minus the contribution of inputs, and it absorbs capacity-utilisation swings, changes in firm mark-ups, factor misallocation and measurement error. Reading a slowdown in TFP growth as "technological progress is slowing" is the standard misreading; it may only be falling utilisation. **How to check**: overlay capacity utilisation (Federal Reserve G.17) on the same chart, or switch to the San Francisco Fed (Fernald) utilisation-adjusted quarterly TFP series. If the TFP turn and the utilisation turn point the same way, do not conclude that technological progress is slowing.
- **M-03** [L0] Penn World Table versions cannot be spliced. Version 11.0 (released October 2025) moved to the 2021 round of ICP purchasing-power parities, revised the 2017 data, and replaced the adjusted Maddison/Wu China series with the official one. Comparing TFP levels or GDP-per-capita rankings across versions produces false conclusions. Re-run the whole span on a single version.
- **M-04** [L0] The labour income share is dominated by how self-employed "mixed income" is split. ILO, OECD and national statistics offices assign mixed income between labour and capital differently, which can move the level by 5-10 percentage points. US NIPA capitalised R&D and other IP products in 1999 and again in 2013, which mechanically raised the capital share. Comparing levels across countries is almost always wrong; compare only each country's own trend.
- **M-05** [L0] China's flow-of-funds table (physical transactions) lags by about two years, and every economic census triggers a large revision of the history. Using it to judge the current distribution of income is therefore always mistimed. The provincial and national income-approach GDP accounts have also failed to reconcile for a long time. **How to check**: state the table year and whether it is the post-census revised version. For the current distribution, use high-frequency proxies (household-survey disposable income, tax receipts, total wage bill) and use the flow-of-funds table only as a structural cross-check.
- **M-06** [L0] Governance and business-climate indices are endogenous and have structural breaks. The Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI) aggregate survey and expert perceptions, and an economic downturn itself lowers the scores, so using them to explain the composition of growth is circular. The World Bank's Doing Business report was discontinued in 2021 after data manipulation in the 2018 and 2020 editions. B-READY is the successor project, but its method and sample do not continue the old series. Never splice the two into one time series.
- **M-07** [L0] Conclusions about a country's "stage of development" are highly sensitive to the PPP benchmark round. The ICP runs a benchmark only every six years (2011, 2017, 2021). Each round, with its backward revisions, shifts every country's relative GDP-per-capita (PPP) position as a block, and has in the past rewritten the date at which China overtakes the United States in one step. Any narrative built on a PPP ranking must state the benchmark year.
- **M-08** [L1] The size of the Fed balance sheet is not market liquidity. What matters is the redistribution among bank reserves, the ON RRP facility and the Treasury General Account (TGA) on the liability side. While ON RRP was being drained during QT, reserves barely fell. After it was drained, the same pace of balance-sheet reduction pulled reserves out directly. Watching total assets alone reads the liquidity effect of these two phases backwards.
- **M-09** [L1] Treating the market-implied policy path as a forecast. Fed funds futures and OIS paths are risk-neutral prices that include term and convexity premia, and their record at turning points is poor. They are a snapshot of consensus. Their research value lies entirely in the gap between the market path and your own judgment; quoting the path as a view gives up your information edge.
- **M-10** [L1] Term premium and r* are unobservable model estimates. ACM and Kim-Wright can differ by 50-100bp at a single date, and both revise the whole history as the sample lengthens. Quoting one day's term premium as fact, or using a point estimate of r* as a valuation anchor, mistakes model output for an observation. **How to check**: show both estimates (ACM from the New York Fed; Kim-Wright from the Federal Reserve Board, FRED THREEFYTP10) with their as-of dates, and report the difference between them as a range instead of one model's point value. Do the same for r*: show the Laubach-Williams and Holston-Laubach-Williams estimates side by side.
- **M-11** [L1] The leading power of yield-curve inversion has been contaminated by QE and compressed term premia. The lag from inversion to recession varies widely between 6 and 24 months, and 2022-2024 produced the longest "inverted but no recession" window on record. Treat inversion as one condition among several, never as a timing tool. **How to check**: give the start date, depth and duration of inversion on both the 10y-3m (FRED T10Y3M) and the 10y-2y (T10Y2Y) measures, and list them with credit spreads and the unemployment trend as conditions. Never give a "recession in X months" date from the curve alone.
- **M-12** [L1] Weekly changes in H.8 bank credit are driven largely by accounting and coverage effects: mergers, non-bank entities brought onto balance sheets, and asset transfers during crises (for example the 2023 regional-bank episode). They are not genuine credit expansion or contraction. Before reading any H.8 move directionally, check the Federal Reserve's Notes on the Data and exclude coverage events.
- **M-13** [L1] Chinese credit data have clear coverage breaks. From January 2023, three kinds of non-deposit institution (consumer-finance companies, wealth-management companies and financial asset investment companies) were brought into financial statistics, and "RMB loans to the real economy" and "loan write-offs" in total social financing were adjusted accordingly. From the January 2025 data, a revised M1 definition applies (it adds personal demand deposits and the reserves of non-bank payment institutions). If you compute year-on-year growth without the official like-for-like series, the growth rate gains or loses several percentage points from nothing.
- **M-14** [L1] The BIS credit-to-GDP gap uses a one-sided HP filter (lambda = 400,000) to estimate the trend, which has a serious end-point problem. The latest gap is revised wholesale as the next few quarters arrive, and the real-time value and the final value can have opposite signs. It cannot be the sole basis for a real-time warning. **How to check**: read it together with the BIS debt service ratio (DSR) for the same country. State the retrieval date. Re-run a one-sided HP filter yourself on data up to the current period and compare how much the last several quarters' gaps were revised. If the revision is the same order of magnitude as the current gap, draw no warning conclusion.
- **M-15** [L1] A narrowing credit spread does not necessarily mean better risk appetite. It can come from a shorter index duration or a change in rating mix (a smaller CCC weight in the HY index, a changed energy share). Always read the spread against its historical percentile, the index composition and the quality spread (CCC minus BB). The quality spread usually turns before the aggregate spread.
- **M-16** [L1] Reading low volatility as safety is a Minsky-type error. VIX is a 30-day implied price for known risks and contains no unknown information. Volatility and leverage are reflexive, and the lowest-volatility periods have often been the periods of highest leverage and greatest fragility (1998, 2007, February 2018). **How to check**: never use VIX alone. Read it with the VIX term structure (VIX against VIX3M) and with leverage proxies (margin debt as a share of total market capitalisation, per M-18; the net short position of leveraged funds in VIX futures in the CFTC Commitments of Traders report). Low volatility combined with high leverage is written as "rising fragility", not "safe".
- **M-17** [L1] TIC data are attributed by custodian country, not by beneficial owner. Holdings in Belgium, Luxembourg and the Cayman Islands do not represent local money. The monthly data are also heavily revised and show large valuation effects (a change in market value caused by a rate move is misread as buying or selling). Before concluding that "a country is selling Treasuries", look through the custodian location and strip out valuation effects.
- **M-18** [L1] FINRA margin debt is in nominal dollars and is not adjusted for market value, so it rises with the equity index and "sets a new record" in every bull market. The meaningful measures are margin debt as a share of total market capitalisation, or the gap between the growth of margin debt and the growth of the index.
- **M-19** [L2] The three vintages of US GDP, plus the annual and five-yearly benchmark revisions, rewrite the historical turning points. The two negative quarters of the first half of 2022 were erased in later revisions, which is the standard warning against concluding "recession" from first prints. When GDP and GDI diverge for a long time, use their average (GDO); GDP alone over- or under-states the state of the cycle.
- **M-20** [L2] Seasonal-adjustment distortion. The extreme values of the pandemic polluted the X-13ARIMA-SEATS seasonal factors, which caused a multi-year dispute over "residual seasonality" in US first-quarter GDP and January CPI. In China, the moving Spring Festival means January and February must be read combined, and a single-month year-on-year figure is meaningless. Before calling a turning point from a single seasonally adjusted month-on-month change, test for residual seasonality.
- **M-21** [L2] CPI and PCE are not interchangeable. Their weighting systems (Consumer Expenditure Survey against national accounts), health-spending coverage (out-of-pocket against including third-party payments) and index formulas (Laspeyres against Fisher) all differ. The long-run average gap is about 0.3 percentage points, and the Fed's 2% target is anchored on PCE, not CPI. US CPI also moved from two-year weights to single-year weights from 2023, and the mechanical effect of the weight update is released in a lump when the year changes.
- **M-22** [L2] The CPI shelter component is a lagged smoothing of the stock of existing leases and has no leading content of its own. OER and primary-residence rent cover all leases, including tenants who have not moved, and lag the turn in new-lease rents by about 3-4 quarters. Using CPI rent to judge the direction of services inflation will always be a year late; use new-lease measures such as the BLS New Tenant Rent Index or the Zillow Observed Rent Index.
- **M-23** [L2] The JOLTS response rate has fallen from above 60% to around 30%, and the spread of free online postings may have systematically raised the recorded number of openings. The absolute level of the V/U ratio is therefore not comparable before and after the pandemic. Read only its direction and its percentile in its own history, and do not extrapolate old thresholds (for example, V/U = 1.2 implying wage acceleration).
- **M-24** [L2] The two employment measures diverge, and benchmark revisions are large. The household survey (CPS) and the establishment survey (CES) have long diverged, CES is affected by the birth-death model, and the 2024 annual benchmark revision cut several hundred thousand jobs in one step. The 90% confidence interval on a single month's non-farm payroll change is about plus or minus 130,000, so most single-month changes are not statistically significant. Drawing conclusions from one month is the most common over-reading.
- **M-25** [L2] Potential output and the output gap are unobservable model quantities, and the real-time estimate can differ from the final value by several percentage points. Orphanides' work on the real-time output-gap misjudgments of the 1970s shows that using the real-time estimate as a policy criterion helped cause the Great Inflation. This is the classic case of the real-time data problem. **How to check**: use the historical vintages of CBO potential output (GDPPOT) in ALFRED. Compare how the estimate of the same quarter's gap changed across vintage years, and publish that change as the error band for the current gap. If the sign of the gap can flip within the error band, do not use it as a policy criterion.
- **M-26** [L2] Diffusion indices have survivorship bias and distort magnitude. PMI and regional Fed surveys ask for "the share of firms getting better", so they measure direction, not size. Firms that fail in a recession drop out of the sample, which makes the index look better than the economy. The mapping from ISM to hard data such as industrial production has drifted visibly since the pandemic, and converting to GDP with an old regression coefficient gives a systematically high number.
- **M-27** [L2] The Sahm rule confirms; it does not warn. It identifies the fact that a rise in unemployment has already started. Its author has said publicly that when labour supply expands because of immigration and a recovering participation rate, a rise in unemployment may not reflect a demand recession, and the rule can give false positives. Using it as a leading indicator misreads how it is built. **How to check**: decompose the source of the rise in unemployment. Use BLS Employment Situation Table A-11 (unemployed persons by reason) to separate job losers from new and re-entrants, and show labour participation and immigration flows next to it. If the rise comes mainly from new and re-entrants, label it "supply-side driven, rule may be a false positive".
- **M-28** [L2] A fiscal impulse is not the size of the deficit. Automatic stabilisers mechanically widen the deficit in a recession, so reading a cyclical deficit as "policy is stimulating" gets the direction backwards. The correct method is the first difference of the structural primary balance (excluding cyclical effects and interest), with one-off items shown separately.
- **M-29** [L2] Early nowcast readings carry very little information. Models such as GDPNow have only a few hard data points early in the quarter, so they are driven mostly by the prior of the bridge regression and are extremely sensitive to a single data point. An extreme early-quarter reading, amplified by the media, often creates a false narrative. The model has real discriminating power only late in the quarter, when about two-thirds of the data have been released.

### Industry-chain layer (L3)

- **L3-01** [Three meanings of capacity] Nameplate (design) capacity, effective capacity and ramped-up capacity can differ by 20-30%. Industry associations and the OECD steel database use nominal steelmaking capacity, while poor yields during ramp-up, maintenance shutdowns and energy-driven output limits all reduce effective capacity. Computing "excess" as nominal capacity minus demand systematically overstates the excess and calls the cycle bottom too early. **How to check**: put nominal, effective and ramped-up capacity in three columns of one table, each with its own source. If only nominal capacity exists, estimate effective capacity as "nominal x the industry's historical average utilisation" and tag it [C]. Always compute "excess" on the effective basis. For data centres and power, MW also has a gross versus critical-IT split; see L5-26.
- **L3-02** [Planned capacity is not committed supply] This is the most frequent error in industry-chain research. SEMI World Fab Forecast explicitly flags the "probability of reaching volume production" for future fabs. Planned projects in EIA-860M have material cancellation and delay rates. LBNL data show that only about one in five projects entering power interconnection queues is eventually built. Weight planned capacity by probability and shift its timeline by the historical delay distribution before it goes into a model.
- **L3-03** [Capex-to-price lags cannot be carried across industries] Wafer-fab line expansion takes 12-18 months, a new fab 24-36 months plus 6-12 months of ramp. Polysilicon and lithium-salt refining take 12-18 months. An ethylene cracker takes 3-4 years and an LNG liquefaction train 4-5 years. A greenfield copper mine takes about 15-18 years counted from discovery (including exploration, feasibility and permitting; on the S&P Global basis the average for the 2020-23 start-up cohort is 17.9 years, with exploration and study taking most of it), while construction alone after the final investment decision takes about 4-7 years. State which segment you mean when you cite one (this is consistent with the capex-and-cash-flow entry in `references/indicators.md`). An assembly line takes only 3-6 months. Moving one industry's lag constant to another shifts the whole supply-demand balance by several years in time, which is the most expensive error in heavy-asset research. **How to check**: treat the figures above only as order-of-magnitude priors. In the finished report, replace them with the median measured interval of 3-5 completed projects in the industry (per Step 2 of `references/industry.md`), and state the start point (discovery, permit, FID, start of construction) and the end point (first output, ramped-up output).
- **L3-04** [Three contaminations in China's above-scale industrial data] The above-scale threshold covers only enterprises with annual main-business revenue above RMB 20 million (raised from RMB 5 million in 2011). The sample rotates each year and the base is recomputed on the new sample, so "subtract cumulative values to get the month" will not match the official year-on-year rate. January and February are published combined to avoid the moving Spring Festival. Together these three create growth jumps that do not exist. **How to check**: always use the statistics bureau's published year-on-year rate (already adjusted to a comparable sample), never your own monthly figure from subtracting cumulative values. Read January-February only as the combined value. Mark a break in any long series that crosses 2011, where the threshold changed.
- **L3-05** [Inventory in value terms diverges from inventory in physical terms] US M3 inventories are measured at current cost, and China's above-scale finished-goods inventory is also in value terms. In a period of large PPI swings, a rise in nominal inventory can be pure price revaluation, not restocking. Deflate with the PPI of the matching industry. Many "active restocking has started" calls in 2021-2022 came from not deflating.
- **L3-06** [Active restocking and passive build-up cannot be told apart from inventory growth alone] The same rise in inventory means healthy restocking if new orders are rising, but passive build-up from collapsing demand if new orders are falling. The two mean opposite things for future prices. Always read inventory together with new orders (or the inventory-to-sales ratio together with shipments). A single inventory series has no directional information.
- **L3-07** [Gross margins across stages are not comparable on the face of the numbers] Companies at different stages put freight, warehousing and depreciation in cost of sales or in selling expense inconsistently. After the new revenue standard around 2020, Chinese A-share companies moved freight from selling expense into cost of sales, and under ASC 606 US companies mostly put freight in COGS. Comparing gross margins directly across stages, or across 2020, gives a wrong picture of value distribution. The safe method is to compare the change in the gap between segments of the same company.
- **L3-08** [Survivorship bias in the listed-company basis] Extrapolating industry capacity and profit from listed companies misses small unlisted capacity, which is exactly the capacity that exits first at the bottom of a cycle. As a result the speed of capacity clearing is underestimated and the bottom is pushed out. In the other direction, IPOs and backdoor listings make the "listed-company capacity" series jump for reasons unrelated to real capacity. **How to check**: subtract the sum of listed companies from the industry total published by an association, customs or the statistics bureau to get the unlisted exposure, and give its share. Where the unlisted share is high, judge the speed of clearing from the industry total. Mark a break at each IPO or backdoor-listing year.
- **L3-09** [Member coverage and advocacy bias in industry-association data] Association statistics usually cover only member firms, and new entrants that arrive during an expansion often have not yet joined, so association capacity is systematically below actual. Associations also speak for their industry. In periods of overcapacity and "anti-involution" campaigns they have an incentive to under-report output and over-report cuts, so cross-check the wording of their weekly commentary against third-party hard data from customs and exchanges.
- **L3-10** [Customs data: code breaks and transshipment distortion] HS codes are revised every five years (HS2017, HS2022, HS2028). Sub-heading splits and mergers break the series, and year-on-year across a revision year is meaningless. Transshipment through third countries (Mexico, Vietnam and others) disconnects bilateral figures from real flows, and the US-China bilateral statistical discrepancy has been large for a long time. When you compute unit price as value divided by quantity, mixed quantity units (units, kilograms, sets) distort the unit-price series completely. **How to check**: compute year-on-year only inside one HS version. Across versions, re-map using the WCO correlation tables. Confirm the quantity units are the same before computing a unit price. For bilateral trade, pull both the exporter's and the importer's side, and flag possible transshipment when the gap is large.
- **L3-11** [Price samples confused with contract types] Long-term contract prices, spot prices and posted quotes can diverge for long periods. For items where long-term contracts dominate, such as iron ore, LNG and copper concentrate, inferring industry profit from spot prices is badly wrong, and the annual copper-concentrate TC benchmark can even move in the opposite direction to the spot TC. Also watch for an "average transaction price" that is published in a week with no transactions; that is willingness to quote, not a transaction fact. **How to check**: label every price as long-term, spot or posted, and give the traded volume for the period. Label periods with no transactions "quote", not "transaction price". When inferring industry profit, weight by the actual traded mix of long-term and spot.
- **L3-12** [Equating concentration with bargaining power] A stage with concentrated share but substitutable products, low switching costs and a short expansion lag (some packaging and assembly, for example) earns no economic rent. A real bottleneck needs high concentration plus high switching cost (long qualification or validation cycles) plus rigid capacity. A "chokepoint" story that meets only the first condition usually has its price rent arbitraged away within 2-4 quarters.
- **L3-13** [Ignoring qualification queues and so overstating supply elasticity] Automotive-grade chip validation takes 2-3 years, aircraft-parts airworthiness certification takes several years, and medical-device approval takes several years. Built physical capacity is not capacity that can ship. Looking only at the timetable for capacity coming on stream and not at the qualification timetable systematically overstates how fast near-term supply recovers, and repeatedly misjudges when prices will fall back in a bottleneck stage. **How to check**: show the qualification or validation period as its own segment on the supply timeline (physical start-up date plus qualification period equals shippable date). Take the period from published qualification cases or regulatory approval times in that industry. If no public data exist, write a range and tag it [C].
- **L3-14** [Shutdown is not exit] Capacity that is only idled, with no demolition, no permit cancellation and no site disposal, comes back quickly when prices rise and forms a hidden ceiling on price increases. Judge real capacity clearing from evidence of demolition and permit cancellation, not from the fall in output alone.

### Market and single-stock layer (L4 / L5)

- **L4-01** [L4-short interest] FINRA short interest is a twice-monthly figure published 7 business days after the settlement date, so using it to judge "crowding" is at least two weeks stale. It also contains no over-the-counter synthetic shorts such as total return swaps. Archegos-style exposure is invisible in any public data.
- **L4-02** [L4-short interest] FINRA daily short volume is a measure of trading volume, not of positions, and a large part of it is the sell leg of market makers' two-sided quotes. Treating the short volume ratio as the share of short positions is the most common hard error in retail and semi-professional research.
- **L4-03** [L4-short interest] Do not cite "official data on institutional short positions". The compliance date for SEC Rule 13f-2 / Form SHO has been exempted and extended repeatedly, and the latest deferral puts the first filing in February 2028. No official disclosure of institutional short positions exists before then. Likewise, public dissemination under Rule 10c-1a / FINRA SLATE has not yet started.
- **L4-04** [L4-holdings] Form 13F covers only US-listed long positions of filers on the 13F list. It excludes shorts, cash, non-US holdings and most derivatives, and a filer may request confidential treatment to delay disclosure. A 45-day lag means you see positions others have already finished building. Using 13F to judge that "smart money is buying" is a timing mismatch. **How to check**: 13F answers "who holds", not "who is buying". Show the report period (quarter end) and the filing date (about +45 days) in two columns. Read position changes against 13D/13G event dates and Form 4 for timing. Give passive and active shares by filer type.
- **L4-05** [L4-buybacks] Daily buyback data do not exist. On 19 December 2023 the Fifth Circuit vacated the SEC's Share Repurchase Disclosure Modernization rule (including the daily Exhibit 26 table), leaving only the monthly totals under Item 703. Any analysis that claims to locate a company's support buying on a particular day of a sell-off is made up.
- **L4-06** [L4-index] The price effect of index inclusion has weakened markedly over the past decade (passive share has risen, but arbitrageurs front-run, and rule transparency prices the event in early). Carrying over the 1990s-2010s rule that inclusion means positive excess return is an outdated conclusion. **How to check**: estimate the effect of the current event only from comparable inclusion events of the past ten years, over the window from announcement to effective date (the event study in section 2, E2, of `references/judgment.md`). State the sample period and the number of samples. Do not cite excess-return findings from before 2010.
- **L4-07** [L4-expectations] An EPS beat is itself an outcome, and consensus is systematically guided low. What carries information is the composition of the beat: tax rate, buybacks, FX and one-offs versus true price and volume. Beat/miss statistics without this decomposition are noise.
- **L4-08** [L4-options] Gamma exposure and max pain depend on assumptions about market-maker direction. Public open-interest data cannot distinguish opening from closing trades or buyers from sellers, so any directional inference is a guess. Treating it as a deterministic mechanism reverses in extreme markets. **How to check**: report only open interest, implied volatility, skew and the percentile of the implied distribution (E4) as facts. If you cite gamma exposure or max pain, tag it [C] and state the market-maker direction you assumed.
- **L4-09** [L4-volatility] Single-stock IV and skew rise mechanically in the weeks around earnings. An "IV percentile" computed without removing event days systematically mistakes a routine earnings season for panic.
- **L4-10** [L4-price series] Stock splits, special dividends, trading halts and stock-for-stock mergers distort momentum, volatility and drawdown entirely if they are not adjusted correctly. Free data sources often use adjustment factors that differ from the exchange or CRSP, and splicing sources always creates breaks. **How to check**: compute adjustment factors yourself from split ratios and dividends in exchange and company announcements. When splicing sources, check continuity over 5 trading days on each side of every split or ex-dividend date. Keep both the unadjusted close and the adjustment factor in the working file.
- **L4-11** [L4-flows] An ETF's daily holdings file is a stock, not a flow. Only the change in shares outstanding (creations and redemptions) corresponds to fund flows. Treating the change in market value of holdings as inflow confuses price change with flow.
- **L4-12** [L4-reverse-solve] A price back-solve must use the same date and the same share count throughout, and the implied multiple must not be counted again alongside margin and time cost. (1) Do not combine today's equity market value (current share count) with the target-date net debt (which already includes cash from equity issued before the target date) to get target-date enterprise value EV_T. That mismatches both date and share count, and the equity for the new shares never enters EV. Also do not subtract the target-date present value of contracts from the EV at t = 0: the two sides are one discount period apart. (2) The implied multiple is the solution of an equation with the margin pinned. A multiple solved at one margin changes when you substitute the base-case margin, and at the base-case margin it can equal the base-case multiple exactly. Listing "the multiple gap" and "the time-cost gap" as two separate differences writes the same equation twice. **How to check**: EV_T = E x (1+k)^t + equity issued before the target date + target-date net debt. The target-date net debt and the three scenarios share the one ledger from L5-22. Reconcile with the contract present value at the same date. Give the reverse-solve table two rows, "multiple solved at a fixed margin" and "margin solved at a fixed multiple". When reconciling with the three scenarios, list the two forms of the same equation as one item.

- **L5-01** [L5-RPO] The practical expedients for RPO (contracts with an original term of one year or less, the invoice-as-right-to-invoice portion under ASC 606-10-55-18, and variable consideration allocated to wholly unsatisfied performance obligations) can keep a lot of real commitments out of the disclosure. Comparing absolute RPO across companies is nearly meaningless. Compare only the continuity of one company's own definition over time, and read which expedients that company elects. See also L5-18 (ASC 842 lessor contracts do not enter RPO) and L5-19 (the RPO tag is polluted by total contract value).
- **L5-02** [L5-deferred revenue] Contract liabilities are driven by collection rhythm (annual billing changed to monthly, shifted invoice dates), and they are not a demand indicator. When a SaaS company moves to monthly billing, deferred revenue falls "from nothing", and reading that as a demand collapse is the classic trap. The reverse also happens: the "deferred revenue" total can contain advance payments received by an ASC 842 lessor for capacity that has not yet commenced (deferred lease revenue). That is not an ASC 606 contract liability, but it is a hard signal of signed, not-yet-commenced capacity. The cash flow statement may also book the whole increase under the 606 label. **How to check**: (1) Whenever you cite "deferred revenue", say whether it is 606 or 842 and whether it is current or non-current, taking the split from the balance sheet notes (XBRL: ContractWithCustomerLiabilityCurrent / Noncurrent against DeferredIncomeNoncurrent; DeferredRevenue is the total). (2) Compute the change by subtracting the two period-end balance-sheet balances. Do not rebuild it from the cash flow tag IncreaseDecreaseInContractWithCustomerLiability. On one lessor-type issuer that tag was several times the true increase on the 606 basis, so bulk-pulling by tag is wrong by that multiple. (3) For SaaS-type companies, first read the payment terms (billing terms) in the revenue note and MD&A for changes, and only then discuss demand.
- **L5-03** [L5-backlog] Backlog has no standard definition. A defence contractor's total backlog and funded backlog (appropriated by Congress) can differ by multiples. Some definitions include orders the customer may cancel without liability. Cancellation terms sit in contracts that are not public, so the "hardness" of a backlog cannot be judged from the disclosure. **How to check**: quote the defining sentence of backlog verbatim from the 10-K and mark whether it is funded or total and whether it includes orders cancellable without liability. Compare across companies only on the same definition. Replace "hardness" with two things: the verbatim disclosure of cancellation and termination terms, and the historical conversion rate (opening backlog in a quarter divided by the next four quarters of revenue, over the latest 8 quarters). For "contracted value" at landlord-type data-centre companies, see L5-25.
- **L5-04** [L5-non-GAAP] Non-GAAP measures and key metrics such as ARR, NRR and MAU are defined entirely by the company. A change of definition is often noted in one line of a reconciliation footnote, and the history is not restated, which distorts year-on-year comparison. Auditors express no opinion on non-GAAP figures. **How to check**: lay out the adjustment-item names from the reconciliations of the latest 8 quarters as a matrix. List every item that appears or disappears in any quarter and trace it to the footnote. Compare the definition sentence of custom metrics such as ARR and NRR year by year against the original text, and break the series where the definition changed.
- **L5-05** [L5-segments] ASC 280 uses the management approach, so one reorganisation can break a segment's history without full restatement. After ASU 2023-07 added "significant segment expense" disclosure, many companies disclose only the one expense line the CODM actually reviews regularly, so the granularity is still not comparable across companies. **How to check**: in the quarter of a reorganisation, look in the 8-K / 10-Q for a history table restated on the new basis. If none exists, cut the series at the reorganisation and say so. After ASU 2023-07, check how many significant segment expenses the segment note lists and whether it is only the one the CODM reviews regularly.
- **L5-06** [L5-depreciation] Lengthening useful lives (for example, servers from 4 years to 6) can lawfully raise EPS and margins from nothing, and the change is mentioned in one sentence of the accounting-policy note. Reading the resulting margin improvement as operating leverage coming through is a misreading that has recurred many times at cloud-computing companies in recent years. **How to check**: compare the useful-lives sentence in the 10-K accounting-policy note word for word with the prior year. In a year with a change, quantify "(depreciation at old life minus depreciation at new life) / net income" and strip it out of the margin analysis. Put peers' depreciation lives in a column alongside.
- **L5-07** [L5-off-balance-sheet] Supply-chain finance and reverse factoring put what is in substance interest-bearing debt into accounts payable. They lower net debt, lengthen DPO and create the appearance of working-capital improvement. A dedicated note exists only after ASU 2022-04 (the rollforward from FY2024), so earlier periods are not comparable and net-debt series in historical databases are systematically understated.
- **L5-08** [L5-sample] Detailed disclosures such as Schedule II (Reg S-X Rule 12-09) can be omitted by smaller reporting companies. Using it for an industry cross-section of allowance adequacy leaves a survivorship-style gap in which small companies are systematically absent.
- **L5-09** [L5-insiders] Form 4 has had a Rule 10b5-1 check box only since 2023, so earlier history cannot separate planned from unplanned trades. The real decision date is the plan adoption date, which appears only in the quarterly disclosure under Reg S-K Item 408; the date on Form 4 is the execution date. Insider-sale data that have not been cleaned are badly polluted by forced tax-withholding sales (code F). There is a drafting trap too: when the basis-date share price is adjusted, a global replace on the old price string will also overwrite any Form 4 filed transaction price, conversion price, strike price or historical close that shares the same digits, although these are independent facts. Change only the values derived from the current price. The rule is the basis-date rule in SKILL.md. **How to check**: before finalising, compare every Form 4 price in the body one by one against the original filed values saved in your governance data file.
- **L5-10** [L5-contracts] Since 2019, a company may delete competition-sensitive terms from an EX-10 directly, without applying for confidential treatment. The most important terms, price, minimum purchase quantities and rebate tiers, are often blacked out, so inferring unit economics from the contract text is badly biased. **How to check**: go through each section of the EX-10 and mark the redactions ([***] or "portions omitted"), and list the names of the redacted terms in a table. Infer unit economics only from unredacted items. Fill a missing price from the earnings release or call, label it "company statement, not a contract term", and grade it under the SKILL.md evidence-grading rule.
- **L5-11** [L5-commitments] The "table of contractual obligations" under Reg S-K Item 303 was deleted in the 2020 MD&A modernisation, so you cannot assume every company provides a summary of future purchase and lease obligations. When a company stops providing the table, a year-on-year comparison reads it as falling obligations. **How to check**: assemble the obligation table yourself from the commitments and contingencies note (ASC 440 purchase and capital commitments), the ASC 842 lessee maturity table and the debt maturity table, and state the three sources and the cut-off date. For how remaining capex is measured, see L5-24.
- **L5-12** [L5-customers] Disclosure of customers at 10% or more usually gives a percentage with no name (ASC 280 does not require naming). Sales through EMS firms, contract manufacturers or distributors also hide true end demand: the volume sold to a contract manufacturer reflects the order expectations of the end customer, not end shipments. **How to check**: lay the 10% customers out as a year-by-year percentage matrix from the 10-K. For sales through EMS, contract manufacturers or distributors, trace the end customer in the filings of the contract manufacturer and the large customer (customer-concentration and purchase-commitment notes). If the counterparty's filings do not mention it, say the check is one-sided.
- **L5-13** [L5-inventory] Looking only at total days of inventory mixes two opposite things. A surge in finished goods is usually a "process" signal of weakening demand, while a surge in raw materials may be a "cause", deliberate stocking. Split by tier and read it together with purchase commitments.
- **L5-14** [L5-fiscal year] A company on a 52/53-week fiscal year gains an extra week every 5-6 years, which adds about 2% to year-on-year growth from nothing, and most databases do not adjust for it. Likewise, drifting fiscal-quarter end dates move holidays into different fiscal quarters (worst in retail). For issuers that do not close in December, the fiscal year number follows the **year in which the period ends** (a fiscal year from 1 July 2025 to 30 June 2026 is FY26); counting from the midpoint of the fiscal year is one year short. A fiscal quarter that does not match a calendar quarter can also leave a threshold date unreachable (a draft once put a quarterly revenue threshold in a fiscal quarter that, by the company's own delivery calendar, could not reach it). **How to check**: confirm the "fiscal year ended" date and whether it is a 52/53-week year on the 10-K cover. In XBRL, check dei:DocumentFiscalYearFocus, DocumentFiscalPeriodFocus and DocumentPeriodEndDate. Scale year-on-year in a 53-week year to a 52/53 basis and say so. For an issuer that does not close in December, build a fiscal-quarter to calendar-quarter map in the working file first, and write thresholds and catalysts by calendar date with the fiscal quarter in brackets.
- **L5-15** [L5-impairment] Goodwill impairment is a pure "outcome" and extremely late, and management has a strong incentive to delay recognising it. The information value of the impairment announcement is far lower than that of the fragile valuation assumptions already named in the earlier auditor's critical audit matter (CAM).
- **L5-16** [L5-XBRL] Traps in bulk-scraping SEC XBRL. Companies can define extension tags, and even standard tags are not unique (Revenues against RevenueFromContractWithCustomerExcludingAssessedTax against various Axis combinations), so the same "revenue" maps to different elements at different companies. Before any bulk cross-company comparison, map the tags and hand-check at least 10 companies. For a reverse-merger or shell-listed issuer, the XBRL facts before the merger closed belong to the shell, so a long series must start in the quarter the merger closed. A quarterly value and a year-to-date value of the same tag can have different bases (for ContractWithCustomerLiabilityRevenueRecognized, the quarterly value is "opening balance of the quarter recognised in the quarter" while the year-to-date value is "opening balance of the fiscal year recognised", so the sum of quarters far exceeds the year-to-date value), and they must not be added or differenced. **How to check**: for every fact in companyfacts, check start, end, frame and form. Before pulling a series, look up the merger completion date in the 8-K Item 2.01. For a tag with both quarterly and year-to-date values, use one set only. Two more known tag-level traps are in L5-02 (the cash flow contract-liability tag) and L5-19 (the RPO tag).
- **L5-17** [L5-look-ahead bias] Item 4.02 restatements in 8-Ks and later retrospective adjustments make financial databases rewrite history silently. Any historical test must use point-in-time (as-available-then) data and not the latest restated values. Otherwise the conclusion is contaminated by look-ahead bias.

- **L5-18** [L5-RPO/842] A contract that leases self-owned assets by the building (or by dedicated cluster) is treated in full as an ASC 842 lessor operating lease if the lease component dominates. It is not in RPO and not in 606 contract liabilities. The revenue note adds a separate sentence for "contracted lease arrangements" (one issuer tags it LessorOperatingLeasePaymentsToBeReceived), and the prepayment goes into "deferred lease revenue" inside deferred revenue (see L5-02). Company earnings releases often quote only the sum of the two. A cloud vendor's 606 contract runs three to five years and a landlord's 842 contract ten to twenty, so a difference of an order of magnitude in EV / contract value is a difference in time scale, not in valuation. **How to check**: (1) Search the revenue note for lease component / lessor / operating lease and decide first which standard each type of contract follows. (2) Pull three XBRL tags separately and reconcile them: RevenueRemainingPerformanceObligation (606), LessorOperatingLeasePaymentsToBeReceived (842), and the company's extension aggregate tag. The aggregate must equal the sum of the first two, with a difference of rounding only. (3) Before comparing EV / contract value, put everything on one standard and convert to an annualised amount (contract value divided by remaining term).
- **L5-19** [L5-RPO/tag] A company may use the same RevenueRemainingPerformanceObligation element to tag the total of a contract signed after period end. In one filing, a 10-Q can carry a post-period contract total at one date alongside a much smaller true RPO at the period-end balance sheet date, in the same document. In companyfacts these values sit under the same tag as the true RPO with no frame, so bulk-pulling a series by tag draws a false RPO jump. **How to check**: take only instant values whose end date equals the balance sheet date. Prefer values that have a frame. When one quarter shows several values, go back to the RPO maturity table in the note and reconcile.
- **L5-20** [L5-share count] Issued is not outstanding, and the cover date is not the quarter end. (1) In some quarters XBRL CommonStockSharesOutstanding is identical to CommonStockSharesIssued (treasury shares not deducted) and is deducted in the annual period, so a long series draws a false turning point (a drop in the series that is only a basis change from three periods without the treasury deduction to later periods with it). (2) dei:EntityCommonStockSharesOutstanding is the share count on the cover date of the 10-Q / 10-K, not at quarter end. An ATM issuance in the last weeks of a quarter then lands in the following quarter. The cover-page share count recommended in the "float and shares-outstanding trajectory" entry of `references/indicators.md` represents only the cover date. **How to check**: take the quarter-end share count from the share-capital line of the balance sheet or the closing balance in the statement of equity, and compute "issued minus treasury shares". In XBRL, check whether TreasuryStockCommonShares is non-zero and whether Outstanding and Issued are identical. If they are identical while treasury stock is non-zero, the tag is wrong. Use the cover-date share count only for "latest supply" and state the date. Never mix the two bases in one series.
- **L5-21** [L5-dilution] Four traps in convertible-bond capped calls and prepaid forwards. (1) Capped-call offset shares = notional shares x (min(spot, cap price) minus conversion price)+ divided by spot. The shortcut "notional shares minus principal divided by cap price" holds only when spot equals the cap price exactly. (2) A capped call can be partly unwound, and the remaining covered shares are usually not disclosed, so you cannot treat the remaining principal as fully covered. (3) The profit-and-loss exposure on own-stock derivatives disappears after reclassification to equity. Once the ASC 815-40 equity-classification conditions are met (for example, shareholder approval of repurchasing the underlying shares), the capped call and prepaid forward move from Level 3 derivative assets into APIC and no longer pass through earnings. Do not extrapolate the fair-value gains and losses from before the reclassification as normal. (4) The anti-dilution table may list only one tranche of a prepaid forward. Back the other out by dividing the fair value in the derivative note by the closing price on the reclassification date, and the two tranches together can come to materially more shares than the table shows. **How to check**: in the statement of equity, find the lines "Settlement of ... Capped Call" and "Reclassification ... to stockholders' equity", and search the MD&A for "unwind". In the derivative note, check which tranches are still measured at fair value at period end, and their balances. Cross-check the prepaid-forward share count as "fair value divided by price on the reclassification date" against the anti-dilution table. Tag any mismatch [C] and put it on the list of items to verify.
- **L5-22** [L5-valuation ledger] The source of funds and the target-date balance sheet must be one set of accounts. The hard rule is in section 5 of `references/judgment.md` (one cash balance cannot be both a source of funds and an asset in hand at the target date). This entry adds four specific forms. (1) Symmetry: if new equity is treated as "spent" (shares added, net debt not reduced), existing cash must also be treated as spent. Draws on committed facilities enter net debt according to delivery progress. (2) Scope: the scope of target-date net debt must equal the scope of the assets that generate the cash flows being valued. If you put the construction cost of next-phase capacity into net debt and book its value elsewhere, you reach a wrong attribution such as "the contracted part leaves nothing for equity" (settled separately, the contracted part leaves positive value per share). (3) Strictness: use the same definition of usable funds for the valuation input and the funding-gap analysis (one draft used a broad definition for valuation and a strict one for the gap, and the mechanism diagram used a third number). (4) Interest: stable-state or target-date net debt cannot hold principal only. Cash interest during construction, cash paid for tax withholding, employee trust funding and operating deficits all go into net debt. Interest accrues on the outstanding balance, not on cumulative draws. Interest on new debt and interest income on cash are either both counted or both left out, and the choice is stated. **How to check**: build a month-by-month (or quarter-by-quarter) cash ledger from the basis date to the target date. For each period list opening cash, contract cash receipts, capex, customer prepayments, draws on committed facilities, repayments, interest (outstanding balance x annual rate / 12), cash interest income and equity issued. Closing net debt = closing debt minus closing cash. The same rules apply to all three scenarios. Also run one version that spends only the contracted capex and settle it separately. Compare the size of interest and tax withholding in the ledger with interest paid and tax withheld in the 10-Q cash flow statement. The reverse-solve (L4-12) and the three scenarios share this one ledger.
- **L5-23** [L5-scenarios] The downside scenario cannot reuse a static DCF that assumes contracts are honoured in full. When all three scenarios assume all capacity is built and rent is received in full, the "downside weight" is only a valuation scenario in which the market grants no renewals and no pipeline. It is not a probability of failed delivery (one draft's downside came from a path in which contracts were booked in full). The scenario price and the trigger conditions must describe the same world (one draft's triggers read "the sponsor's support does not materialise, a key site slips by more than a quarter, equity is issued below a given price", while the price assumed on-time lease start, 20 years of rent in full and no dilution). Where a pessimistic price is held up by an equity-issuance assumption, say so separately (one pessimistic price assumed an issue of billions of dollars at a stated price, and without the issue it was far lower). **How to check**: list the assumptions of each scenario in a table (delivery calendar, share signed, contract price, issue price and amount, residual value), and have the price come out of that table. Every item in the trigger conditions must map to a change in the assumption table. The downside must include at least one contract-level failure (exercise of a termination right, slippage beyond the grace period, counterparty default with no credit support). For each scenario, also compute a no-issuance (all-debt) comparison price, and explain the difference in the scenario table when it is large.
- **L5-24** [L5-capex] Three traps in remaining capex. (1) "MW not yet energised x all-in unit cost" overstates. Deduct the part already spent that sits in construction in progress, and add unpaid construction costs held in accounts payable and accrued construction liabilities (one draft handled neither side). (2) Use the company's own reported unit cost for that site first, and do not carry one site's guidance to another (a $/MW figure that applied to one business unit was extrapolated to a different site). (3) Equipment delivered but unpaid sits in accounts payable. If you do not count it in net debt, you must count it in remaining spend. Choose one; do not omit both (one draft treated a large SPV payable as "already paid", which overstated two valuation paths by a few dollars per share). **How to check**: remaining cash spend = total purchase contracts minus capitalised cost already booked (the matching increase in PP&E and finance-lease ROU assets) plus capital-related unpaid amounts in payables and accruals. Cross-check it against "unrecorded commitments in the commitments note plus capital payables". Reconcile site by site against the remaining capex and capex per MW disclosed in earnings releases and investor presentations. A jump in month-on-month XBRL AccountsPayableCurrent that cannot be explained by operations signals capital payables.
- **L5-25** [L5-contract value] The "$X billion contracted" that a landlord-type company advertises is nominal rent over ten to twenty-five years. The ASC 842 lessor note includes in "minimum rent receivable on commenced leases" only the commenced portion, which can be a small fraction of the nominal amount. The same company may also switch its basis on joint ventures (a contract amount shown at 100% in one period and at the company's ownership share in a later period), or even show MW at the company's share and dollars at 100% in the same period. A credit-enhancement or backstop coverage ratio can tell opposite stories when its denominator changes (fixed nominal total against remaining unpaid rent). **How to check**: for the figures you tag [Fixed], take only the lessor maturity analysis table (XBRL LessorOperatingLeasePaymentsToBeReceived and its by-year tags), and reconcile the sum of the years with the note total. Split the nominal amount by the joint-venture share disclosed in each period, and mark the dates on which the company switched basis. Show the coverage ratio on both denominators. Before comparing EV / contract value across companies, equalise joint-venture share and remaining term (for the two standards 606 and 842, see L5-18).
- **L5-26** [L5-capacity basis] MW at data-centre and compute companies has two bases, gross (power connection) and critical IT (critical IT load). One company mixes them across its earnings release, presentation and 10-Q. The ratio between two figures for the same site can equal the design PUE exactly. Platform totals in presentations are typeset without stating the basis. Among peers, one reports gross and another reports IT, which makes the per-MW multiple differ by 20-25% (a "contracted gross MW" figure converts to a materially smaller IT figure). **How to check**: label the basis of every MW figure before it goes into a table. Test "IT / gross is about 1 / PUE" using the company's own stated PUE. If it fails, the bases are mixed or joint-venture shares are mixed in (for example, a row where pipeline IT exceeds gross). Add up the platform total site by site to find out which one is gross. For peer comparison, convert everything to critical IT and state the conversion basis.
- **L5-27** [L5-revenue base] A contract with large prepayments or escalators has three sets of bases: GAAP straight-line revenue against contract cash receipts, first-year rent against average rent, and effective interest rate against cash coupon. A multiple applies only to sustainable cash EBITDA. If you first spend the prepayment as a source of capex funds and then put GAAP straight-line revenue into stable-state EBITDA and apply a multiple, you sell the spent money a second time (in one case the stable-state EBITDA was cut materially once the cash basis replaced GAAP straight-line). For escalating leases, using the average rent as the "stable state" raises the base (the per-share value is lower on the first-year rent). Interest is the same: the effective rate, with issuance-cost amortisation and hedges included, is not the cash coupon. **How to check**: list cash flows year by year from the contract schedule or the 8-K payment schedule and mark the prepayment offset period. Use one definition of stable-state EBITDA and one treatment of deferred revenue on the EV side, and write both in one table. Label the rent-base row "first-year / average" and the interest row "effective-rate basis / cash-coupon basis", and run a sensitivity for each.
- **L5-28** [L5-coverage multiple] Principal amortisation within debt service is already the cash form of capital recovery. Deducting depreciation from EBITDA again before comparing with debt service deducts the same thing twice (a draft reported "coverage after GPU depreciation" that way). Using multi-year average EBITDA against an amortisation that exists only in the early years produces a multiple that does not exist in any year (the multi-year average multiple lay between the early-year and the later-year values and matched neither). **How to check**: list EBITDA, cash interest and principal amortisation year by year, and give two measures separately: "EBITDA / (interest + amortisation)" and the capital-recovery multiple "(EBITDA minus cash interest) / depreciation". Do not combine them into "coverage after depreciation". When the amortisation period is shorter than the depreciation period or the contract term, report the multiple by segment of the period.
- **L5-29** [L5-residual value] The discount date for a residual value must fall after the last cash flow of that asset. Discounting the residual of one batch of equipment at 4.5 years while counting the renewal period out to years 6-7 means collecting rent after the sale (correcting this cut the present value of the residual materially). When sites have different lease terms, the residual cannot be recovered in one common expiry year (one model recovered all residuals in one year while a site's lease ran many years longer, which overstated the residual present value). **How to check**: write "last cash-flow year" for each asset, and make the residual discount period at least that year, discounting by site and by batch. Run a 0-30% sensitivity on the residual rate and state the base (original order value or booked cost).
- **L5-30** [L5-search] Zero hits for an abbreviation does not mean "not named". Filings use the full legal name (a fund manager's short brand name against its registered legal name). A draft wrote "not named in the 10-K" on the strength of zero hits. A counterparty may appear only in Item 9B (Other Information) or the MD&A, while the subsequent-events note gives only amount, rate and term (one correction then wrote "named in both the note and Item 9B", when in fact all mentions were in Item 9B). A financing signed after period end may have no separate 8-K Item 1.01 and appear only in the 10-K. **How to check**: build an alias table first (legal name, abbreviation, subsidiaries, SPV names) and then use EDGAR full-text search. Conclude "not named" only after checking four places: the subsequent-events note, Item 9B, recent developments in the MD&A, and the exhibit index. State in the working file which places you checked. Cite down to the Item or note number.
- **L5-31** [L5-attribution] A company-level total must not be written into the row of a single site or segment, and when the company has disclosed the components, do not estimate them pro rata. (One draft wrote a company-wide "operating AI cloud of about 40 MW" into the row for one site, and the replacement wording offered in the first correction then wrote a company-wide "mining of about 380 MW" into the same row. Another draft estimated a backstop by building by MW share, when the earnings release had already disclosed the amount that takes effect after delivery of that building.) **How to check**: next to each component in a table, note whether the number is disclosed on this dimension, and put figures on a total basis only in the total row or in a table note. Before estimating a component, search the earnings release, presentation and call for a disclosed component. Run replacement wording suggested by an audit through this check as well.
- **L5-32** [L5-series basis] If numerator or denominator changes basis midway through a series or a percentile, the chart shows a false turning point. Examples: a denominator that moves from the pre-sale nominal total to the post-sale one (a ratio rises from a few percent to over five percent only because the denominator doubled); a current value computed on the report's own EV basis placed against a history on the base basis (a multiple said to sit at the "90th percentile of its own history" is at the 100th percentile when placed in the series on the same basis); net income attributable to the parent in one place and including non-controlling interests in another, in the same chapter; and a series whose first point is degenerate (the share of 606 in a series is 100% at the first point because no 842 contract existed yet). **How to check**: in the figure or table note, state the numerator and denominator basis of every series and percentile, and the dates of any switch. Recompute the current value of a percentile on the same basis as the history series. Delete degenerate first points or annotate them.

### General (cross-layer)

- **G-01** [General: cause/process/outcome labelling] The commonest qualitative error is to treat an indicator that "lags but is volatile" as a leading indicator. Share price, trading volume, consensus estimates, 13F, goodwill impairment and say-on-pay results are all outcomes. The test is "the change in this indicator must, physically or contractually, happen before the outcome", not "this indicator appears to move first".
- **G-02** [General: evidence boundary] Three ways of writing an inference as a fact, each a recurring failure in practice. (1) Writing an illustrative model as a contract term (a chapter title and a KPI took "support falls to zero in year N" from an illustrative model made before Reg FD pricing, a file that says on its face that all amounts are estimates). (2) Deducing a negative fact from silence in the filings and tagging it [A] ("no credit enhancement of any size has been signed" when the primary documents support only "not disclosed"; "the regulatory approval and the transfer of transmission rights are both incomplete" has the same flaw). (3) Tagging a timing or purpose [A] when it was deduced from a contract term plus an event date (a billing start date inferred from a statement-of-work term plus an acceptance date; "restricted cash may be used only to buy GPUs and repay debt" when the note says only that the cash is contractually restricted). **How to check**: before tagging [A], confirm the original text contains the sentence and you can cite the note, Item or page. Write a negative fact as "not disclosed as of date X" and list in the working file the places you searched. Tag a conclusion deduced from a term plus an event [C] and write the derivation chain (original term text plus the source of the event). Quote illustrative models, guidance and forecasts in the company's own words, state "company illustration / estimate, not a contract term", and keep them out of the [Fixed] tier.

## Part 2. Methodology index

Whenever you use a framework, state in the report **which framework you used, what problem it solves and the
conditions under which it fails**. A framework is scaffolding, not a conclusion. A conclusion reached with a
framework still needs primary data behind it.

### Cross-layer (usable at L0-L5; listed once, here)

- Reverse DCF / market-implied expectations (Rappaport & Mauboussin, *Expectations Investing*). It solves the current market value or share price for a set of falsifiable operating assumptions (growth, margin, investment rate, competitive advantage period; at the industry-chain layer, the implied capacity, price and share assumptions). This turns "is it expensive?", a question that can only be settled by feel, into "do these assumptions hold?", which can be falsified. The macro counterpart is "Back out implied macro assumptions from market prices" below. (L3 / L4-L5; section 2, E1, of `references/judgment.md`)
- The financial instability hypothesis and the Minsky moment (Hyman Minsky; the hedge, speculative and Ponzi stages of financing). At the macro layer it explains how stability itself breeds fragility, and it makes identifiable the point at which L1 debt structure degrades from self-repaying cash flow to dependence on refinancing (check against the BIS debt service ratio and corporate maturity-wall data). At the single-stock layer it detects a migration of financing structure from hedge to speculative to Ponzi, and is meant for business models that survive on continuous refinancing. (L1 / L5)
- S-curves and technology diffusion (the Bass diffusion model, 1969; Carlota Perez, *Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital*, with its five phases of irruption, frenzy, turning point, synergy and maturity). Use it to judge whether penetration is in the introduction, acceleration or deceleration segment, and to avoid extrapolating introduction-phase growth linearly into the mainstream market. Perez's phases explain why a financial bubble is a structural companion of the infrastructure build-out phase and not just irrationality. (L0-L2 / L3)
- The inventory cycle (Kitchin, about 3-4 years) and the equipment investment cycle (Juglar, about 7-11 years). At the macro layer they split fluctuation into an inventory restock/destock rhythm and a capex rhythm, so you can locate the current segment and when the next one comes (matching the inventory-to-sales ratio, ISM customer inventories, capacity utilisation and core capital-goods orders). At the industry-chain layer they set the beat for restocking and destocking phases and turn inventory data from noise into a signal with phase. Used nested, the two separate an "inventory-driven decline" from a "capacity-driven decline". The year ranges are empirical ranges (Kitchin 1923; Juglar 1862; Schumpeter's 1939 multi-cycle classification), not constants. (L2 / L3)
- Value-chain analysis and the smile curve (Porter's value chain / Stan Shih's smile curve). It locates the distribution of value along the chain and the direction it is moving, and explains why the two ends (R&D, brand and channel) earn excess profit while the middle manufacturing stage is squeezed. At the single-stock layer it locates the company's profit-capture stage in the chain and shows who ends up receiving the industry's upswing. It is the bridge from industry-chain research to single-stock research. (L3 / L5)

### Macro layer (L0 / L1 / L2)

- The debt-cycle framework (Ray Dalio, *Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises*: a short-term debt cycle of about 5-8 years, a long-term debt cycle of about 50-75 years, and four ways to deleverage: default and restructuring, austerity, wealth transfer, debt monetisation). It answers "why does the same policy rate have a completely different effect at a different debt stock?", and gives the test for whether deleveraging is deflationary or "harmonious".
- Debt-deflation theory (Irving Fisher, 1933). It answers "why does deflation reinforce itself when nominal debt is rigid?": falling prices raise the real debt burden, force sales, and push prices down further in a positive feedback loop. It is the theoretical forerunner of the balance-sheet recession.
- The balance-sheet recession (Richard Koo). It answers "when the private sector shifts from maximising profit to minimising debt, why do rate cuts and quantitative easing both stop working and the fiscal deficit becomes the only way out?" It is the core framework for Japan in the 1990s and for deleveraging by Chinese households and firms today.
- The financial cycle (BIS, Borio and others: a medium- to long-term cycle composed of credit and property prices, about 16-20 years long, far longer than the traditional business cycle). It answers "why can a GDP-frequency business cycle not show the build-up of a crisis?", and maps directly onto two observables, the credit-to-GDP gap and the DSR.
- The empirical framework of credit booms (Schularick & Taylor, *Credit Booms Gone Bust*, a 17-country, 140-year panel). It answers "which variable best predicts financial crises?". Its finding (credit growth, not money growth) gives credit-gap-type warning indicators an empirical anchor.
- Crisis taxonomy (Reinhart & Rogoff, *This Time Is Different*, an eight-century database covering sovereign default, banking crises, inflation crises and currency collapses). It answers "why do different types of crisis have different precursors, durations and shapes of recovery?", and prevents applying the lessons of a banking crisis to a sovereign debt crisis.
- Stock-flow consistent (SFC) models (Godley & Lavoie) and the three-sector balance identity (private balance + government balance + foreign balance = 0). They test the internal consistency of a macro narrative. Any scheme in which "the government cuts its deficit, the private sector raises saving and trade does not improve" fails the accounting identity, so it can be falsified without any empirical work.
- Growth accounting and endogenous growth theory (Solow residual decomposition; Romer's knowledge-spillover growth). They answer "is current growth input-driven or efficiency-driven?". Krugman's "paper tiger" critique of the East Asian model used exactly this decomposition. It is also the standard L0 tool for judging whether the catch-up dividend is exhausted.
- The Lewis turning point and the dual-economy model (W. Arthur Lewis). It answers "once surplus rural labour is exhausted, how do wages, inflation and the growth model shift structurally?". It is the timing framework for judging when a catch-up economy moves from factor-driven to efficiency-driven growth.
- Demographic dividend decomposition and National Transfer Accounts (Mason-Lee's first and second demographic dividend framework; the NTA project). It answers "how does a change in the dependency ratio translate quantitatively into per-capita output growth and a contribution to the saving rate?", linking the L0 demographic variables to the L2 saving-investment balance.
- The Balassa-Samuelson effect. It answers "why does the real exchange rate of a catch-up economy appreciate on trend?", and in which direction PPP and market-rate comparisons are biased at different stages of development. It is an essential correction in cross-country comparison.
- The Taylor rule and its variants (Taylor's 1993 original rule, the balanced-approach rule, the first-difference rule). They turn the central bank's behaviour into a function, giving a falsifiable benchmark for the policy path and an observable called "deviation" (the Cleveland Fed's Simple Monetary Policy Rules page is a public implementation).
- State-space estimation of the natural rate r* (the Laubach-Williams and Holston-Laubach-Williams models). They answer "is policy tight or loose?". The answer is the gap between the real rate and r*, not the absolute level of the nominal rate.
- The Mundell-Fleming trilemma and Hélène Rey's "dilemma, not trilemma" on the global financial cycle. They address the trade-off among capital flows, exchange-rate regime and monetary independence in an open economy, and explain why a floating exchange rate does not fully insulate a country from US monetary spillovers. They are the basic framework for emerging-market vulnerability.
- The modern decomposition of the Phillips curve (the Bernanke & Blanchard 2023 wage-price decomposition, which splits inflation into four channels: energy and food shocks, supply shortages, labour-market tightness and expectations). It answers "who contributed to this round of inflation?", and so which part will fade by itself and which must be pushed down by demand tightening.
- Beveridge curve analysis (the Blanchard-Domash-Summers and Waller debate). It answers "can the labour market cool without pain?", that is, whether the vacancy rate can fall along the steep segment of the curve without raising unemployment materially. It is the core mechanism in a soft-landing judgment, not a conclusion.
- Structural fiscal balance and fiscal impulse measures (the standard IMF *Fiscal Monitor* and OECD method: remove the cyclical component, one-off items and interest). They answer "is a wider deficit stimulus, or the automatic result of a recession?", and turn the direction of fiscal policy into a comparable first difference.
- Net errors and omissions in the balance of payments (reconciling the BOP identity with IIP stocks). It answers "how large is the unrecorded capital outflow?", and is the standard way to study true cross-border flows in an economy with capital controls (China in particular).
- Nowcasting and dynamic factor models (Stock-Watson dynamic factors; the bridge-regression implementation of the Atlanta Fed's GDPNow). They answer "how do I estimate current growth in real time from high-frequency data before GDP is released?", compressing the lag of the "outcome" in the epistemology to near the observation frequency of the "process".
- Back out implied macro assumptions from market prices (use breakeven inflation to back out the implied inflation path, the OIS curve to back out the implied policy path, and Damodaran's implied equity risk premium method to back out implied growth and the discount rate). They answer the prior question "what has the market already priced?", so that research bets only on the expectations gap and does not reproduce a consensus already in the price.

Cross-layer entries (Minsky, S-curve and Perez's five phases, Kitchin/Juglar cycles) are in "Cross-layer" above.

### Industry-chain layer (L3)

- The capacity cycle (capex cycle). It addresses the point that prices in heavy-asset industries are set by supply lags, not by current demand: high prices lead to expansion, capacity arrives in a cluster some years later, prices collapse, expansion stops, and the industry becomes tight again. It is the backbone model of industry-chain research.
- The cobweb model (Ezekiel, 1938). It explains how, when production has a lag, supply decided on last period's price makes prices oscillate by themselves. The oscillation diverges when supply elasticity exceeds demand elasticity and converges in the opposite case. Use it to judge whether an industry's cycle will converge by itself.
- The hog cycle. The best-known empirical form of the cobweb model. Use it in industries with a clear breeding or construction lag (hogs, aquaculture, some chemicals) to locate the turning points of restocking and culling.
- The bullwhip effect (Forrester effect, MIT system dynamics). It explains why small swings in end demand are amplified stage by stage as they pass upstream. Use it to correct the commonest industry-chain misjudgment, "upstream order volume equals true end demand".
- Porter's Five Forces. A structured framework for value distribution: whether a stage can pass on cost increases, and who ends up with the profit.
- Cost-curve analysis and marginal-producer pricing (cost curve / marginal cost support). Use percentiles of cash cost to judge where the price floor is, who stops production first and how long clearing will take. It is the only quantifiable method for judging a cycle bottom.
- The learning curve and Wright's Law (Swanson's Law as the solar special case). Use cumulative output to forecast the fall in unit cost, and from that the date at which parity is crossed. It answers "when does demand take off non-linearly?".
- The experience curve and minimum efficient scale (BCG experience curve / MES). It explains the positive feedback in which share and cost cause each other, and infers how many firms the industry can finally hold, that is, the steady-state structure after clearing.
- The SCP paradigm (structure-conduct-performance, industrial organisation theory). It links changes in industry structure (concentration, entry barriers), firm conduct (pricing, expansion) and final performance (profitability) into a traceable causal chain.
- Teardown and bill-of-materials analysis. It uses the share of material cost to quantify how much of the whole product's value each stage captures and how that changes between generations, turning value distribution from a qualitative story into numbers.
- Input-output analysis (Leontief). It uses official input-output tables to quantify the full-consumption and pull coefficients between upstream and downstream, and so estimates the elasticity and reach of a shock to one stage as it moves along the chain (data sources: China's National Bureau of Statistics input-output tables, the OECD ICIO tables).
- The Herfindahl-Hirschman index (HHI). It turns concentration from a vague description into a comparable number, for comparing bottleneck strength and antitrust attention across stages.
- Crossing the Chasm (Geoffrey Moore). It identifies the demand gap between early adopters and the mainstream market, and explains why some emerging industry chains go through a "false falsification", a stall in demand, while penetration is still in single digits.

Cross-layer entries (reverse DCF, S-curve and Bass diffusion, Kitchin/Juglar cycles, value chain and smile curve) are in "Cross-layer" above.

### Market and single-stock layer (L4 / L5)

- Competitive advantage period (CAP) and the economic-profit framework (Stern Stewart EVA). It anchors valuation on how long excess returns can last, which forces the moat judgment into the numerical model instead of leaving it as narrative.
- The ROIC tree and DuPont decomposition. They locate whether returns are driven by turnover, margin or leverage, and stop ROE from hiding the false quality created by leverage and buybacks.
- Christensen's disruptive-innovation framework (low-end disruption and new-market disruption). It identifies early structural threats while the incumbent's reported results still look good, and corrects the bias of extrapolating from the incumbent's view.
- Cohort analysis and unit economics (cohort / LTV-CAC / payback). They split aggregate growth into three separately verifiable lines (acquisition, retention, monetisation) and identify "bought growth" business models.
- The Beneish M-Score. It uses eight accruals and growth ratios to screen for the likelihood of earnings manipulation, as a first machine screen of financial quality.
- The Piotroski F-Score. Nine binary fundamental signals measure the direction in which financial condition is improving, used especially to remove value traps from a low-valuation sample.
- The Altman Z-Score / Ohlson O-Score. Quantitative scores of bankruptcy and distress probability, used for the downside boundary of highly leveraged names.
- The Sloan accrual anomaly. Evidence shows companies with high accruals have worse subsequent earnings and returns, which gives "earnings quality" a measure that can be priced.
- Montier's C-Score and the manipulation taxonomy in Schilit's *Financial Shenanigans*. They organise the qualitative signs of forensic accounting (profit diverging from cash, rising DSO, changes in capitalisation policy, one-off items becoming routine) into a checklist that can be run item by item.
- Morningstar's five economic moat sources (intangible assets, switching costs, network effects, cost advantage, efficient scale). They split "moat" into five propositions, each with its own evidence requirement, so narrative cannot replace evidence.
- The residual income model (Ohlson model). It discounts book value plus excess earnings, which cuts the valuation's sensitivity to terminal-value assumptions markedly, and suits financial and heavy-asset companies.
- Sum-of-the-parts valuation (SOTP) and implied-value back-solving. It splits a multi-business company and backs out the value the market implies for one business, to find losses hidden by the main business or assets that are under-valued.
- Scenario analysis and probability-weighted valuation (decision tree). A distribution replaces a single-point target price, so a conclusion carries a downside boundary and a position-sizing implication by construction.
- Variant perception / expectations-gap analysis. State where you differ from consensus and the falsifiable observables for that difference, so the research has a trading implication and a path to being proved wrong.
- Event study (cumulative abnormal return, CAR). It quantifies the historical average pricing reaction to a type of disclosure event, to judge whether a new event of the same type is already priced in.
- Scuttlebutt (Philip Fisher, *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits*). First-hand interviews with customers, suppliers and former employees fill in the "process" information that financial statements cannot show, and are one of the lawful sources of information at L5 that can lead disclosure. This package does no first-hand channel research (a standing limitation, section 8 of `references/judgment.md`), so the method is not available. Feasible substitutes are cross-checks against counterparty filings (customer-concentration and purchase-commitment notes in the customers' and suppliers' 10-Ks), regulatory dockets (state public utility commission and FERC dockets, ISO interconnection queues; a power supply agreement can be traced to a state commission docket) and SEC comment letters (EDGAR CORRESP / UPLOAD).
- The leading / coincident / lagging three-way classification (the NBER / Conference Board business-cycle indicator system). A mature counterpart of this framework's cause / process / outcome labelling, giving a public, citable methodological basis for classifying indicators.
- Point-in-time backtesting and restatement-aware backtests. They validate any historical regularity with the data vintage available at the time, which systematically removes look-ahead bias and survivorship bias.

Cross-layer entries (reverse DCF, Minsky, value chain and smile curve) are in "Cross-layer" above.
