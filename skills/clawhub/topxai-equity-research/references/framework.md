# Analysis Framework: the operating system of TopxAI Equity Research

> The three process files (`references/equity.md`, `references/industry.md`, `references/macro.md`) are built on this framework.
> This file answers "how to think". The process files answer "in what order to work".

---

## 0. What a report must deliver

Not information. **Judgment**. And not just judgment: **judgment that can be tested**.

> Prices, earnings, CPI prints and headlines are all **outcomes**.
> Outcomes are public, free, and already priced by the market.
> What a report adds is how the **process** behind those outcomes works, and what that process will produce next.

If a reader finishes a report knowing only what has already happened, it is a news digest and has no value.
A report counts as research only if the reader gets three things: the **mechanism** (why it happens),
the **next step** (so what will happen), and the **observation point** (how I find out I am wrong).

---

## 1. Epistemology: the three questions

Put **every** data point and every judgment in the report through three questions.
This is the foundation of the whole system. Every later framework calls it.

### Question 1: Is this a cause, a process, or an outcome?

| Tag | Meaning | Examples | Research value |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[Cause]** | A precondition or structural constraint. Already locked in, but the consequences have not yet unfolded | Age structure, capacity under construction, signed long-term contracts, institutional arrangements, position on a technology curve | **Highest.** It determines the future and is almost irreversible |
| **[Process]** | A mechanism that is running now. You can watch the outcome being produced | Capacity utilisation, inventory turnover, credit origination pace, hiring and layoffs, order conversion, channel prices | **High.** It leads the outcome that will later be reported |
| **[Outcome]** | A result that has already happened. Already announced, already priced | Reported net income, CPI, GDP, share price, rating changes, news events | **Low.** Use it to calibrate and to falsify, never as the body of an argument |

**Hard rule (the executable version): the load-bearing chain of every core conclusion must contain at least one [Cause] or [Process].**
A conclusion supported only by [Outcome] (example: "last quarter's net income beat, so next quarter looks good") is downgraded to "Observation".

Warning: an earlier version of this rule read "pages of cause + process divided by pages of outcome must exceed X".
That check was fake-executable. The author tags both the numerator and the denominator, and the tagging standard can be loosened on the spot, so the self-check proves nothing.
The load-bearing chain check replaces it. The object under check is now a short list of 3-5 numbers that can be verified one by one.
The classification of an indicator follows the labels in `references/indicators.md`. If you disagree with that library, state why.

Warning: the most common error is **using an [Outcome] as a [Cause]**: explaining next quarter with last quarter's net income growth.
Net income is an outcome. It is generated by [Process] items (orders, capacity, prices, costs), and those are in turn determined by [Cause] items
(capital expenditure, contracts, technology route). Analysis must run cause, then process, then outcome. Never backwards.

### Question 2: Is this fixed, probabilistic, or unknown?

| Tag | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **[Fixed]** | Almost irreversible | Contract value already signed, capacity under construction and its start-up date, demographic structure, sunk cost, the debt maturity schedule that already exists |
| **[Probabilistic]** | Has a distribution; a probability can be estimated | Policy tendency, demand elasticity, competitor response, technology yield ramp speed |
| **[Unknown]** | Structurally unknowable | Timing of a geopolitical conflict, a disruptive technical breakthrough, an extreme policy reversal |

**The only source of excess insight is mismatch:**

- The market treats a [Probabilistic] or [Unknown] item as [Fixed]: **overconfidence**, which points to a short or underweight opportunity
  (example: treating company guidance as a contract, treating an institutional forecast as built capacity).
- The market treats a [Fixed] item as [Probabilistic]: **excess discounting**, which points to a long or overweight opportunity
  (example: cash flows under a signed ten-year contract valued like a cyclical stock).

**The core thesis of a report must land on one explicit mismatch point.** If you cannot find a mismatch, you have no view.
Write an honest "structure briefing" and do not manufacture a view.

### Question 3: Who sets the price on this chain, and what constrains them?

Price is not a function of truth. It is a function of **capital**. So ask:

1. **Who is the marginal buyer or seller?** (passive index, active long-only, hedge fund, strategic capital, retail, market maker)
2. **What constrains them?** (length of evaluation period, liability duration, risk stop-loss lines, redemption pressure,
   index inclusion rules, cost of leverage, regulatory capital charges)
3. **Under what conditions will they change behaviour?**

This question decides **"even if you are right, when does the market admit it"**.
A call that is right on research but wrong on timing by three years is a wrong call as a product.
The reader is someone who will put money behind a decision.

## 2. L0, the first-principles layer: five groups of slow variables

L0 is not for prediction. It is for **drawing the boundary of possibility**: which outcomes cannot happen at all under these constraints.
When a slow variable changes direction, the valuation anchor of every layer above it is replaced at once, and the market is usually slowest to react.

### L0-1 Productivity
Position on the technology S-curve (introduction, steep, saturated), slope of unit-cost decline, energy and compute cost, total factor productivity.
This decides **how big the pie can get**, and who is entitled to a seat.
Key question: is the cost curve still falling steeply, or has it flattened? Once it flattens, competition moves from technology to scale and channels.

### L0-2 Relations of production and distribution
Capital versus labour share of income, ownership structure, the relationship between platforms and workers, tax system and transfers.
This decides **how the pie is split**, and so where the long-run centre of the profit margin sits.
Key question: is today's high margin held up by a technology barrier, or by a distribution pattern that is temporarily favourable? The latter mean-reverts.

### L0-3 Institutional order
Property protection, regulatory framework, international rules (export controls, tariffs, technical standards, settlement systems, cross-border data).
This decides **the boundaries of the game**. It is the most underrated variable: when it changes, valuation does not move, the valuation **system** changes its anchor.
Key question: which institutional premises does this business depend on, and are they loosening or tightening?

### L0-4 Demographic cycle and "collective experience"
Two layers. The second is often ignored and extremely important:

- **Structural layer**: age structure, dependency ratio, household formation rate, labour supply. This sets the long-run floor for demand and for cost.
- **Experience layer**: **has this generation of market participants lived through this kind of cycle?**
  The same size of decline produces entirely different behaviour in a group that has seen a full downturn and a group that has not.
  A generation with no such experience, in its first downturn, will: admit it later, cut losses later, believe "this time is different" more,
  and more easily treat policy support as a permanent promise.

Key question: when did the dominant participants last go through a comparable cycle? If the answer is "never",
**volatility will be larger than in historical comparables, and confirmation of a turning point will be slower.**

### L0-5 Stage positioning
Development stage of the economy (industrialising, urbanising, post-industrial, ageing), position in the debt cycle, position on the industry S-curve,
and **historical analogies together with the conditions that invalidate them**.

Warning: historical analogy is the most dangerous tool in this framework. Using it requires **three invalidation conditions**, and each must meet all of these:
1. **Observable**: it points to a specific public data series or event. "The policy environment is different" is a correct platitude and does not count.
2. **Structural**: it describes a difference in mechanism (participant structure, constraints, institutional arrangement), not a difference in magnitude.
3. **Directed**: it states "if this condition holds, **which part** of the analogy no longer applies": duration, magnitude, or the way the cycle clears.

Three correct platitudes do not count. If you cannot write invalidation conditions that meet all three tests, treat the analogy as rhetoric.
It may not enter the conclusion chapter. It may appear only in the background chapter, marked "background reference only".

## 3. The transmission chain: L1 to L5

Five layers, plus three attributes that must be given explicitly: **lag, damping or amplification, and reflexive loops**.

```
L1 Capital & liquidity  --lag 3-12M-->  L2 Macro economy  --lag 6-18M-->  L3 Industry chain
                                                                               |
                                              +--------------------------------+
                                              v
                                        L4 Market pricing  <-- reflexive loop --+
                                              |                                 |
                                              v                                 |
                                        L5 Single-company micro ----------------+
```

| Layer | Core question | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| **L1 Capital / liquidity** | How much money, how expensive, how willing to take risk | The denominator of valuation, plus risk appetite |
| **L2 Macro economy** | Aggregate demand and supply, prices, the policy reaction function | The **total** of the earnings numerator |
| **L3 Industry chain** | Structure, bottlenecks, value distribution, capacity cycle | **How that total is split** along the chain |
| **L4 Market pricing** | How much is already priced, who holds the position, what consensus is | **How much spread is left** in your judgment |
| **L5 Single-company micro** | Can this company capture its share | Ability to deliver |

### Three attributes that must be written out

**1. Lag.** How long does transmission take between layers? Give a range and say what it rests on.
Example: a change in the policy rate reaches corporate capex decisions in 2-4 quarters typically; from capex to installed heavy-asset capacity,
2-4 years is only an order of magnitude. Sector-specific prior constants live in two places: the "capex cash flow" entry under L3-B in `references/indicators.md`,
and the L3 entry "the lag from capex to price does not transfer across industries" in `references/pitfalls.md`. The copper figures in both places
are aligned to the S&P Global basis (about 15-18 years from discovery; about 4-7 years of construction after final investment decision). When you cite them, say which segment you mean.
When writing the report, follow `references/industry.md` Step 2: replace the prior with the median measured interval of 3-5 completed projects in that industry.
Do not apply the constant directly.
**A transmission-chain analysis with no lag estimate is empty talk.** A right direction with the wrong timing still loses the reader money.

**2. Damping or amplification.** What buffers or amplifies the transmission?
Buffers: inventory, long-term contracts that lock prices, policy hedges, FX, subsidies.
Amplifiers: leverage, capacity rigidity (cannot be stopped), channel stuffing, mechanical buying and selling by passive capital.

**3. Reflexive loop.** Does the L4 price feed back and change L1-L3?
Typical loops: share price rises, financing cost falls, capex expands, capacity glut, prices collapse, share price falls.
Or: credit spreads tighten, highly levered firms survive, obsolete capacity is not cleared, the industry earns low returns for a long time.
**Wherever a self-reinforcing loop exists, state its breaking condition.** That condition is the turning point.

### Top-down and bottom-up must cross-check
The industry size computed top-down and the sum of the main companies' capacity or orders computed bottom-up must be compared. **If they do not match, something is wrong.**
Where did the gap go? Is one side's definition wrong, or is there a hidden assumption nobody has found? This cross-check often produces the hardest conclusion in the report.

## 4. From "analysis" to "tradable judgment"

Finishing the analysis does not make a report usable. This section covers **why**. **How to do it and what to deliver** is in `references/judgment.md` (the mandatory procedure).

Warning: of the four gates below, "consensus" and "mismatch" often cannot be evidenced with free data
(sell-side consensus is paid data, and the "market-implied verdict" cannot be observed directly). **Do not force an answer.**
Sections 1, 2 and 8 of `references/judgment.md` give the evidence methods and the degrade path. Follow those.

The four gates:

### 4.1 What is the consensus
Write the market consensus down in plain words first: the sell-side consensus numbers, the current valuation percentile, and **the assumptions implied by price**
(back-solve the current market cap into "implied revenue, free cash flow or share in N years").
If you do not know the consensus, you cannot know whether you differ from it.

### 4.2 Where do you differ, and where does the difference come from
| Source of difference | Meaning | Durability |
|---|---|---|
| **Information gap** | I hold public information others have not seen (buried in footnotes, filings, local announcements) | Short. It disappears once it spreads |
| **Interpretation gap** | Same information, a different reading framework (example: strip out a one-off gain and the conclusion reverses) | Medium. Depends on how good the framework is |
| **Timing gap** | Others will reach this conclusion too, but 2-4 quarters later (because they wait for data to be disclosed) | Most tradable. Use with an observation calendar |

### 4.3 Falsifiability
**Every core conclusion must carry one sentence: "If X happens, this judgment is wrong."**
X must be a publicly observable event or numeric threshold with a definite date.
A conclusion with no X is not a judgment, only an attitude. Delete it or downgrade it to "Observation".

### 4.4 The observation calendar
Arrange all the X items above and the key [Process] indicators on one timetable:
when it is disclosed, where to look, what reading counts as confirmation, what reading counts as falsification.
This is the most practical page of the report for the reader, and it is the reason to read the next report.

## 5. The eight components every report must have

For equity, industry and macro reports alike, all eight are required (layout positions are in the process files):

1. **Mechanism diagram.** One figure that shows the cause, process, outcome transmission path, with lags and reflexive loops marked.
2. **Evidence grading.** Tag every key number `[A]` primary disclosure, `[B]` authoritative media, `[C]` institutional estimate, `[D]` unverified.
   When two definitions conflict, **show both**. Never silently pick one.
3. **Certainty-layer table.** Split the core variables into [Fixed] / [Probabilistic] / [Unknown], and state which class the market currently treats each as (the mismatch point).
4. **Bull / bear comparison.** Three columns: "strongest bull case vs strongest bear case vs this report's judgment". Source the first two columns from the bull/bear debate held before the rating is set
   (step 2.5, in `references/pipeline/02-research.md`: `debate-bull.md` and `debate-bear.md`),
   and mark **which risks are already priced in** (a risk that is already priced is not a new reason to be bearish).
5. **Falsification conditions plus observation calendar.** See 4.3 and 4.4.
6. **Known limitations.** Data gaps, items not verified in time, and methodological boundaries, listed one by one
   (including the three standing entries, see section 8 of `references/judgment.md`).
   **Disclosing limitations raises credibility. Concealing them is the red line we cannot cross.**
7. **Misreadings section.** Widely repeated but wrong claims in the market, corrected one by one with the right reading.
   This is where the reader most directly feels that the report beats free content.
8. **Audit revision log** (produced by the post-draft audit): three columns, "original wording, revised wording, nature of change".
   If a report calls itself audited or revised, the whole meaning of that label is in this table. Without it, the reader has been shown no evidence that an audit took place.

> A report missing any of components 1-8 fails the pre-release self-check.
> It must also pass the **tradability final check** in section 9 of `references/judgment.md`: consensus; mismatch evidence; catalysts;
> rating basis (three-horizon outlook, the annual horizon settleable); the decision chapter's four items plus the five operating-research items;
> evidence-strength binding; settlement of the prior report's calls.

## 6. Quality red lines (a violation sends the report back for redo)

1. **No fabrication.** Every number must trace to a source with a URL. If you cannot get it, write "not verified". Never present a derived value as a measured one.
2. **No reversal of cause and outcome.** Do not use lagging indicators to explain the future (see section 1, question 1).
3. **No bare analogies.** Every historical analogy carries invalidation conditions (see L0-5).
4. **No hidden limitations.** Data gaps must be disclosed.
5. **No impersonation of a licensed firm.** No analyst signature or practice-licence number. A rating is a "relative research judgment, not a buy or sell order".
   Disclaimers and disclosures come from `assets/disclaimer.md` as written: not investment advice; a truthful statement of how the report was produced and reviewed
   (never claim a review that did not happen); every conflict-of-interest item written out one by one (write "none" where the answer is none);
   the conditional nature of forward-looking statements; and a correction mechanism.
6. **Conclusion strength may not exceed evidence strength.** The load-bearing chain of a core conclusion may not contain [D].
   Where it contains [C], run a ±30% sensitivity and show the conclusion does not flip (section 6 of `references/judgment.md`).
7. **No guessed probabilities.** Scenario probabilities may come from only three sources: historical frequency, market-implied values, or an explicit subjective assignment
   (which must state its basis and be sensitivity-tested). Defaulting to 25/50/25 counts as a violation of red line 1.
8. **Ratings must be settleable.** The annual horizon states the benchmark (with the reason for choosing it), the start date and a 12-month term; otherwise it cannot be falsified
   (section 4 of `references/judgment.md`). The monthly, quarterly and annual outlooks must all be present; the format is in `references/quality-bar.md`, "Rating and outlook".

| Stage | Which file |
|---|---|
| Choosing a topic, deciding report type | The pipeline map in `SKILL.md`, then `references/pipeline/01-kickoff.md` |
| Thinking through what to analyse | **This file** (the framework) |
| Running the research step by step | `references/equity.md` / `references/industry.md` / `references/macro.md` |
| **Turning analysis into a tradable judgment** | **`references/judgment.md`** (mandatory procedure plus the pre-release final check) |
| **Admission red lines and the pre-release landing checklist** | **`references/quality-bar.md`** (seven-stage process, three-horizon outlook, decision chapter, the seventh audit track on reader experience) |
| Looking up indicators and sources / measurement pitfalls | `references/indicators.md` / `references/pitfalls.md` |
| Writing, audit, translation, typesetting, release | `references/pipeline/03-writing.md` and the later pipeline steps |
| Disclaimer and conflict-of-interest text | `assets/disclaimer.md` (use as written) |

All three process files share this framework's cause / process / outcome tags, fixed / probabilistic / unknown tags, transmission chain and eight components.
They differ only in entry point and weight: macro starts from L0 and L1, industry chain starts from L3, and single-company work starts from L5 and checks back to L0.
