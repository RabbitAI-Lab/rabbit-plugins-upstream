# Leak Tracing, Tarps, Drying, and the Insurance Evidence Pack

## Why the drip point lies

Water is routed twice between sky and bucket:

1. **On the roof:** gravity and shingle courses move water sideways.
   Entry points cluster where roof geometry interrupts the shingle
   field — chimneys, valleys, vent/plumbing stacks, skylights, dormer
   flashing, eaves (ice dams in season). A missing tab 3 m uphill may
   present as a drip at the room's far corner.
2. **In the attic/ceiling void:** water runs the UNDERSIDE of the
   sheathing (surface tension) and the tops of joists before it finds a
   seam in the drywall to drop through. Ceiling drywall is also not
   sealed — it drinks, stains spread in rings, and the visible edge is
   usually downstream of the real entry.

Rule: **the stain is where water exits the building, not where it
entered.** Trace from above whenever the attic is accessible.

## Reading stains

| Sign | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Wet, dark, actively growing edge | Active leak, follow it now |
| Dry brown ring, solid center | Old episode (past storm) — still fix, different urgency |
| Multiple concentric rings | Repeating leak — flashing/valley suspect |
| Soft/spongy drywall | Saturated — structural failure is close; bucket first |
| Bulge | Standing water pooled on the drywall — release deliberately |

## The attic trace (flashlight, phone camera, chalk)

1. Find the drip from below; mark the ceiling spot; go up with a
   flashlight (headlamp better — hands free).
2. Walk ONLY joists/trusses (never the drywall ceiling — it's not a
   floor, and it's wet).
3. Look UP for the wet sheathing path — darker ply, drips, water
   beading on nail points (nail-back condensation vs leak: leaks wet
   the wood, condensation rusts nail tips in cold attics).
4. Chalk-mark the wet zone's uphill extent; photograph with the roof
   features visible in frame (the photo that includes the chimney
   flashing often solves the case).
5. Wet insulation: pull back a batt — water pools UNDER it against the
   ceiling. If insulation is wet, the ceiling below it is a mold clock
   (see drying).

If the attic path is inconclusive → tomorrow's hose test: one person
   inside watching, one outside running water LOW on the suspect
   feature 5 minutes at a time, working uphill until the drip answers.

## Emergency tarping — the cutoff discipline

GO if ALL: daylight or well-lit, roof dry, pitch ≤ 6/12, single story
(or safe access), someone home, harness/anchor available above 6 ft.
STOP if ANY: rain still falling hard, roof wet/frosted/mossy, pitch
>6/12, two+ stories, night, you're alone, or the leak zone is near
power lines. Emergency roofers exist exactly for the STOP cases — the
ER visit from a fall costs more than any roof.

Tarp rules:

- Extend ≥ 1 m past the damage in every direction; overlap courses
  like shingles — UPPER over LOWER.
- Run the tarp from the ridge (or a dry course above) downhill — never
  anchor mid-slope only.
- Anchor with furring strips/boards along edges and 2x4 "burritos"
  weighted over the ridge — **never nail through the shingles**; every
  nail hole is a future leak and a warranty fight.
- Pooling on a tarp = re-pitch it; standing water finds the tarp's own
  edge.
- Interior alternative (safer, underrated): in the ATTIC, plastic sheet
  draped to route water into a bucket, plywood wedge under the leak
  point. Nobody needs to be on a roof at midnight.

## Insurance evidence pack (photograph BEFORE mitigating)

Shot list, phone timestamps ON:

1. Wide exterior: roof, the storm context (branch on roof, hail line)
2. Room wide, then the stain close-up with a tape measure for scale
3. The bulge/drip in video 30 s (sound of dripping counts — narrate date)
4. Attic: wet path, chalk marks, the suspect feature in frame
5. Everything you touch during mitigation, again after (tarp placed,
   insulation pulled, ceiling opened)

Call distinctions:

- **Sudden vs gradual:** storm-torn shingles = sudden (covered, minus
  deductible); 10-year-old failing flashing = wear/maintenance
  (usually denied — but the storm damage ON TOP may still be covered;
  let the adjuster classify, don't pre-classify in the call).
- **Mitigate, don't repair:** insurers REQUIRE reasonable emergency
  mitigation (tarp, dry, extract) and reimburse it with receipts;
  permanent repairs BEFORE the adjuster inspection jeopardize the
  claim. Emergency stabilization ≠ replacement.
- **Renters:** landlord notified IN WRITING day-of (photo of the
  message); your policy is renters insurance — it covers belongings +
  sometimes displacement, not the building. The landlord's fight is
  with their insurer; your fight is documenting your property damage.

Keep a claim journal: who called, when, claim number, what promised.
Follow-up in writing every time a phone promise matters.

## The drying clock (mold at 24–48 h)

Sequence:

1. **Extract first:** shop-vac standing water, pull wet batts (bag and
   seal — carry them through the house in bags), open the ceiling over
   wet-insulation zones (drywall alone over a wet void = incubator).
2. **Air movers + dehumidifier:** 1 heavy LGR dehumidifier per ~40 m²
   of affected area, fans aimed across wet surfaces (not into the
   void), room closed to size the drying chamber.
3. **Measure:** pin meter or the screwdriver test (soft = wet). Log
   readings daily per spot. Target: drywall back to ≤ 15% moisture,
   two consecutive stable days before closing anything.
4. **Kill what won't dry:** ceiling tiles, compressed cellulose,
   carpet padding under wet carpet — replace, don't negotiate.
5. **Escalate to remediation** when: 48 h passed wet, area > 1 m² of
   suspected mold, musty smell persists after drying, or anyone in the
   house has respiratory issues. Remediation opens walls to the stud
   and costs multiples of drying — but hidden mold costs more.
6. **Close only after:** readings stable-dry, smell gone, humidity
   normal, AND the exterior entry actually fixed. Sealing wet framing
   is the mistake that repeats this whole file in 3 months.
