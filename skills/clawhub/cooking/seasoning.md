# Seasoning — Salt, Acid, Fat, Umami, Aromatics

Seasoning is a measurement problem disguised as a taste problem. The percentages below are the whole craft; the tasting protocol is how you land on them without a scale in the middle of a dish.

**Read `salt_type` from `~/Clawic/data/cooking/config.yaml`, and `## Household` in `memory.md`, before giving any salt figure**: the same teaspoon holds 3 g or 6 g depending on the jar, and the household's heat and salt tolerance is already recorded.

## The Salt Conversion Nobody Checks

| Salt | Grams per teaspoon | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fine sea salt / fine table salt | ~6 g | Densest; iodized versions can read faintly metallic in delicate dishes |
| Morton kosher | ~4.8 g | Flatter flakes, denser than Diamond |
| Diamond Crystal kosher | ~3 g | Hollow pyramids; half the weight of table salt in the same spoon |
| Flaky finishing salt (Maldon type) | ~2-3 g | Finishing only — it dissolves on the tongue, which is the entire point |
| Coarse sea salt | ~5 g | Varies most; weigh it |

A recipe written in Diamond Crystal and made with table salt is **twice as salty**. This single conversion is behind more inedible dishes than any technique. If the brand is unknown, weigh; if weighing is impossible, use half the volume and correct upward.

## Target Percentages by Weight

| Application | Salt as % of weight | Of what |
|---|---|---|
| Soups, sauces, braises, purées, stews | 0.8-1.0% | Finished weight, after reduction |
| Meat dry brine | 1.0% | Raw weight of the meat |
| Wet brine | 5-6% (50-60 g/L) | The brine, for 1-4 h |
| Bread dough | 1.8-2.2% | Flour weight |
| Fresh sausage | 1.5-2.0% | Meat weight |
| Vegetable ferment (kraut, kimchi) | 2.0% | Vegetable weight |
| Brine pickles | 3-5% | The brine |
| Pasta and blanching water | ~1% (10 g/L) | The water |
| Boiled potatoes and grains | 1% of the water | Salt the water, not the plate |

Formula: `salt (g) = weight (g) × target %`. Weigh the pot empty once and write the number in `## Kitchen`; every future braise then has its salt figure in one subtraction.

Tolerance: most palates detect a change of about 0.1 percentage points in a savory dish, and read below 0.6% as under-seasoned and above 1.4% as salty. That narrow band is why "season to taste" fails beginners — the target is a 0.4-point window, not a philosophy.

## When Salt Goes In

- **Early, in the liquid**: for anything that will absorb — grains, pasta, beans, potatoes, braises. This is the only chance to season the interior; salt at the end sits on the surface.
- **In advance, on meat**: 1% by weight, 45 minutes minimum (below that the surface is wet from drawn-out moisture at exactly the wrong moment), ideally 12-24 h uncovered in the fridge (`meat.md`).
- **At the end, on anything reduced**: Rule 5. A stock or a pan sauce doubles its concentration when it halves.
- **On the plate, as flakes**: for texture and a bright top note on steak, tomatoes, chocolate, and eggs. It is a different ingredient from dissolved salt.
- **Never blind into a dish holding cured or salty components** — anchovy, olives, capers, bacon, parmesan, soy, stock cubes, miso. Add those first, taste, then decide.

## Acid — The Most Under-Used Correction

Acid does what salt does for brightness, without adding salt. When a dish is already at 1% and still reads dull, the missing axis is almost always acid.

| Acid | Strength | Best in |
|---|---|---|
| Distilled vinegar | ~5% acetic | Pickles; too harsh for finishing |
| Wine, cider, sherry vinegar | 5-7% | Braises, dressings, deglazing |
| Balsamic | 6%, with sugar | Finishing, glazes — sweet as much as sour |
| Lemon, lime juice | ~5-6% citric | Anything fatty, fish, greens, at the end |
| Wine (cooking) | ~0.5-0.7% tartaric plus alcohol | Deglazing; needs to reduce or it tastes raw |
| Yogurt, buttermilk, crème fraîche | ~1% lactic | Creamy acidity; adds fat and acid at once |
| Tamarind, sumac, verjus, pickle brine | varies | Where lemon would be obvious |

Start at 1 tsp per litre and re-taste. Acid added at the end keeps its aroma; acid added early loses volatiles and just becomes sourness. Acid also **firms** — it slows the breakdown of pectin and starch, which is why tomatoes and wine go into beans and potatoes only after they are tender.

## Fat

Fat carries aroma compounds that water cannot, which is why a fat-free version of a good dish tastes hollow rather than lighter. Three jobs: carrying flavor, coating the palate (perceived richness), and emulsifying a sauce so it clings.

- Finish sauces with 10-20 g of cold butter per portion, off heat, swirled not stirred — it emulsifies and adds body without a thickener.
- Toasting spices in fat before liquid extracts fat-soluble aromatics; the same spices stirred into a watery pot at the end taste dusty.
- Chili heat (capsaicin), turmeric color, and most herb aromas are fat-soluble. Blooming them in oil for 30-60 seconds is not a flourish; it is extraction.

## Umami and Depth

| Source | What it brings | Watch |
|---|---|---|
| Soy sauce, fish sauce, miso, Worcestershire | Glutamate plus salt | Counts toward the salt budget — subtract it |
| Tomato paste, sun-dried tomato | Glutamate, sweetness | Fry the paste 1-2 min until it darkens, or it stays raw and metallic |
| Parmesan, aged cheese, rinds | Glutamate | A rind simmered in a soup is free depth |
| Dried mushroom, kombu | Guanylate, glutamate | Soaking liquid is the ingredient; strain the grit |
| Anchovy | Glutamate plus inosinate | Dissolves completely in hot fat; nobody tastes fish at 1-2 fillets per pot |
| Cured meat, bonito, dried shrimp | Inosinate | Combines multiplicatively with any glutamate source |
| MSG | Glutamate, no salt penalty | ~0.3-0.5% of dish weight; it is the same molecule the ingredients above deliver |

The synergy is the useful part: glutamate on its own is savory, glutamate plus a nucleotide (inosinate from meat or bonito, guanylate from dried mushroom) reads far more savory than the sum. Tomato plus anchovy, mushroom plus parmesan, dashi's kombu plus katsuobushi — every canonical pairing is this effect.

## Aromatics and Heat

- Whole spices toasted dry until fragrant (30-60 s, they burn in 90) then ground beat pre-ground by a wide margin; ground spices lose most of their aroma within a year of opening, which is why spice replacement is a `## Due` row.
- Hardy herbs (rosemary, thyme, bay, oregano) go in early and cook; delicate herbs (basil, cilantro, parsley, dill, mint, chives) go in off heat or they turn to hay.
- Dried herbs are ~1/3 the volume of fresh for the same effect, and the substitution only works for hardy herbs — dried basil and dried parsley are different, weaker ingredients, not concentrated ones.
- Black pepper's aroma is volatile: ground fresh at the end, not simmered for an hour.
- Chili heat builds and does not reverse. With mixed tolerances at the table, build the dish mild and serve the heat alongside as oil, flakes, or hot sauce (`spice_level` sets the default).
- Garlic: raw is pungent, sweated is sweet, browned is nutty, burnt is bitter within seconds. It goes in after the onion, not with it.

## The Tasting Protocol

1. **Taste the base** once the aromatics and liquid are in. Correct salt to roughly 70% of target here — there is more evaporation coming.
2. **Taste after reduction**, when the volume is final. Now correct to the full 0.8-1.0%.
3. **Taste before serving**, at serving temperature. Cold and lukewarm food reads less salty and less sweet than hot food, so a dish seasoned cold will be over-seasoned hot, and a chilled dish (gazpacho, potato salad, ice cream) needs *more* salt and sugar than it seems to at room temperature.
4. Between corrections, **reset the palate** — a sip of water, a bite of plain bread. Three tastes in a row all read the same because the palate adapts.
5. Taste with the thing it will be eaten with. A sauce tasted alone is always under-seasoned for pasta and over-seasoned for a plain protein.
