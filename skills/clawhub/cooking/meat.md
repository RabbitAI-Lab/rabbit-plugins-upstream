# Meat — Beef, Pork, Lamb, Poultry

The whole craft is one distinction: **tough and dry are opposite failures with opposite fixes.** Tough means collagen has not converted to gelatin and needs *more* time above 70°C. Dry means muscle fibers contracted and expelled water and needs *less* heat, or a lower target. Diagnosing which one is on the plate decides everything else.

## Cut Selection

Muscles that work are tough and flavorful and full of collagen; muscles that idle are tender and mild. That single axis predicts the method.

| Cut family | Collagen | Method | Target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loin, tenderloin, rib, sirloin, chicken breast | Low | Fast dry heat: sear, grill, roast hot | Doneness temperature, then stop |
| Chuck, brisket, short rib, shin, oxtail, shoulder, cheek | High | Slow moist or slow dry: braise, stew, smoke | 90-96°C internal, held |
| Chicken thigh, duck leg, lamb shoulder | Medium-high | Either, but reward is at 79-85°C+ | Well past the safety minimum |
| Flank, skirt, hanger, bavette | Medium, coarse grain | Fast and hot to medium-rare, then sliced thin across the grain | 52-54°C, slicing does the tenderizing |

A tough cut cooked to medium-rare is chewy. A tender cut cooked to 90°C is sawdust. Neither is a seasoning problem.

## Dry Brining

Salt at **1.0% of raw weight**, applied in advance, is the highest-return step in meat cookery: it seasons the interior, holds moisture through cooking, and dries the surface so it browns.

- **Timing**: 45 minutes minimum. Below that, salt has drawn moisture to the surface and not yet been reabsorbed — the worst possible moment to sear. 12-24 h uncovered on a rack in the fridge is the target for anything with skin.
- **Uncovered matters** for poultry and pork belly: the fridge is a dehumidifier, and a dry skin is the difference between crisp and leathery.
- **Wet brine** (5-6%, 1-4 h) adds water rather than concentrating flavor, which suits lean birds destined for long cooking and suits nothing that needs crisp skin. Choose one; never both.
- Pre-brined or "enhanced" supermarket meat (pork and poultry are frequently injected with a salt solution) is already at target — salting it again gives a 2% result. Check the label.

## Searing and Reverse-Sear

- **Sear-first** for anything under ~3 cm: surface dry, pan above 180°C, oil with a high smoke point, do not move it until it releases itself. Protein sticks until the crust forms and then lets go — pulling early tears it and leaves the crust in the pan.
- **Reverse-sear** for anything thicker or expensive: low oven (110-135°C) until 10-15°C below target, rest 10 minutes, then a 45-90 second sear per side. The interior is evenly cooked edge to edge instead of showing a grey band, the surface is drier so it browns faster, and the timing is forgiving.
- Butter goes in at the end of a sear, not at the start: it burns at ~150°C. Baste with butter, garlic and thyme for the final minute.
- Searing does not seal in juices. Seared meat loses slightly *more* moisture than unseared; the reason to sear is flavor and crust (SKILL.md Rule 3).

## Roasting

- **High-then-low** for crust with a controlled interior (230°C for 15-20 min, then 160-180°C to temperature). **Low-then-high** for evenness with a late crust. Do not run one temperature for a large roast and expect both.
- Whole poultry at 190-200°C convection: roughly 40 minutes per kilogram as a *planning* estimate only. Always finish by temperature — birds vary by shape more than by weight.
- Rack the meat so hot air reaches the underside; a roast sitting in its own juices boils on the bottom.
- Basting mostly cools the surface and slows browning. It is worth it for a lean bird's breast, not for a fatty joint.
- Add root vegetables to the tray only for the last portion of the cook, or they collapse; they also block airflow under the meat.

## Braising and Low-and-Slow

Collagen converts to gelatin meaningfully above ~70°C, and does so at a useful rate between 74°C and 96°C — the reason a braise takes hours rather than minutes. The finished target is **90-96°C internal**, held until a probe slides in with no resistance. That last phrase, not a clock, is the endpoint.

- Sear first for fond and color, then build the liquid on that fond (`sauces.md`).
- Liquid comes **halfway up**, not over the top. A submerged braise is a boil; the exposed surface is what develops flavor and lets you baste.
- Oven at 135-160°C, or a hob at a bare simmer where the surface only trembles. A rolling boil tightens the muscle fibers even while collagen converts — that is how a braise ends up both falling apart and dry.
- Braises are better the next day because gelatin and fat redistribute and the fat cap can be lifted off cold. Cool them safely first (`safety.md`).
- Pressure cooker: reaches ~121°C at full pressure, cutting a 3-hour braise to roughly 45-60 minutes, with less flavor development from evaporation. Finish uncovered to reduce (`equipment.md`).

## Poultry

- Breast and legs want different endpoints — 74°C and 79-85°C. Solutions: spatchcock (flatten, everything cooks evenly in ~45 min), cook the parts separately, or shield the breast with foil for the first stretch. Roasting a whole bird to a single number always sacrifices one part.
- Skin crisps only when it is dry and rendered. Dry-brine uncovered overnight, start at high heat, and never cover a roasted bird with foil while it rests if crisp skin matters.
- Pink meat near the bone in a bird that has reached 74°C is myoglobin and marrow pigment, not undercooking. The thermometer is the authority.
- **Do not rinse raw poultry**: it aerosolizes bacteria across roughly a metre of the sink and everything in it. Pat dry with paper towel (`safety.md`).
- Duck breast starts in a **cold** pan, skin down, to render the fat slowly over 8-12 minutes; a hot start seizes the skin around unrendered fat.

## Pork and Lamb

- Pork loin and chops at 63°C with a 3-minute rest are safe and juicy; Trichinella is inactivated by 58°C, and the 71°C target is a leftover from a solved problem. Ground pork still needs 71°C.
- Pork shoulder and belly are collagen cuts: 90-96°C, hours, no shortcuts.
- Lamb behaves like beef for tender cuts and like pork shoulder for the collagen ones. Lamb fat carries the flavor most people either love or dislike — trimming it changes the dish more than trimming beef fat does.
- Crackling: score the skin without cutting into the fat, dry it hard, salt it, and give it a blast at 230°C at the start or the end. Moisture is the only thing that prevents it.

## Ground Meat

- Grinding moves surface bacteria throughout, so **71°C/160°F is the whole batch's minimum**, with no rare interior available. Only meat ground in-house from a whole cut immediately before cooking changes that calculation, and only for the person making it.
- Fat content decides texture: ~20% fat for burgers and meatballs, ~10-15% for sauces where fat gets skimmed anyway. Lean ground beef makes dry burgers no technique fixes.
- Handle it as little as possible for burgers — compaction makes them springy. Salt the *outside* just before cooking; salt mixed through the mass dissolves myosin and produces a sausage texture. That texture is exactly what you want for meatballs and kofta, which is why those are mixed hard and burgers are not.
- Brown ground meat in batches in a wide pan; a crowded pan of mince at 100°C is grey and never develops fond.

## Resting and Slicing

- Steaks and chops: 5-10 minutes, tented loosely. Roasts and whole birds: 20-30 minutes. Tight foil traps steam and softens any crust.
- Rest on a rack, not on the plate you will serve on, so the underside is not sitting in liquid.
- **Slice across the grain**, always, and thinner for coarser-grained cuts. Flank and skirt sliced with the grain are inedible; the same cut sliced across at 3-4 mm is tender.
- Carve a roast only when it is being served — sliced meat cools and dries within minutes.
