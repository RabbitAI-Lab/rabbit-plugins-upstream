# Eggs and Dairy — Custards, Emulsions, Cheese

Every egg and dairy failure is a temperature ceiling being crossed. Egg white sets at 62-65°C, yolk at 65-70°C, whole-egg mixtures scramble above ~85°C, cheese breaks above ~82°C, and cream curdles when boiled with acid. Knowing the ceiling turns each of these from a knack into a number.

**Read `~/Clawic/data/health/profile.md` before proposing anything with raw or lightly cooked egg or unpasteurized dairy**: pregnancy, immunosuppression, egg allergy, and lactose intolerance all change the answer, and severity decides whether a shared whisk matters.

## Setting Temperatures

| Preparation | Sets / breaks at | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Egg white | Begins 62°C, firm 65°C | — |
| Egg yolk | Thickens 65°C, firm 70°C | Jammy yolk: hold the egg at 63-65°C |
| Whole beaten egg | Sets 73-80°C | Scrambled: pull at 63-65°C, carryover finishes it |
| Stirred custard (crème anglaise) | Thickens 80-84°C, scrambles above 85°C | Pull at 80°C; it coats a spoon and holds a drawn line |
| Baked custard, quiche, flan | Sets 80-85°C | Pull at 79-82°C with a slight wobble at the centre |
| Cheesecake | — | 65-70°C centre; cracks come from overbaking and fast cooling |
| Hollandaise | Breaks above 85°C | Work at 65-75°C |
| Cheese sauce | Proteins tighten above 82°C | Add cheese off heat |
| Milk | Skins at 70°C, scorches on a dry pan bottom | Stir, and use a heavy pan |

Adding starch (1-2% of the liquid weight) to a custard raises its scrambling ceiling substantially — it is why pastry cream can be boiled and crème anglaise cannot.

## Eggs, Method by Method

**Boiled** — start in already-boiling water for peelable, consistent results, and drop straight into ice water. From boiling, at fridge temperature, large eggs: 6 min soft with a liquid yolk, 7-8 jammy, 9-10 fudgy, 12-13 fully set. Add ~1 minute above 1,500 m altitude, where water boils cooler. Fresh eggs peel badly because the pH of the white is still low; a week-old egg peels cleanly, and steaming instead of boiling helps more than any vinegar or salt trick.

**Scrambled** — two schools, both valid. Low and slow (French): constant stirring off and on the heat, small curds, 8-10 minutes, pull while visibly wet. Hot and fast (diner): large curds, 60-90 seconds, pull while wet. Both pull *early*: carryover finishes them, and eggs that look done in the pan are rubber on the plate. Salt in advance (15 min before) gives a more tender result; adding milk or cream mostly dilutes and can make them weep.

**Poached** — the freshest egg available, because an old white spreads. Barely simmering water, a splash of vinegar to firm the outer white, a gentle vortex, 3 minutes. Straining the egg through a sieve first removes the watery outer white that makes the wisps.

**Omelet** — French: constant agitation, rolled, no colour, ~2 minutes. American: set, folded, lightly coloured. Filling goes in only after the egg has set enough to hold it, or it weeps.

**Fried** — sunny side up over medium, basted with the pan fat; crispy-edged over high heat in a generous amount of oil.

**Frittata and quiche** — start on the hob, finish in a 160-175°C oven; a hot oven curdles the custard into a sponge. Roughly 1 whole egg per 120 ml dairy for a quiche that slices cleanly.

**Meringue** — whites at room temperature, an absolutely fat-free bowl (a speck of yolk prevents foaming), 1/8 tsp cream of tartar per white for stability, sugar added slowly once soft peaks form. French: raw, baked. Swiss: whites and sugar heated to 71°C over a bain-marie, then whipped — stable and safe. Italian: 118°C sugar syrup beaten in — the most stable. Overwhipped whites go grainy and then collapse; there is no fix but starting again.

## Emulsions

An emulsion is fat suspended in water (or the reverse) held by an emulsifier — lecithin from egg yolk, mustard, or the proteins in dairy. It breaks when the fat arrives faster than the emulsifier can coat it, or when heat or cold destabilizes it.

| Sauce | Base | Fat | Ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mayonnaise | 1 yolk + acid + mustard | Up to ~200 ml oil per yolk | Room temperature; splits if the oil is added fast |
| Aioli | Garlic paste + yolk | Olive oil, often cut with a neutral oil | Blending whole olive oil can turn it bitter |
| Hollandaise | Yolks + reduction or lemon | 100-120 g clarified butter per yolk | 85°C |
| Beurre blanc | Reduced wine and shallot | Cold butter, cube by cube | Never boil after the butter goes in |
| Pan sauce mounted with butter | Reduced pan juices | 10-20 g cold butter per portion | Off heat only |
| Vinaigrette | Mustard, honey, or yolk | 3 parts oil to 1 acid | Holds hours with an emulsifier, minutes without |
| Carbonara | Yolk, pecorino, pasta water | Rendered guanciale fat | Off heat; residual pan heat is enough and anything more scrambles it |

Repairs for all of them are in `rescue.md`. The prevention is identical everywhere: build on the base, add the fat slowly, and control the temperature.

## Cream, Milk, and Yogurt

- **Whipping**: cream needs ≥30% fat (double/heavy cream). Cold bowl, cold cream. Soft peaks for folding, stiff peaks for piping; ten seconds past stiff is butter. Over-whipped cream is rescued by folding in a little fresh liquid cream.
- **Boiling with acid** curdles. Add dairy off the boil, or stabilize with a starch slurry (1 tsp cornstarch per 250 ml) before the acid goes in.
- **Tempering**: to add cold dairy or eggs to something hot, ladle the hot liquid into the cold one while whisking, then return the mixture. The reverse — cold into hot — is how curdled soup happens.
- **Yogurt in hot dishes**: full-fat only, tempered, off the boil. Low-fat yogurt splits reliably.
- **Reduction**: cream reduces to a sauce; milk breaks. Half a cup of cream reduced by a third has more body than any amount of milk.
- **Buttermilk substitute**: 1 tbsp lemon juice or white vinegar per 240 ml of milk, rested 10 minutes. It supplies the acid that reacts with baking soda; it does not supply buttermilk's thickness (`substitutions.md`).
- **Brown butter**: melt, foam, then watch the milk solids at the bottom go golden and smell nutty at around 150-160°C. It goes from brown to burnt in under a minute; pour it out of the pan immediately.
- **Ghee and clarified butter**: milk solids removed, so the smoke point rises to ~250°C and it keeps for months (`heat.md`).

## Cheese

- Melting depends on age and moisture. Young, high-moisture cheeses (mozzarella, young cheddar, Gruyère, fontina, raclette) melt smoothly. Aged, low-moisture cheeses (parmesan, aged cheddar, pecorino) have tightly bound proteins and break into strings and oil.
- **Sauce rule**: off heat, cheese in handfuls, whisk between. Around 82°C the proteins tighten and expel fat.
- A starch stabilizes: 1 tsp cornstarch tossed with 200 g grated cheese, or a béchamel base, or the sodium citrate approach (roughly 2-3% of the liquid weight) that keeps even aged cheese smooth.
- Pre-grated cheese is coated in anti-caking starch and cellulose, which helps a sauce marginally and hurts a melt on top.
- Salt: hard cheeses run 1.5-2% salt and count toward the dish's budget (`seasoning.md`).
- Serve cheese at room temperature — 30-60 minutes out of the fridge. Cold suppresses aroma more than any other variable on a cheese board.

## Ice Cream and Frozen Custards

- Base: crème anglaise cooked to 80-84°C, chilled thoroughly, then churned. Fat 10-16%, sugar 14-18% of the base by weight — sugar is not only sweetness, it depresses the freezing point and is what makes it scoopable.
- Too hard out of the freezer means not enough sugar, fat, or alcohol; too soft or icy means too much sugar or a base that was not cold before churning.
- Chill the base to below 4°C before churning. A warm base makes large ice crystals, which is the entire definition of icy ice cream.
