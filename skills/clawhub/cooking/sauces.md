# Sauces, Stocks, Braising Liquid, and Thickening

A sauce is four decisions: what liquid, what thickens it, what enriches it, and when it gets seasoned. Get the order wrong — season before reducing, thicken before tasting — and no amount of correction later recovers it.

**Before building a sauce from a past dish**, read `## Repertoire` in `~/Clawic/data/cooking/memory.md`: a sauce that split or over-reduced here before will have its cause and correction already recorded, and repeating the diagnosis costs more than reading it.

## Stock

| Stock | Ratio | Time | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chicken | 1 kg bones : 2 L water | 3-4 h | Roast the bones first for brown stock, straight in for white |
| Beef / veal | 1 kg : 2 L | 6-8 h | Knuckle and marrow bones give the gelatin |
| Fish | 1 kg frames : 2 L | 30-45 min | Bitter beyond 45 minutes; use non-oily fish |
| Vegetable | 1 kg : 2 L | 45 min | No brassicas; they dominate |
| Shellfish shells | 500 g : 1.5 L | 20-30 min | Roast the shells first; the highest-value scrap in the kitchen |

- **Never boil.** A bare simmer, surface just trembling. Boiling emulsifies fat into the liquid and turns it cloudy and greasy, and there is no repair.
- **Never salt a stock.** It is an ingredient that will be reduced later (SKILL.md Rule 5).
- Skim the grey foam in the first 20 minutes; it is coagulated protein and it will cloud the stock.
- Add aromatics in the last hour — a mirepoix simmered for six hours tastes of nothing.
- A good stock **gels when cold**. That is the visible test for collagen extraction, and gelatin is what gives a sauce body without any thickener at all.
- Cool it safely: it is the classic danger-zone offender at 4 litres in a deep pot (`safety.md`).
- Store: 4-5 days refrigerated, 3-6 months frozen. Reduced to a quarter of its volume it freezes as cubes that reconstitute.

## Thickeners

| Thickener | Ratio per 240 ml liquid | Activates | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roux (flour + equal fat) | 15 g light · 30 g medium · 45 g thick | Cooked in, simmered 2-3 min | Opaque, stable, reheats; darker roux thickens less per gram |
| Cornstarch slurry | 7-8 g starch in 15 ml cold water | Full power at ~95°C | Glossy, clear; breaks down with long boiling or strong acid |
| Arrowroot / tapioca | Same as cornstarch | ~80°C | Clear and glossy, freeze-stable, but goes slimy if boiled |
| Beurre manié | Equal soft butter and flour, whisked in cold | Immediately, then simmer 2 min | Last-minute correction; adds shine |
| Reduction | — | Time | Concentrates flavor *and* salt (Rule 5) |
| Purée of the dish's own solids | A ladleful, blended, returned | Immediately | Adds no new flavor — usually the best answer |
| Egg-yolk liaison | 1 yolk per 240 ml, tempered | 80-84°C, never boiled again | Velvety; will scramble if it boils (`eggs-and-dairy.md`) |
| Gelatin from stock | — | — | Body without any starch; the reason good stock matters |
| Bread, nuts, ground almonds | 20-40 g | Blended | Traditional in romesco, ajoblanco, picada |

Two conversions worth memorizing: **cornstarch has about twice the thickening power of flour by weight**, and a **dark roux thickens roughly half as much as a blonde one** because toasting breaks down the starch — a gumbo needs far more dark roux than a béchamel needs blonde.

Lumps come from temperature shock, not from bad luck: add warm liquid to hot roux gradually while whisking, or whisk cold starch into cold liquid before heating. Never cold liquid into hot roux, and never dry starch into hot liquid.

## Pan Sauces

The five-minute sauce that makes a seared protein look deliberate:

1. Sear the protein, remove it, pour off excess fat but keep the fond (`heat.md`).
2. Sweat a shallot or garlic in the residual fat, 30-60 seconds.
3. Deglaze with 60-120 ml of wine, stock, or vinegar, scraping the fond loose.
4. Reduce by half to two-thirds. This is where the flavor concentration happens.
5. Off heat, whisk in 10-20 g of cold butter per portion, plus any resting juices from the protein.
6. **Season now.** Acid last: a squeeze of lemon or a splash of vinegar after the butter.

If the sauce turns oily, it was boiled after the butter went in. Off heat, a splash of cold liquid, whisk hard (`rescue.md`).

## Braising Liquid

- Liquid comes halfway up the meat, never over it. The exposed surface keeps browning and the liquid concentrates into the sauce (`meat.md`).
- Deglaze the searing fond into the braising liquid — that fond *is* the sauce's backbone.
- Aromatics that go the distance: onion, carrot, celery, bay, thyme stems, tomato paste fried until it darkens. Aromatics that go in at the end: fresh herbs, citrus zest, vinegar.
- Finishing a braise: lift out the meat, skim the fat (easiest when the braise has been chilled and the fat cap lifts off solid), reduce the liquid hard, then adjust seasoning and acid. Most home braises are under-reduced and under-acidified.
- A braise held overnight is better: gelatin sets, fat separates cleanly, and the flavors equalize. Cool it in shallow containers first (`safety.md`).

## The Mother Sauces, Usefully

| Sauce | Base | What it becomes | The trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Béchamel | Milk + blonde roux | Mornay (cheese), soufflé base, lasagne, croquettes | Cook the roux 2 minutes or it tastes of raw flour; infuse the milk with bay and onion |
| Velouté | White stock + blonde roux | Suprême, allemande, chicken pot pie | Only as good as the stock — there is nowhere for a weak stock to hide |
| Espagnole / demi-glace | Brown stock + dark roux, reduced | Bordelaise, Robert, madeira | Hours of reduction; a well-gelled brown stock reduced hard is the shortcut |
| Tomato | Tomato, aromatics, time | Everything | Fry the paste; sugar is a correction for bad tomatoes, not an ingredient |
| Hollandaise | Yolk + butter emulsion | Béarnaise, maltaise, mousseline | 85°C ceiling (`eggs-and-dairy.md`) |

Modern kitchens mostly build sauces from a well-made stock plus reduction plus a mounting fat, and reach for roux only where the opacity and stability are wanted.

## Tomato, Pesto, and Cold Sauces

- Tomato sauce: fry the paste until it darkens (1-2 min) before adding liquid; simmer uncovered 30-45 min for a fresh sauce, 2-3 h for a ragù. Sugar is a repair for under-ripe tomatoes; a grated carrot does the same job with less obviousness. Acid balances a canned-tomato sauce more often than sugar does.
- Emulsified pasta sauce: the pasta cooking water is the emulsifier — starch plus salt. Finish the pasta in the pan with the sauce and a ladle of that water, tossing hard, for 30-60 seconds. That toss, not cream, is what makes a sauce cling (`rice-and-pasta.md`).
- Pesto: basil bruises and oxidizes with heat and blade friction; a mortar or short pulses keep it green. Salt, garlic, nuts, cheese, then oil last.
- Chimichurri, salsa verde, gremolata: raw herb, acid, oil, aromatics — the answer whenever a rich dish is missing brightness and there is no time to build anything (`seasoning.md`).
- Cold emulsions (mayonnaise, aioli, remoulade) are in `eggs-and-dairy.md`.

## Gravy

Roast drippings, fat separated and measured, then equal flour by weight cooked in for 2 minutes, then stock whisked in warm at roughly 240 ml per 15 g of flour for a pourable gravy. Deglaze the roasting tin over direct heat first — everything worth having is stuck to it. Season only after it has reduced to the consistency you want, and finish with the resting juices from the meat, which are the single most concentrated flavor available.

## Soups

- **Purée soups**: sweat the aromatics, cook the main vegetable in just enough stock to cover, blend, then thin to the consistency wanted. Blending before thinning gives control; the reverse gives soup that has to be reduced.
- Blending hot liquid expands violently — fill the jug half full and vent the lid, or use a stick blender in the pot.
- **Brothy soups**: the broth is the dish. Everything else is garnish added late so it keeps its texture.
- **Creamy without cream**: a starchy potato, white beans, bread, cashews, or a roux. Fat gives mouthfeel; starch gives body. Most "needs cream" soups need salt and acid.
- Seasoning: soups are served hotter than they are tasted while cooking, and salt reads stronger hot — taste at serving temperature (`seasoning.md`).
