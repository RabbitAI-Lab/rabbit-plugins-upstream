# Frying — Deep, Shallow, and the Coating

Frying is boiling in oil. The oil is a heat-transfer medium at 175-190°C, and the food's own moisture leaving it is what makes the bubbles. Everything that goes wrong is one of two things: **the oil was not hot enough**, or **too much food went in at once** — which is the same failure, one step earlier.

**Read `## Kitchen` in `~/Clawic/data/cooking/memory.md` before a deep fry**: whether there is an extractor, how much oil the pot holds, and how many times the current oil has been used are all recorded there, and the last one decides whether the batch will taste of old fryer.

## The Temperature Window

| Temperature | Use |
|---|---|
| 150-165°C | First fry of double-fried chips, confit, low-and-slow renders |
| 170-180°C | Chicken and anything thick that must cook through — the workhorse setting |
| 180-190°C | Second fry, tempura, fritters, breaded thin cutlets, anything already cooked |
| 190-200°C | Very quick items only; most oils are near their limit |
| Below 160°C | Food absorbs oil instead of expelling steam — the definition of greasy |
| Above 200°C | Surface burns before the interior cooks; oil degrades fast |

Use a thermometer. Without one: a cube of bread browns in about 60 seconds at 180°C, and a wooden chopstick or spoon handle held in the oil throws a steady stream of small bubbles.

## Temperature Recovery — the Number Nobody Manages

Adding cold food crashes the oil temperature; how far it crashes is set by the ratio of food to oil.

- **Keep each batch at or below ¼ of the oil's weight.** 2 litres of oil takes about 400-500 g of food per batch.
- Expect a drop of 10-30°C per batch, and **wait for recovery** before the next one. Frying into oil that has not recovered is how batch three comes out pale and greasy while batch one was perfect.
- Start 10-15°C above target so the drop lands in the window.
- A heavy pot (cast iron, thick-based) holds temperature far better than a thin one. Thermal mass is the whole game.
- Fill the pot no more than halfway: oil expands, foams up with wet food, and a boil-over onto a burner is a fire.

## Coatings

| Coating | Build | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Naked / dusted | Cornstarch or rice flour dusting only | Light, crisp; for wet items like squid |
| Standard breading | Flour → egg → breadcrumb | Sturdy, holds sauce; the schnitzel and cutlet standard |
| Panko | Same, coarse Japanese crumb | Crispest and lightest of the crumbs |
| Tempura batter | Ice-cold sparkling water + low-protein flour, mixed lumpy | Delicate, lacy; must be used within minutes |
| Beer batter | Beer + flour + leavening | Puffy, sturdy, for fish |
| Buttermilk dredge | Buttermilk soak → seasoned flour, pressed in with a few drops of the liquid | Craggy fried-chicken crust |
| Double dredge | Flour → liquid → flour again | Thickest crust; for anything that needs long frying |

Rules that apply to all of them:

- **Dry-wet-dry, and dry means dry.** Pat the food, then flour, then egg, then crumb. Wet food sheds its coating in the oil, every time.
- Press the coating on and **rest breaded items 10-20 minutes** in the fridge before frying; the coating hydrates and grips.
- Use one hand for dry and one for wet, or both hands end up breaded.
- Cornstarch or rice flour in the dredge (25-50% of the flour) gives a crisper, less bready crust, because neither develops gluten.
- Baking powder in a dredge (about 1 tsp per 120 g) raises the pH and produces a more blistered, crunchier surface.
- Tempura batter must stay cold and barely mixed: lumps are correct, and the moment it warms and develops gluten it turns bready.

## Double Frying

The technique behind good chips, twice-fried chicken, and most restaurant crunch:

1. First fry at 150-165°C until cooked through but pale. Moisture leaves, the interior sets, the surface dries.
2. Rest at least 10 minutes — up to hours, or refrigerate, or freeze.
3. Second fry at 185-190°C for 1-3 minutes to brown and crisp.

It works because the two goals — cooking through and crisping the surface — need different temperatures, and trying to hit both in one pass makes a burnt outside and a raw middle.

Chips specifically: a waxy-to-medium potato, cut, rinsed of surface starch, then par-boiled or soaked, dried hard, then double-fried. A 1% vinegar addition to the par-boil water keeps them from collapsing.

## Shallow and Pan Frying

- Oil 5-10 mm deep, one layer of food, turned once. Same temperature logic, less thermal mass, so batches must be smaller.
- Add food away from you so any splash goes away from you.
- Shallow frying browns unevenly at the oil line; press thin cutlets flat or spoon oil over the top.
- Butter cannot deep-fry (smoke point ~150°C) but shallow-fries well when mixed with oil, or clarified (`heat.md`).

## Draining and Holding

- Drain on a **rack**, never on paper on a plate. Paper traps steam under the food and makes the bottom soggy within a minute.
- Season **immediately** out of the oil, while the surface is hot and slightly oily — salt adheres then and will not later.
- Hold in a 90-100°C oven on a rack for up to 30 minutes. Do not cover, and do not stack.
- Fried food is at its best inside 5 minutes and declines steeply after 20. Sequence the meal so it is last (`equipment.md`).

## Oil Management

- Best oils: refined peanut, sunflower, rice bran, canola, or refined olive — high smoke point, neutral, and they hold up to repeated heating. Extra-virgin olive oil fries fine at the lower end of the window but is expensive and loses its aroma.
- **Reuse**: cool, strain through a fine sieve or coffee filter, store in a sealed container away from light. Roughly 3-6 batches for clean items like chips; discard after 1-2 batches of fish or strongly spiced coatings, which pass their flavor on.
- **Discard when** the oil is dark, smells rancid or fishy, foams around the food, smokes below its normal point, or has become viscous. All five are signs the oil has broken down.
- Never pour oil down the drain. Cool it, seal it in its original container, and dispose of it with solid waste or at a collection point.
- **Fire**: oil fires are smothered, never watered. Lid on, heat off, leave it covered until completely cool. A wet item dropped into hot oil, or water on an oil fire, produces a violent steam explosion.

## Air Frying

An air fryer is a small, fast convection oven — it does not fry, and the crust it produces is drier and less rich. It is genuinely excellent for reheating already-fried food, for frozen items, and for small batches of vegetables and chicken pieces.

- Conversion from a conventional oven recipe: **reduce the temperature by 15-25°C and the time by about 20%**, then check early.
- Toss food in 1-2 tsp of oil; without any it dehydrates rather than browning.
- One layer, gaps between pieces. A crowded basket is a steamer with a fan.
- Reheating fried food: 180°C for 3-5 minutes beats a microwave by an enormous margin.

## Diagnostics

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pale and greasy | Oil below ~160°C, or too much per batch | Fewer pieces, wait for recovery, verify with a thermometer |
| Dark outside, raw inside | Oil too hot, or pieces too thick | Lower to 165-170°C, or cook through first and crisp second |
| Coating fell off | Food was wet, or the breading was not pressed and rested | Dry hard, press, rest 10-20 min in the fridge |
| Batter slid off in sheets | Batter too thin, or the surface too smooth and wet | Dust with flour or cornstarch before the batter |
| Oil foaming violently | Wet food, or degraded oil | Dry the food; if it persists with dry food, the oil is finished |
| Food sticking to the pot | Dropped onto the base before the surface set | Lower gently and hold for two seconds before releasing; do not stir for the first 30 seconds |
| Kitchen full of smoke | Oil above its smoke point, or an old oil whose smoke point has fallen | Lower the heat, change the oil, open a window |
| Crisp for two minutes then soft | Drained on paper, stacked, or covered | Rack, single layer, uncovered, serve immediately |
