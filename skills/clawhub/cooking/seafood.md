# Seafood — Fish and Shellfish

Fish has almost no connective tissue and very short muscle fibers, so it goes from raw to overcooked in a window roughly a third as wide as meat's. Every technique here is about narrowing the target and getting out early.

**Read `~/Clawic/data/health/profile.md` before any shellfish, and `## Household` in `~/Clawic/data/cooking/memory.md`**: shellfish allergy is among the most common severe food allergies and the most likely to be cross-contaminated by a shared pan, a shared fryer, or a stock.

## Doneness

| Item | Pull | Serves at | Cue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Salmon, trout, arctic char | 47-50°C | 49-52°C medium-rare, 54-57°C medium | White albumin on the surface means it went past 55°C |
| Tuna, swordfish | 40-45°C | 43-49°C rare to medium-rare | Cooked through, both are dry — this is a texture choice, not safety |
| Cod, haddock, halibut, sea bass, snapper | 57-60°C | 60-63°C | Flakes with gentle pressure but still looks moist and slightly translucent at the centre |
| Whole fish, on the bone | 57°C at the spine | 60°C | The flesh lifts cleanly off the backbone |
| Shrimp, prawns | 47-49°C | 52-54°C | A loose `C`; an `O` is overcooked and rubbery |
| Scallops | 49°C | 52-54°C | Opaque at the edge, translucent centre |
| Squid, octopus | — | Either <2 min or >45 min | The middle is the rubber zone; there is no medium |
| Mussels, clams | — | Until they open, then off | Two minutes past opening turns them to erasers |
| Lobster | 55-60°C tail | 60°C | Tail meat opaque; overcooking makes it fibrous |

The USDA's single figure for fish is 63°C/145°F; most of the numbers above are lower and are texture-driven choices for whole-muscle fish from a trusted source. `doneness_policy: usda` means give 63°C and say what it costs; the risk calculus changes for pregnancy, immunosuppression, and the very young or old (`safety.md`).

## Buying and Freshness

- Whole fish: clear, domed eyes; bright red gills; firm flesh that springs back; a smell of clean sea and nothing else. Ammonia or "fishy" means past it — that smell is trimethylamine, a decomposition product, and it never improves with cooking.
- Fillets: translucent and glossy, not chalky or gaping. Gaps between muscle segments mean it was frozen badly or is old.
- **Frozen-at-sea is usually fresher than "fresh"** at an inland counter, where "fresh" often means thawed. Ask; a thawed fillet must not be refrozen raw.
- Shellfish alive: mussels and clams close when tapped. Discard any that stay open before cooking. After cooking, one that stayed shut is usually just tightly hinged, not dangerous — prise it, smell it, decide.
- Live crustaceans should be lively. A limp, room-temperature lobster has been dead long enough to matter, and its digestive enzymes start on its own flesh quickly.
- Anything to be eaten raw needs to have been frozen to the parasite-kill standard (roughly −20°C for 7 days, or −35°C for about 15 hours) — that, not the shop's label, is what makes fish "sushi grade" (`safety.md`).

## Getting the Skin Crisp

1. Dry the skin properly: paper towel, then 20-40 minutes uncovered in the fridge, or salt it lightly and blot again.
2. Score the skin on thick-skinned fish (sea bass, snapper) so it does not curl. Two or three shallow cuts.
3. Hot pan, thin film of oil, skin down, and **press flat for the first 20 seconds** with a spatula so the whole skin contacts the pan as it contracts.
4. Leave it. 70-80% of the total cook happens on the skin side; the fish visibly turns opaque from the bottom up.
5. Flip for the last 30-60 seconds, or finish it under a grill without flipping at all.

Sticking is almost always a wet surface or a pan that was not hot enough. Fish, like meat, releases when the crust forms.

## Method Selection

| Method | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Pan-sear | Skin-on fillets, scallops, thick steaks | Needs a dry surface and a hot metal pan |
| Roast, 200-220°C | Whole fish, thick fillets, tray bakes | 10-12 min per 2.5 cm of thickness as an estimate; finish by temperature |
| Slow-roast, 120-140°C | Salmon, thick white fish | Very forgiving — the window widens from two minutes to ten |
| Poach in fat or stock, 60-70°C | Delicate fillets, tuna confit | The liquid temperature *is* the doneness target; it cannot overcook |
| Steam | Whole fish, dumplings, shellfish | Keeps every drop of moisture; no browning at all |
| En papillote | Fillets with aromatics | The parcel steams; open it at the table for the aroma |
| Deep-fry | Battered white fish, calamari, whitebait | 180-190°C, small batches (`frying.md`) |
| Grill over fire | Oily fish, whole fish, prawns | Oil the fish not the grate, use a basket for delicate fish (`grilling.md`) |
| Cure | Salmon, sea trout | Salt-and-sugar cure draws water out; see below |

## Shellfish Specifics

- **Mussels and clams**: purge clams in salted water for 20-30 minutes to release grit; mussels need debearding just before cooking, not hours ahead — debearding kills them. Steam in a covered pot with a splash of wine over high heat for 4-6 minutes.
- **Shrimp**: shells make stock in 20 minutes and are the difference between a good bisque and a beige one. Brining shrimp briefly (a 3-5% solution for 15-30 min, optionally with a little baking soda) gives a noticeably snappier texture.
- **Scallops**: buy "dry" scallops if the choice exists; "wet" ones are soaked in a phosphate solution, weep in the pan, and will not brown. Dry them hard and sear 90 seconds a side.
- **Squid and octopus**: the two-speed rule is real. Under two minutes at high heat, or 45-90 minutes of gentle simmering. Anything between is rubber.
- **Crab and lobster**: kill humanely and cook promptly; the meat degrades fast. Steam rather than boil for better texture and less waterlogging.

## Curing and Raw Preparations

- **Gravlax-style cure**: equal parts salt and sugar by weight, roughly 15-20% of the fillet's weight, 24-48 h refrigerated under light weight, then rinsed and dried. The fillet loses moisture and firms; it is cured, not cooked, so freezing for parasites still applies for wild fish.
- **Ceviche**: acid denatures the surface proteins and makes fish opaque — it does **not** pasteurize. Treat it exactly like raw fish for safety purposes. 15-30 minutes in citrus is a marinade; two hours is a chalky, over-firmed one.
- Salt-baked whole fish (a crust of egg-white and coarse salt) seasons through the skin and insulates — one of the most forgiving whole-fish methods there is.

## Frozen Fish, Handled Right

- Thaw in the fridge overnight, or sealed in cold water for 30-60 minutes. Never on the counter, never in warm water.
- Thin fillets can go from frozen straight into a hot pan or a 200°C oven — add roughly 50% to the time, and it often beats a badly thawed fillet.
- Refreezing thawed raw fish costs texture badly. Cook it, then freeze the cooked dish.
