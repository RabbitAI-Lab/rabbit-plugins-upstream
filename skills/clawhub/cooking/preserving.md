# Preserving — Ferments, Pickles, Cures, Jam, Canning, Freezing

Preserving works by removing what microbes need: water (drying, salting, sugar), oxygen (fat seals, vacuum), a hospitable pH (acid), a hospitable temperature (freezing), or by killing them outright (heat). Every method below is one or more of those five, and the safety line in each is a number, not a judgement.

**Read `ferments.md` in `~/Clawic/data/cooking/` at the start of any preserving session**: what is already running, when it needs checking, and how the last batch of the same thing turned out — including the ones that failed, which is the only reliable way to tell "normal" from "throw it out".

## The Safety Lines

| Line | Number | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Acid boundary | **pH 4.6** | *Clostridium botulinum* cannot grow below it. Above it, a sealed anaerobic jar needs a pressure canner |
| Boiling does not sterilize | Spores survive 100°C | Which is why low-acid food in a jar needs 116°C, only reachable under pressure |
| Ferment salt floor | **1.5%** of vegetable weight | Below it, the wrong organisms outcompete the lactic acid bacteria |
| Ferment salt ceiling | ~3.5% | Above it, fermentation stalls |
| Nitrite for cured meat | **2.5 g cure #1 per kg** = 156 ppm | A safety measure against botulism in cured, anaerobic, low-temperature meat. Never estimated |
| Refrigerated ferments | 4°C or below | Cold slows, it does not stop |
| Freezer | **−18°C** | Above it, quality loss accelerates and some spoilage continues |
| Parasite kill in fish for raw eating | −20°C for 7 days, or −35°C for ~15 h | Domestic freezers often cannot hold −20°C reliably |

Two rules follow from these and admit no exceptions: **never invent a canning recipe for a low-acid food**, and **never guess a nitrite quantity**. Use a tested recipe from a food-safety authority for pressure canning, and a 0.1 g scale for cure.

## Vegetable Ferments

Salt at **2% of the vegetable weight** for kraut, kimchi, and most brined vegetables; 3-5% for a separate brine that vegetables sit in.

1. Weigh the prepared vegetables. Multiply by 0.02. That is the salt.
2. Massage or brine until liquid covers everything. **Everything must stay submerged** — that is what makes it anaerobic. A weight, a cabbage leaf, or a water-filled bag does it.
3. Ferment at 18-22°C. Cooler is slower and cleaner; above 25°C it goes soft and can go off.
4. Burp daily or use an airlock. Active fermentation produces real pressure.
5. Taste from day 3. When it is sour enough, refrigerate. Cold does not stop it, only slows it.

Timelines at 20°C: kraut 1-4 weeks, kimchi 3-7 days then refrigerated, cucumber pickles 3-10 days, hot sauce mash 1-4 weeks, giardiniera 5-10 days.

Normal: cloudy brine, bubbles, a sour and yeasty smell, a white film of kahm yeast (harmless, skim it). Not normal: fuzzy mould in any colour, pink or orange slime, a smell of rot or solvent, or anything that has been above the brine and gone soft. Mould on the surface of a submerged ferment means the seal failed — discard the batch rather than scraping.

Softness usually means too little salt, too warm, or tannin-free cucumbers; a grape or oak leaf adds tannins that keep pickles crisp.

## Vinegar Pickles

Quick pickles are not preserves; they are a refrigerated condiment, good for 2-4 weeks.

- Standard brine: **1 part vinegar (5% acidity) : 1 part water**, plus 2% salt and sugar to taste. Never dilute below 1:1 unless following a tested canning recipe — dilution is exactly what pushes the pH above the safe line.
- Pour hot brine over raw vegetables for crunch; simmer them briefly for softer, more penetrated pickles.
- Aromatics: dill, garlic, mustard seed, coriander, chilli, bay, peppercorn. Add a tannin source for crispness.
- Shelf-stable pickles need a tested recipe, correct headspace, and a water-bath process. Refrigerator pickles do not.

## Curing Meat and Fish

- **Equilibrium curing** is the reliable home method: salt at 2-3% of the meat's weight (plus 0.25% cure #1 where nitrite is needed), vacuum-sealed or bagged, refrigerated, turned daily. Because the salt is measured against the meat, it cannot over-salt however long it sits — unlike a salt box, which requires timing.
- **Cure #1** (6.25% sodium nitrite) at **2.5 g per kg of meat** for anything cured, cooked, or cold-smoked at low temperature. **Cure #2** contains nitrate as well and is for long dry-cures that hang for months. They are not interchangeable, and neither is optional in the applications that call for them.
- **Dry-cured whole muscle** (bresaola, lonzino, coppa, duck breast): cure, rinse, then hang at 12-15°C and 70-80% relative humidity until it has lost **30-40% of its post-cure weight**. Record both the start weight and the target in the batch's row in `ferments.md`; the endpoint is a percentage, not a date.
- **Cold smoking** happens below 30°C and does not cook — the meat must be cured first, or it is a botulism risk. Hot smoking cooks and cures far less.
- **Fish cures** (gravlax): equal salt and sugar at 15-20% of the fillet weight, 24-48 h under light weight, rinsed and dried. Wild fish intended for raw eating still needs the freezing step (`seafood.md`).
- **Bacon and pancetta**: 2.5% salt, 0.25% cure #1, 1-1.5% sugar, 7-10 days refrigerated in a bag, turned daily, then rinsed, dried overnight, and cooked or cold-smoked.

## Jam and Preserves

Set depends on three things together: **pectin, acid, and sugar**.

- Standard ratio: roughly **1:1 fruit to sugar by weight** for a long-keeping jam; 60-70% sugar of the fruit weight for a fresher, softer, shorter-keeping one that lives in the fridge.
- Setting point: **104-105°C**, or the cold-plate test — a spoonful on a chilled plate wrinkles when pushed. Both, if the batch matters.
- Low-pectin fruit (strawberry, cherry, peach, fig) needs added pectin, or apple, or lemon. High-pectin fruit (apple, quince, citrus peel, currant, gooseberry) sets on its own.
- Acid is required for pectin to gel: 1-2 tbsp lemon juice per kg of fruit is the usual correction, and it also drops the pH toward the safe zone.
- Don't double a jam batch. Evaporation is limited by surface area, so a double batch takes far longer to reach setting point and overcooks (SKILL.md Rule 7).
- Sterilize jars in a 130°C oven or a boiling water bath, fill hot, seal hot. A properly sealed jar of high-sugar jam keeps a year in a cool dark place; once opened, it lives in the fridge.

## Canning

The one area where improvisation is genuinely dangerous.

| Food | pH | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Jam, jelly, most fruit, pickles with a tested brine, tomatoes **with added acid** | ≤4.6 | Water-bath canning, 100°C, time by recipe and jar size |
| Vegetables, beans, meat, fish, soup, stock, pesto, most sauces | >4.6 | **Pressure canner only**, 116°C at about 15 psi, time by tested recipe |
| Garlic or herbs in oil | >4.6, anaerobic | Refrigerate and use within days; the classic botulism vehicle |
| Anything you cannot find a tested recipe for | — | Freeze it instead |

Tomatoes sit near the line and modern varieties are often above it, which is why every current tested recipe adds lemon juice or citric acid. That addition is not for flavor.

Adjust water-bath time for altitude: add roughly 1 minute per 300 m above 300 m, or use the pressure adjustment in the tested recipe. Check seals after 24 hours — a lid that flexes did not seal, and that jar goes to the fridge.

## Freezing

- Freeze **fast and cold**. Slow freezing grows large ice crystals that rupture cells, which is why home-frozen food weeps on thawing and commercially blast-frozen food does not. Spread items in a single layer first, then bag them.
- **Blanch vegetables before freezing** — 1-3 minutes then an ice bath. It deactivates the enzymes that keep degrading colour, texture, and flavour at freezer temperatures.
- Wrap tightly and exclude air. Freezer burn is sublimation: ice leaving the surface of the food and depositing elsewhere in the package. It is not a safety issue, only a quality one.
- Label with contents and date. Practical quality windows at −18°C: soups and stews 3-6 months, raw meat 4-12 months, mince 3-4, fish 3-6 (oily fish shorter), bread 3 months, blanched vegetables 8-12, butter 6-9, stock 6.
- **Do not freeze**: whole eggs in shell, most raw salad vegetables, potatoes in stews (they go mealy), cream sauces that split, and fully thawed raw meat you plan to refreeze raw.
- Thaw in the fridge, or sealed in cold water changed every 30 minutes, or in the microwave immediately before cooking. Never on the counter (`safety.md`).
- A power cut: a full freezer holds safe temperature about 48 hours unopened, a half-full one about 24. Food still containing ice crystals can be refrozen; food above 4°C for more than two hours cannot.

## Drying and Fat Seals

- **Herbs**: air-dry in the dark, or 40-50°C in a dehydrator or the lowest oven with the door ajar. Dried herbs are ⅓ the volume of fresh for the same effect and lose most of their aroma within a year (`seasoning.md`).
- **Fruit and vegetables**: 55-70°C until leathery with no wet centre. Sulphuring or an acid dip keeps light fruit pale.
- **Confit** is a fat seal over salted, slow-cooked meat: kept fully submerged and refrigerated it lasts weeks, and the salt and the anaerobic fat together do the preserving. Anything not submerged spoils first.
- **Salt-packing** (anchovies, lemons, capers) relies on enough salt to make water unavailable — that means fully packed, not sprinkled.

## When to Throw It Out

Discard without tasting: any bulging or leaking jar or can; any home-canned low-acid food that was not pressure-canned; a ferment with fuzzy mould or a rotten smell; anything with pink, orange, or black slime; garlic or herbs stored in oil at room temperature; and any preserve whose seal failed and was not refrigerated.

Botulinum toxin has no smell and no taste, and the food it grows in can look and smell fine. That is why the rule is a process, not a sniff. When in doubt, the cost is one jar.
