# Grilling, Barbecue, and Live Fire

A grill is two appliances in one: a searing surface and an oven. Almost every grilling failure — burnt outside, raw inside, flare-ups, food stuck to the bars — comes from trying to use one when the dish needed the other.

**Read `## Kitchen` and `## Household` in `~/Clawic/data/cooking/memory.md`**: which grill exists, how its zones actually behave, and the household's doneness preferences are recorded there. Outdoor cooking is also the setting where guest constraints matter most, so check `contacts.md` for anyone being cooked for (`memory-template.md`).

## Two-Zone Setup — the Foundation

Bank all the coals to one side, or leave one set of gas burners off. You now have a hot direct zone and a cooler indirect zone with a lid over it.

- **Direct** for anything under ~2.5 cm thick and under 10 minutes: steaks, chops, prawns, vegetables, burgers.
- **Indirect** for anything thicker, bone-in, or fatty: whole chickens, roasts, ribs, thick chops. Lid down, it is a convection oven.
- **Both, in sequence**, for the best results: indirect until 10-15°C below target, then direct for the crust. This is a reverse-sear over fire, and it is the single technique that most improves home grilling (`meat.md`).
- The escape route is the entire point of two zones. Anything that flares or colours too fast moves to the cool side instead of being fought.

## Temperature by Zone

| Zone | Temperature | Hand test (palm 12 cm above the grate) | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Screaming hot | 260°C+ | 1-2 seconds | Thin steaks, searing, blistering peppers |
| Hot | 200-260°C | 2-4 seconds | Most direct grilling |
| Medium | 175-200°C | 5-6 seconds | Chicken pieces, sausages, thick vegetables |
| Low / indirect | 110-160°C | 8-10 seconds | Roasts, whole birds, ribs, anything needing time |
| Smoking | 105-120°C | — | Barbecue proper |

The hand test is a calibration, not a substitute for a lid thermometer — and a lid thermometer reads the air at the dome, which can be 20-30°C away from the grate.

## Charcoal, Gas, and Wood

- **Charcoal**: lump burns hotter and less evenly and lights faster; briquettes burn cooler, longer, and more predictably. A chimney starter with newspaper or a firelighter is the only method worth using — lighter fluid taints food, and coals are ready when they are ashed over grey, 15-20 minutes.
- **Airflow controls temperature.** The bottom vent feeds oxygen, the top vent pulls the draft. Choke the bottom to cool, open both to run hot. Never fully close the top with food inside; the smoke turns acrid.
- **Gas**: convenient, controllable, and does not produce the smoke that comes from fat dripping onto coals. Preheat with the lid down for 10-15 minutes; add a smoker box for wood flavor.
- **Wood**: burn it down to coals before cooking, or the food is coated in raw smoke. Fruit woods are mild, oak and hickory medium, mesquite strong and easy to overdo. Never use treated, painted, or resinous softwood.
- **Pellet cookers** are convection ovens with real smoke and precise temperature control; they excel at low-and-slow and struggle to sear.

## Preventing Sticking and Flare-Ups

- **Clean, hot, oiled — in that order.** Preheat the grate until residue carbonizes, brush it off, then wipe with an oiled cloth. A cold or dirty grate is what welds food to the bars.
- **Oil the food, not the grate**, for anything delicate. Oil on hot bars burns off in seconds.
- Food releases itself when the crust forms, exactly as in a pan. If it will not lift, it is not ready.
- **Flare-ups are burning fat, not cooking heat.** Move the food to the indirect zone until they subside; spraying water spreads grease and lifts ash onto the food. Trim heavy fat caps, and keep the lid ready — closing it starves the flame.
- Sugary marinades and barbecue sauce burn: brush them on only in the last 5-10 minutes, over lower heat.

## Barbecue — Low and Slow

Target 105-120°C at the grate, for hours, with clean smoke.

| Cut | Time at 110°C | Finish at | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pork shoulder / Boston butt | 1.5-2 h per kg | 92-96°C | The most forgiving barbecue cut |
| Beef brisket | 1.5-2 h per kg | 93-96°C | Probe tenderness beats any number |
| Pork ribs | 4-6 h total | Bend test: cracks on the surface when lifted | 3-2-1 wrapping is a schedule, not a law |
| Whole chicken | 2-3 h | 74°C breast / 79-85°C thigh | Finish hot for skin, or it stays rubbery |
| Beef short rib | 6-8 h | 93-96°C | Behaves like brisket |

- **Thin blue smoke** is what you want — nearly invisible. Thick white or grey smoke deposits soot and bitterness. If the fire is smouldering, it needs more air, not more wood.
- **The stall** is real and is not a broken cooker: somewhere around 65-75°C internal, evaporative cooling from the meat's surface balances the heat going in, and the temperature stops rising for hours. Wait it out, or **wrap** in foil or butcher paper to stop the evaporation. Foil moves faster and softens the bark; paper breathes and keeps it.
- **The smoke ring** is a nitrogen-dioxide reaction with myoglobin, purely cosmetic, and says nothing about flavor.
- Rest big cuts 30-60 minutes, wrapped and in a cool box if needed — the internal temperature holds for hours and the meat improves.
- Bark comes from a dry surface, a rub with sugar and salt, and long exposure. Spritzing slows the bark; wrapping softens it. Choose which you care about.

## What Grills Badly, and What Grills Better Than Anything

Poor candidates: delicate white fish without a basket, anything that needs a sauce built in the pan, thin cuts that overcook before the crust forms, and anything the household wants at a precise doneness in bad light.

Excellent candidates: whole vegetables (aubergine, peppers, spring onions, corn, cabbage wedges, romaine halves), oily fish, whole fish in a cage, skewers, flatbreads straight on the bars, halloumi, and anything that benefits from char.

Vegetables specifically: oil them and salt them before grilling; direct heat and a short time for tender ones; indirect with the lid down for dense ones. A whole aubergine or pepper charred black over direct heat and then peeled is a technique with no stovetop equivalent.

## Marinades and Rubs, Honestly

- Marinades penetrate only 1-3 mm in a few hours. They are surface treatments — which is fine, because the surface is where the flavor is perceived.
- Acid-heavy marinades over about 2 hours turn the surface of fish and thin meat mushy. Yogurt and buttermilk are gentler and genuinely tenderize by a small amount.
- Salt is the ingredient in the marinade that actually gets inside. A dry brine plus oil and aromatics beats a wet marinade for most things (`meat.md`).
- Sugar in a rub browns and then burns. Salt-and-spice rubs for high heat, sugar-containing rubs for low and slow.
- Pat the surface dry before grilling whatever the marinade was — wet food steams and never chars.

## Safety Outdoors

- Grill outdoors only. Charcoal produces carbon monoxide, and every year people are poisoned bringing a barbecue into a tent, a garage, or indoors to cool.
- Keep a separate clean platter for cooked food. Reusing the raw platter is the most common outdoor cross-contamination route, along with a basting brush that touched raw marinade (`safety.md`).
- Outdoor temperatures compress the danger-zone clock: above 32°C ambient, the total budget for food out of refrigeration is **1 hour**, not two.
- Check that gas connections are not leaking with soapy water before the first cook of the season, and never light a gas grill with the lid closed.
