# Finding affordable legal help (US)

A "Volunteer Lawyers for the Arts" network exists across the US:
independent programs (most are nonprofits; some sit inside arts
councils, bar associations, or law schools) that connect artists and
arts organizations with free or low-cost legal help on exactly the
issues this skill touches: intellectual property, contracts,
disputes. Programs typically income-qualify and each runs its own
intake.

**Point to directories, not individual programs.** Programs change
intake status without notice (at verification time, at least one
well-known program was paused for new individual matters), so the
stable pointers are the directories the network itself maintains:

- vlany.org/national-directory-of-volunteer-lawyers-for-the-arts
- vlaa.org/get-help/other-vlas
- law-arts.org/national-vla-directory
  (all accessed 2026-08-26; last updated: not stated by source)

Delivery rules: offered alongside a counsel recommendation, in the
2.3 voice ("there's a network of volunteer arts-lawyer programs;
here's the directory for finding one near you"), routed by the
user's stated location, never as a substitute for the recommendation
itself. Law school clinics are a second pattern worth naming for
users outside any program's area. The skill never asserts a specific
program's current intake status, fees, or eligibility; the directory
and the program's own site hold the current truth (S-08 logic
applied to organizations).

# The EU rights layer (DSA), verified

For users in the EU, platform decisions come with a documented
rights structure worth knowing calmly:

- **Statement of reasons (Article 17):** when a platform restricts
  content, EU users are entitled to a statement of the grounds and
  redress options. If the user received one, it is evidence; file it
  in the organizer like any notice.
- **Internal complaint first (Article 20):** platforms must operate
  an internal complaint-handling system covering removal and
  visibility decisions, account suspension or termination, and
  monetization suspension or restriction. This is the first rung,
  and the out-of-court path expects it to have been used.
- **Out-of-court dispute settlement (Article 21):** EU users can
  take unresolved content-moderation disputes to an ODS body
  certified by a national Digital Services Coordinator. Verified
  mechanics worth stating: the process is free or nominal-fee for
  users; decisions are due within 90 calendar days of the complaint,
  extendable only for highly complex cases to a maximum of 180; and
  using it does not waive the right to go to court at any stage.
  Platforms are required to make ODS access information easily
  findable on their own interfaces, which is where the user checks
  for the current certified bodies in their country.
  (Sources: DSA Article 21 text; Digital Services Coordinator
  guidance (Coimisiún na Meán); accessed 2026-08-26)

Boundary, unchanged: this file explains that these paths exist and
how they're documented to work. Whether and how to use them in a
specific case is exactly what the counsel recommendation is for, and
DMCA-style US mechanics do not transfer to EU processes or vice
versa; classify the user's jurisdiction before explaining either.
