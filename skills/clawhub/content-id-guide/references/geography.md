# Where geography changes the answer

Jurisdiction is asked at intake for a reason: five different layers
of this domain vary by where the user is, and mixing them up
produces confidently wrong guidance. This file is the map; each
verified fact lives in its home reference and is pointed to here.

## 1. The legal layer varies the most

- **The DMCA is United States law.** Takedown, counter notification,
  §512 safe harbor, and the 10-to-14-business-day restoration window
  are US constructs. Platforms extend DMCA-shaped flows worldwide as
  a matter of their own process, and TikTok's policy says as much
  ("the United States DMCA or similar laws in other countries"), but
  the legal rights underneath differ by country, which is exactly
  where counsel earns their fee.
- **The EU has its own structure**, verified in
  references/legal-help.md: DSA statements of reasons, mandatory
  internal complaints, and out-of-court dispute settlement with a
  90-day decision window, free or nominal-fee for users, without
  waiving court access. Article 17 of the DSM directive is the
  regulatory driver behind matching systems at EU scale.
- **Copyright exceptions are not one system.** "Fair use" with its
  four factors is US doctrine. The UK, Canada, Australia, and others
  run fair dealing, a different and generally narrower framework
  built on enumerated purposes; the EU runs enumerated exceptions
  (quotation, parody, and others) that vary in national
  implementation. The skill therefore names which system it is
  explaining, explains the US four factors only as US law, and for
  other systems says which framework applies and routes to counsel
  rather than improvising its contours. Note the verified
  platform-text wrinkle: YouTube's own counter-notification page
  lists "fair use, fair dealing, or public domain" as
  misidentification grounds, so the platform layer already
  acknowledges plural doctrines.
- **Minors' earnings protections vary by region** (trust and
  set-aside arrangements); already routed to counsel as a matter of
  course in S-13.

## 2. Platform mechanics vary by region less, but really

- Program availability and thresholds are region-scoped: YPP
  requirements are shown region-specifically in the Earn tab;
  TikTok's Creator Rewards runs an eligible-country list; Creator
  Music is currently US-only with expansion pending (all verified in
  references/monetization-programs.md).
- Claim actions can be territorial by design: region-restricted
  blocks on YouTube, per-territory match rules on Meta. A claim that
  blocks in one country and not another is usually a territory-scoped
  rights picture, not a glitch.
- Localization of the flows themselves: notices arrive in the
  account's language; the 2.3 localization rule (official terms kept
  in original language, localized official pages only when they
  verify) governs.

## One system, many laws: how an international claim actually works

The single most confusing thing in this whole domain, named plainly:
each platform runs one global matching system, and that system sits
on top of rights that are national and territorial. Both things are
true at once, and every "international claim" mystery resolves into
one of them.

- **The claim travels globally.** Matching, the dashboard, the
  dispute and appeal flow, and the windows are one system per
  platform, the same in every country (that's the verified Tier 1
  material in the platform references).
- **The rights are local.** Copyright is national law, licenses are
  territory-scoped instruments, ownership can genuinely differ per
  region, and collecting societies are national. So a claim's
  *actions* can be territorial by design: a region-restricted block,
  a per-territory match rule, or different claimants on the same
  song in different regions are the rights patchwork showing through
  the global system, not malfunctions.
- **What this means in practice:** a video blocked only in some
  countries usually means the claimant's rights (or the user's
  license) stop at a border; the dispute still runs in the
  platform's one global flow, but the paperwork behind it only
  carries weight inside its own territory clause, which is why the
  evidence checklist asks about territory and why cross-border
  situations (user, claimant, or audience in different places) hold
  a standing counsel recommendation.

> **THE SHORT VERSION** The claim system is worldwide; the rights
> behind it stop at borders. A video blocked in just a few countries
> usually means someone's rights end where the block ends.
>
> **THE FINE PRINT** Read region-shaped claim patterns against the
> territory clause of the license and the claimant's per-territory
> ownership before treating them as disputes. The platform flow is
> uniform; the winning paperwork is jurisdictional. Cross-border
> fact patterns are exactly where proper legal counsel earns their
> fee, and the skill recommends them there as a matter of course.

## 3. Money varies by geography

- The Shorts Creator Pool is computed per country (verified), so
  identical views in different countries yield different
  allocations by design.
- Currency, payout mechanics, tax treatment, and minimum-balance
  rules differ by market (TikTok's payout guidance is
  market-scoped); the skill states none of these from memory and
  routes to the user's own payout dashboard and, where it matters,
  counsel or a tax professional.
- **Currency discipline, firm:** amounts are stated only in the
  currency the user's own notice or dashboard shows, exactly as
  shown. The skill never converts between currencies or states an
  exchange rate, because rates move and any figure it produced would
  be wrong by the time it was read; "how much is that in my
  currency" gets the honest answer that the payout dashboard and
  the user's bank set the real conversion. Preferred payout currency
  is a platform payout setting, found and changed in the user's own
  payout dashboard, never described from memory.

## 4. Rights management varies by geography

- Licenses are territory-scoped instruments: the evidence checklist
  already asks whether documentation specifies territory, and a
  region-shaped claim pattern should be read against the license's
  territory clause before anything else.
- Collecting societies and performing rights organizations differ by
  country, and composition-side money often flows through them; the
  skill names this pattern so artists know the question exists, and
  routes specifics to the user's own society or counsel rather than
  describing any organization's current rules from memory.
- Ownership can genuinely differ per territory (different labels or
  publishers per region), which is the label layer's territory-split
  root cause.

## 5. Clocks vary by geography

- "Business days" are defined by a jurisdiction's calendar: the
  DMCA window runs on US business days including US federal
  holidays, which a creator elsewhere shouldn't count by their own
  calendar. Platform windows stated as plain days are treated as
  calendar days everywhere per S-10.
- EU ODS decisions run on the verified 90-calendar-day window
  (extendable to 180 for highly complex cases).

## Session behavior

When jurisdiction is unknown and a location-sensitive topic arises
(doctrine, money, deadlines, licensing scope), the skill asks the
light intake question before answering the sensitive part, and
answers the rest immediately. When jurisdiction is known, the skill
says plainly which layer of the answer is jurisdiction-specific
("this part is US law; your platform steps look the same, but the
legal rights underneath differ where you are") so the user always
knows which kind of ground they're standing on. Cross-border
situations (user, claimant, or audience in different places) remain
a Tier B counsel recommendation per the escalation map.
