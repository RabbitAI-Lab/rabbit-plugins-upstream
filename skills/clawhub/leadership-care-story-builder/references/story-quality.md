# Story Quality Reference

Sources reviewed online:

- Duarte: contrast and sparkline; move between current reality and desired future, build tension and release, know audience needs, balance stories and data, and use vocal pace/pause/volume intentionally. https://www.duarte.com/blog/ultimate-guide-to-contrast/
- Marshall Ganz / Harvard: public narrative connects Story of Self, Story of Us, and Story of Now to motivate commitment and action. https://marshallganz.scholars.harvard.edu/lsa-public-narrative-leadership-storytelling-action
- Center for Creative Leadership: effective storytelling can connect logic with emotion and inspire action through Head, Heart, and Hands. https://www.ccl.org/webinars/how-storytelling-can-influence-action/
- FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin: leadership stories benefit from setting, main character, problem, overcoming the problem, and lesson learned; stories should be concise, relevant, authentic, and relatable, and avoid boasting, over-detailing, inaccuracy, and excessive emotion. https://leb.fbi.gov/articles/featured-articles/leadership-storytelling
- Schulich ExecEd: leadership storytelling helps provide context, explain decisions, reinforce priorities, and connect everyday work to broader purpose; use stories intentionally when they help people understand change, decisions, challenges, or success. https://execed.schulich.yorku.ca/news/the-power-of-storytelling-in-leadership-communication/

## Quality Heuristics

A strong leadership story should:

- Serve a defined leadership purpose.
- Be relevant to the specific audience.
- Start near a concrete moment.
- Include tension: uncertainty, conflict, tradeoff, risk, or decision.
- Show specific action, not just intention.
- Prove outcome through metric, behavior, customer signal, or credible observation.
- Tie lesson back to team values, priorities, or decision principles.
- End with reflection or action.
- Sound authentic and spoken.
- Respect the audience's intelligence and constraints.

## Common Problems

- No purpose: the story is interesting but not useful.
- Too much setup: context crowds out action.
- No tension: nothing changes.
- Leader-as-hero: the story flatters the speaker instead of serving the audience.
- No proof: outcome is asserted but not shown.
- Vague lesson: audience cannot apply it.
- No call to action: listeners do not know what to do next.
- Too polished: story sounds like PR rather than lived experience.
- Too much data: story becomes a report.
- Too emotional: story overwhelms the leadership point.
