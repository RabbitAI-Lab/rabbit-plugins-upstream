# Social Arbitrage Methodology

Use this reference to decide whether an observation deserves a U.S.-equity research mapping. It adapts Chris Camillo's publicly described method; it does not represent, impersonate, or imply endorsement by Chris Camillo.

## Core equation

An investable research candidate requires all of:

```text
abnormal observable change
+ authentic and independently corroborated evidence
+ causal business mechanism
+ direct issuer exposure
+ plausible issuer-level materiality
+ underrecognized expectations gap
+ observable falsifier
= social-arbitrage research candidate
```

If one term is contradicted, reject the candidate. Do not compensate for a failed gate with high scores elsewhere.

## Operating principles

1. **Change, not popularity.** A large number without a baseline is a discovery lead, not a signal.
2. **Present ground truth before forecast.** Prefer behavior that has changed over stories about behavior that might change.
3. **Mechanism before ticker.** Explain the causal business path before naming a security.
4. **Denominator before excitement.** A large niche trend may be immaterial to a large or diversified issuer.
5. **Expectations before direction.** A true trend already recognized by management, analysts, estimates, media, and price may have no remaining information gap.
6. **Contradictions are evidence.** Preserve and investigate them rather than averaging them into false confidence.
7. **Most observations should fail.** `No qualifying signals` is a valid result.
8. **Research bias is not a trade.** Long, short, and neutral describe the apparent direction of evidence, not an instruction to execute.

## Eleven-step method

### 1. Observe

Look for changes in behavior, language, adoption, abandonment, availability, pricing, scarcity, cost, supply, regulation, culture, or perception. Start outside financial media when practical.

### 2. State a falsifiable claim

Replace “X is trending” with a measurable statement containing:

- Population or geography.
- Behavior or variable changing.
- Direction and comparison period.
- Candidate business consequence.
- Collection window.

Weak:

> Running clubs are everywhere.

Testable:

> U.S. searches, local-club creation, and retailer review velocity for beginner running products accelerated against the same seasonal period, with purchase-intent language spreading beyond established runners.

### 3. Select a baseline

Choose the comparison that best matches the claim:

- Same period last year.
- Pre-event period.
- Prior launch or product cycle.
- Peer brand or adjacent category.
- Geographic control.
- Creator/channel normal performance.
- Typical review, ranking, pricing, or inventory velocity.

Record why the baseline is appropriate and what bias it may introduce.

### 4. Measure abnormality

Evaluate:

- Level and rate of change.
- Acceleration or deceleration.
- Persistence versus a one-day spike.
- Geographic and demographic breadth.
- Intent depth: viewing, asking, trying, buying, repeating, switching.
- Availability, waitlists, discounting, resale, or inventory.
- Changing vocabulary and term migration.
- Divergence from peers or seasonality.

Do not invent numerical precision. If measurement is qualitative, say so.

### 5. Test authenticity and alternatives

Inspect:

- Sponsorship and affiliate incentives.
- Repeated scripts, captions, comments, or posting times.
- Account age and quality.
- Bot-like or coordinated activity.
- Recycled or mislabeled media.
- Algorithmic amplification.
- News-driven curiosity mistaken for demand.
- Local events mistaken for national change.
- Stock-promotion incentives.

List at least one alternative explanation before advancing the signal.

### 6. Corroborate independently

Require at least two meaningfully independent evidence channels for A/B candidates. Independence is based on origin, not URL count.

Examples of independent combinations:

- Creator comments plus search behavior.
- Search behavior plus retailer inventory.
- App rankings plus job postings.
- Public-agency data plus local reporting.
- Product reviews plus issuer segment disclosure.

These are not independent:

- Reposts of one video.
- Articles copying one press release.
- Creators in one paid campaign.
- Multiple dashboards derived from the same source dataset.

### 7. Map the causal mechanism

Write the chain explicitly:

```text
observed change
→ customer / employee / supplier / regulator behavior
→ units / price / mix / retention / cost / capacity / perception
→ exposed issuer segment
→ possible reported result or expectations change
```

Label each unsupported arrow as a data gap. If the chain depends on a weak arrow, reject or cap at C.

### 8. Prove exposure and materiality

Verify:

- Legal issuer and ticker identity.
- Brand, product, customer, supplier, or geographic ownership.
- Segment size and economics.
- Revenue, cost, margin, capacity, or inventory sensitivity.
- Whether exposure is direct or second-order.
- Whether the effect could matter relative to the whole issuer.

Technical capability, shared keywords, or theme adjacency do not prove exposure.

### 9. Assess awareness and expectations

Review:

- Company filings, presentations, guidance, and earnings calls.
- Trade and general media.
- Financial media and analyst framing.
- Estimate revisions when reliably available.
- Recent price and volume reaction.
- Known catalysts and crowded narratives.

Classify awareness:

- **Undiscovered:** Little credible public connection to the issuer.
- **Emerging:** Niche or trade discussion exists; financial connection is limited.
- **Partially disseminated:** Financial coverage exists, but magnitude or mechanism may be misread.
- **Consensus:** Management, analysts, estimates, and price substantially recognize it.

Consensus does not mean the trend is false. It means the original social-arbitrage wedge needs new incremental evidence.

### 10. Define timing, rejection, and falsification

For every A/B candidate state:

- **Why now:** What changed recently.
- **Dissemination path:** What could make broader investors notice.
- **Dominant-event check:** What else could overwhelm the signal in the same window.
- **First rejection test:** The cheapest decisive check to run next.
- **Falsifier:** Observable evidence that would break the thesis.
- **Monitoring:** Exact data, queries, events, and thresholds to revisit.

### 11. Rank or reject

Only after steps 1–10 assign a tier and research bias. A compelling story without completed gates is not an A/B candidate.

## Ten-gate decision table

| Gate | Pass standard | Failure consequence | Unresolved consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Change | Abnormal movement versus a defensible baseline | Reject | C at most |
| Timestamp | Visible date or defensible collection window | Reject stale/undated claim | B at most if other current evidence exists |
| Authenticity | No affirmative manipulation; incentives and alternatives tested | Reject | C at most |
| Corroboration | Two meaningfully independent evidence channels | Reject A/B | C at most |
| Mechanism | Causal path to revenue, cost, margin, supply, retention, or perception | Reject | C at most |
| Exposure | Direct issuer relationship verified | Reject ticker | C mapping hypothesis only |
| Materiality | Plausible effect relative to issuer scale | Reject | B at most with a defined proof need |
| Information gap | Evidence not already fully embedded in expectations | Reject original thesis or neutralize | B at most |
| Falsifiability | Observable disconfirming evidence named | Reject A/B | C at most |
| U.S. universe | Eligible listing, identity, and liquidity verified | Reject | Do not print ticker until resolved |

Evidence that contradicts a gate is a failure. Missing evidence is unresolved. Do not call missing evidence a pass.

## Research tiers

### A — Immediate research

All gates pass with strong evidence. Direct material exposure and an underrecognized expectations gap are supported. The catalyst, first rejection test, falsifier, and monitoring path are clear.

`A` means prioritize deeper investment work. It does not mean buy or sell.

### B — Active watchlist

The signal is credible and no gate is contradicted, but one important proof point is incomplete. Name that proof point prominently. A B candidate still needs all gates to be at least provisionally supported.

### C — Early signal

The change may be real, but issuer attribution, materiality, corroboration, awareness, or timing is incomplete. A C ticker is a mapping hypothesis, not an investable candidate. State every unresolved gate.

### Reject

Use Reject for:

- Static popularity.
- Manipulation or paid virality without organic confirmation.
- Single-source evidence.
- Weak mechanism.
- Theme adjacency without exposure.
- Immaterial issuer effect.
- Consensus information without an incremental wedge.
- Ineligible or unverifiable security.
- Missing reproducible evidence.

Preserve plausible-looking rejections in the report so the research process is auditable.

## Directional research bias

### Long research bias

Evidence suggests underappreciated positive impact through demand, pricing, mix, retention, distribution, supply advantage, lower cost, or favorable perception.

### Short research bias

Evidence suggests underappreciated deterioration through demand decay, substitution, discounting, excess inventory, rising costs, supply constraints, lost relevance, regulation, or negative perception that changes behavior.

### Neutral research bias

Use neutral when:

- The trend is real but the public beneficiary is unclear.
- Exposure is direct but financial direction is ambiguous.
- Real-world strength conflicts with demanding expectations.
- Deterioration is already consensus.
- Timing is too uncertain.
- Evidence channels materially conflict.

Always write `research bias`, never `recommendation`, `trade`, or `position`.

## Default security universe

### Include

- Common stocks and ADRs listed on NYSE, Nasdaq, or NYSE American.
- Operating companies with verifiable issuer identity and market data.

### Exclude by default

- OTC securities.
- ETFs, funds, and closed-end funds.
- Shells, pre-merger SPACs, and acquisition vehicles.
- Illiquid microcaps vulnerable to promotion or manipulation.
- Binary pre-commercial biotechnology stories unless explicitly requested.
- Securities with unresolved ticker, exchange, identity, liquidity, or exposure.

For ADRs, disclose the foreign operating company, listing structure, geography, and currency exposure. U.S.-listed does not mean U.S.-operated.

## Sector lenses

A broad scan considers every lens but does not force output from any lens.

### 1. Consumer, retail, apparel, beauty, food, and restaurants

Look for product adoption, substitution, distribution, assortment, waitlists, discounting, traffic, menu behavior, creator spread, reviews, and demographic blind spots.

### 2. Software, AI, semiconductors, hardware, and devices

Look for present deployments, developer behavior, workloads, jobs, procurement, capacity, latency, switching, and actual use—not total-addressable-market narratives alone.

### 3. Media, gaming, advertising, and entertainment

Look for durable engagement, subscriber acquisition or retention, creator economics, ad demand, content persistence, game activity, venue utilization, and pricing.

### 4. Healthcare and wellness

Use public information only. Look for changed patient or consumer behavior, provider workflows, availability, adherence, wellness substitution, and public health data. Avoid individualized medical conclusions and binary clinical speculation.

### 5. Travel, mobility, housing, home improvement, and finance

Look for searches, bookings, pricing, routes, wait times, repair demand, permits, mortgage behavior, payments, delinquencies, and regional divergence.

### 6. Industrials, logistics, manufacturing, energy, and commodities

Look for order patterns, capacity, bottlenecks, lead times, freight, maintenance, construction, input substitution, utilization, and supplier/customer concentration.

### 7. Weather, climate, agriculture, and regional disruption

Connect the event to geography, severity, timing, repairs, crop or commodity effects, insurance behavior, exposed categories, and issuer scale. Control for normal seasonality.

### 8. Labor, education, demographics, regulation, policy, and culture

Look for job demand, skill changes, migration, enrollment, compliance cost, benefit changes, language, identity, and new cultural practices with measurable business consequences.

## Second-order effects

Search for beneficiaries and casualties beyond the obvious brand:

- Suppliers.
- Distributors.
- Retail channels.
- Complements and substitutes.
- Infrastructure providers.
- Cost bearers.
- Competitors gaining or losing share.

Apply the same exposure and materiality gates. A supplier that can manufacture a component is not necessarily a supplier to the trend.

## Historical lessons, not current calls

Use these only as pattern examples:

- Shelf-space loss: field observation requires geographic validation.
- Wii scarcity: enthusiast communities can reveal demand and availability.
- Target/Missoni: fashion and parent communities can expose demographic blind spots.
- Chuggington: popularity requires ownership and licensing attribution.
- Restaurants: analyst geography can distort national conclusions.
- Under Armour: niche adoption needs spread and issuer-scale analysis.
- Netflix content: conversation persistence may matter more than launch-week volume.
- LaCroix: focused issuer exposure can make a trend denominator-moving.
- Victoria's Secret: substitution and assortment mismatch can produce short signals.
- Chipotle: changing language requires correct taxonomy and customer relevance.
- SeaWorld: negative sentiment must be connected to attendance or perception.
- Weather/home repair: present demand still requires geography, timing, and capacity.
- AI adoption: current use beats distant market-size narratives.

Do not present a historical outcome as evidence for a current ticker.

## Boundaries

### Identity

Describe the method as Chris Camillo-inspired or Camillo-derived. Never claim to be Chris Camillo, speak on his behalf, imply endorsement, or reproduce a proprietary service.

### Material nonpublic information

Do not use, validate, repeat, or build a thesis around possible MNPI. Advise the user not to trade or share it and to seek qualified legal/compliance advice where appropriate. Continue only with a clean-room public-information question that does not use the tip.

### Execution and personalization

Do not provide:

- Exact entry or exit.
- Stop loss.
- Position sizing.
- Leverage.
- Options contract selection.
- Portfolio allocation.
- Brokerage connection or trade execution.

Offer deeper public research, monitoring, or a separate valuation/risk workflow instead.

### Manipulation, privacy, and account safety

- Do not coordinate promotion, brigading, or market manipulation.
- Do not expose private personal data.
- Use public or user-authorized sources.
- Never request brokerage credentials or funds.
- Treat stock promotion and undisclosed sponsorship as disqualifying risks until independently resolved.
