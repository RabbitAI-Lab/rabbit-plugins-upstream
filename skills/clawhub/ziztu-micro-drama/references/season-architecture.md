# Season architecture: 20 to 100 episodes

A micro drama season is a feature film's worth of story (often 40 to 90 minutes in total) cut into pieces that each have to earn the next tap. This file gives the release models, the block templates for 20, 30, 50 and 80 episode seasons, and the rules for the paywall, the villain ladder, the secret reveals, the romance and the finale.

## 1. Pick the release model first

| Model | Typical shape | What it changes |
|---|---|---|
| Paid app (ReelShort, DramaBox, DramaWave, ShortMax style) | 50 to 90 episodes of 1 to 2 minutes; 5 to 12 free episodes, premium usually from around episode 10 | The paywall episode is the most important cliffhanger after episode 1 |
| Free social series (TikTok, Reels, Shorts, posted as Part 1, 2, 3) | 10 to 30 episodes of 60 to 120 seconds | Every episode is someone's first: restate the premise in the first five seconds of each part with a line or a caption |
| Free with ads (Hongguo style in China) | 60 to 100 episodes of 1 to 3 minutes | No paywall; retention per episode is everything, so payoffs come more often |
| India (Kuku TV style) | 25 to 35 episodes of about 2 minutes | Shorter seasons; a slap, confrontation or romantic beat late in each minute |
| Korea and Japan apps | Often 60 episodes of about 2 minutes (Korea); TikTok-native school and workplace series (Japan) | Higher production polish is the pitch; melodrama with restraint |
| Pilot or test | 3 to 10 episodes | Write episodes 1 to 3 as if they were the whole pitch |

When the user does not say, plan 30 episodes for a social or AI-produced series and 50 to 60 for an app, and write episodes 1 to 3 first.

## 2. The season engine

Name the question the whole season answers ("Will Mia take back her grandmother's company, and will she let herself love the man who humiliated her?") and the device that keeps it visible:

- **A clock**: a will deadline, a contract that ends in 100 days, a wedding on Friday, a five-year term.
- **A contract or bargain**: the fake marriage, the bet, the deal with the devil.
- **A hidden truth** the audience holds: she owns the company, he is the lost heir, the baby is his.

The engine should be legible from episode 1's title and logline. Every episode moves it one notch.

## 3. Episodes 1 to 3 decide the series

Producers in China say the first three episodes decide whether a series lives; the same is true of every app's data: most viewers who quit, quit early. So:

- **Episode 1**: the injustice and the wound, the lead's secret or power (shown to the viewer, not yet to the room), the season's engine, and a cliffhanger that makes episode 2 mandatory. Premise clear by 45 seconds.
- **Episode 2**: the first payoff, small and public (a comeback line, a refusal, a flash of power), and a bigger threat.
- **Episode 3**: proof the series delivers its promise: a real face-slap, a reveal to one character, or the bargain sealed. End on the strongest cliffhanger so far.

## 4. Block templates

Each block ends on a cliffhanger stronger than anything inside it. Episode numbers scale with the season length.

### 20 episodes (social series, test season)

| Episodes | Block | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 3 | Hook | Injustice, wound, engine, first payoff, the bargain or the secret is set |
| 4 to 7 | Rising | Tier 1 villain escalates and falls in public (episode 6 or 7) |
| 8 to 10 | Midpoint | The big reveal to one key character; the real villain steps in at 10 |
| 11 to 15 | Pressure | The lead loses ground; a betrayal; the romance is tested |
| 16 to 18 | Bottom and comeback | Lowest point at 16, the plan at 17, the public payoff begins at 18 |
| 19 to 20 | Finale | Every secret out, the villain pays in public, the promise delivered; a season 2 hook in the last 10 seconds |

### 30 episodes

| Episodes | Block | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 3 | Hook | As above |
| 4 to 8 | Rising | Tier 1 villain's campaign; two small payoffs; the lead's power grows in secret |
| 9 to 10 | First break | Tier 1 falls in public (9); a bigger threat arrives (10). On an app, episode 10 is a good paywall |
| 11 to 15 | Complication | Tier 2 villain; first major secret revealed to a key character around 12 to 15; the romance turns |
| 16 | Midpoint reversal | The power balance flips: the lead's secret is out, or the ally is the enemy |
| 17 to 23 | Pressure | Tier 3 villain's plan; losses; a betrayal around 20; the lowest point around 23 |
| 24 to 27 | Comeback | The plan, the allies, the evidence; the public payoff starts |
| 28 to 30 | Finale | The final face-slap, the last secret, the union or the victory; a season 2 hook |

### 50 episodes (app standard)

| Episodes | Block | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 3 | Hook | As above |
| 4 to 9 | Rising | Tier 1 escalation and fall; the bargain deepens |
| 10 | Paywall | The biggest open question so far (see section 5) |
| 11 to 12 | Payoff after the wall | The big reveal lands one or two episodes after the wall |
| 13 to 24 | Complication | Tier 2; the romance moves from hostility to protection; a first kiss or confession around 18 to 22 |
| 25 | Midpoint reversal | The central secret is exposed, or the protector is revealed as part of the wound |
| 26 to 38 | Pressure | Tier 3; separation, a pregnancy or a kidnapping; the lowest point around 36 to 38 |
| 39 to 46 | Comeback | Every ally returns; the evidence; public payoffs every two or three episodes |
| 47 to 50 | Finale | The villain's public fall, the last reveal, the promise delivered, the season 2 hook |

### 60 episodes (a full app season)

| Episodes | Block | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cold open | The humiliation or crisis in the first 3 seconds; the lead and the main antagonist both on screen |
| 2 to 3 | Setup and first payoff | The lead's secret advantage shown to the viewer; the first small comeback; episode 3 ends on a bigger threat |
| 4 to 9 | Rung 1 | Two humiliation and payback cycles; the romance starts; a secret the viewer shares with the lead |
| 10 | Paywall | The biggest cliffhanger yet, unanswered; the answer lands in 11 or 12 and opens a new question |
| 11 to 20 | Rung 2 | Paybacks get bigger and more public; the first real obstacle to the romance; a small reversal every three episodes |
| 21 to 30 | First turn | The first core secret revealed to a key character; a hint of the larger plan. Episode 30 is the second pay hook |
| 31 to 40 | Midpoint pivot | The premise reverses: the lead's advantage is stripped, or the villain's real boss appears; an all-is-lost moment |
| 41 to 50 | Dark middle | Alliances shift, a trusted person betrays, the romance ruptures; still a payoff every three episodes |
| 51 to 57 | Escalation | The final villain exposed, every ally back, the public showdown begins |
| 58 to 60 | Finale | The final confrontation, justice, status restored, the union or the wedding, and a season 2 sting in the last five seconds |

### 80 to 100 episodes

Use the 60 episode template and stretch the middle: two complication blocks (each with its own villain rung and its own reveal) and a second midpoint. Pay hooks near episodes 10, 30 and 60 (or 10, 30 and 50 for an 80 episode run). Payoffs every two to four episodes; a big public face-slap every ten to fifteen. A 40 to 70 episode season needs at least ten real complications (reveals, twists, shifts in who holds power); count them in the grid.

## 5. The paywall episode

- It asks, it does not answer. The viewer must need the next episode, so the paywall cliffhanger is a reveal about to happen, an exposure, a return or a decision, and the payoff lands one or two episodes after the wall.
- The writers' rule of thumb from China's short drama industry: episodes 1 to 4 build the world, the characters and the main line; 5 to 8 push the emotion to its limit; 9 and 10 plant the hook that makes people pay. Episode 10 is the usual first pay point (8 to 12 is the range), with later pay hooks around 30 and 60.
- The best paywall sits right after a small payoff followed by a sudden drop: the viewer has just been rewarded, then everything falls.
- Everything before the wall has paid off at least three times: the viewer has proof the series delivers.
- It belongs to the main story. A side character's cliffhanger at the wall wastes it.
- On a free social series there is no wall, but there is a drop-off point around parts 3 to 5: treat part 3 and part 5 like walls.

## 6. The villain ladder and the payback rhythm

- **Three tiers.** A petty, visible bully first (the head housekeeper, the mean classmate, the mistress), a bigger rival behind them (the ex, the stepmother, the business rival), the real villain at the top (the person behind the original wound). Each tier falls in public before the next steps in, and each fall reveals the next tier.
- **Humiliation, then payback, bigger.** Each payback is in front of witnesses, costs the villain what they value most (status, money, love, reputation), and uses their own weapon against them (the forged document is exposed by the real one; the gossip is broadcast).
- **Rhythm.** Mark every episode COMPRESS (pressure piles on) or RELEASE (pressure pays off). A humiliation and payback cycle is three to six episodes: one or two compress episodes, the reversal, one or two release episodes. A tier falls every eight to fifteen episodes. Never more than two compress episodes in a row.
- **Villains act.** A villain who only sneers is wallpaper. Give them a plan with steps the viewer can watch: steal the design, frame her for the theft, leak the photo, poison the tea.

## 7. The secret map

Dramatic irony is the cheapest suspense there is. For each secret, decide:

| Question | Why |
|---|---|
| When does the viewer learn it? | Usually early (episode 1 to 3), so every scene before the reveal plays as tension |
| When does each character learn it? | Late and one at a time; each discovery is an episode's cliffhanger or payoff |
| Who learns it at the worst moment? | The best reveals happen in public, or to the one person who should not know |
| What does it cost? | A reveal changes who holds power. If nothing changes, it was not a secret |

Reveal each secret in stages (a tease, a partial reveal, the full reveal), each stage once, at its planned episode. Hinting is fine (contradiction clues), repeated almost-reveals are not: two near-misses for one secret is the limit before the audience feels played. The central secret usually comes fully out around 40 to 60 percent of the run.

Track every planted question in the continuity ledger. A question untouched for about 15 episodes is either forgotten by the audience or resented by them: give it a beat or pay it. Unpaid setups are one of the top complaints about micro dramas.

## 8. Romance progression

For romance genres, map these beats to episodes in the grid:

1. Hostile meeting, with a spark the viewer sees (episode 1 to 2).
2. Forced proximity: the contract, the shared house, the fake relationship (2 to 5).
3. Protection: he defends her in public before he admits anything (around 6 to 10).
4. Jealousy: a rival appears (10 to 15).
5. The almost-kiss, interrupted (12 to 18), then the kiss or confession (18 to 25 on 50 episodes).
6. The wound returns: a secret or a misunderstanding splits them (midpoint).
7. Separation and regret (pressure block).
8. The grand gesture in public (comeback block).
9. Union, proposal, wedding or the baby reveal (finale).

## 9. The finale and the season 2 hook

- The finale delivers the promise in its biggest form: the most public payoff of the season, with every villain tier present to watch.
- Every secret in the map is out. Every promise in the title is kept.
- The last ten seconds open season 2 with a new question that uses the old engine: a new heir, a new threat, the villain's call from prison, the twin nobody knew about.

## 10. Rewriting from audience comments

Once episodes are out, comments and retention tell you what to deepen. Keep episodes 1 to 10 locked once published, and keep later outlines flexible:

- When the audience loves a side character, give them a scene in the next block, not a spin-off.
- When comments guess the twist, keep it but move it earlier, or add a second layer to it.
- When a villain is "too much", give them one human moment before their fall; the fall lands harder.
- When retention drops at a specific episode, check that episode's cliffhanger type and its payoff: the usual cause is a slow open, a repeated cliffhanger type, or two losses in a row.
