# Dialogue for micro dramas

Micro drama dialogue is not film dialogue made shorter. It is closer to a fight: every line is a move that changes who has the upper hand, and the viewer hears it on a phone speaker, often with captions on, often half-watching. This file covers the line types, the rules, inner voice, deliveries, localization and a set of weak-to-strong rewrites.

## 1. Every line is a move

Give every line one of these jobs. A line that does none of them is cut.

| Move | Example |
|---|---|
| Jab (an insult, a dig) | "Your dress is from the clearance rack. Last season's." |
| Demand | "Kneel. Apologize to my wife." |
| Lie | "I've never seen this woman before." |
| Threat | "Sign tonight, or your mother's surgery is cancelled." |
| Secret (to the viewer) | "MIA (V.O.): He doesn't know the building is mine." |
| Reveal (to the room) | "She is the chairman's granddaughter." |
| Refusal | "No." |
| Offer | "Marry me by Friday." |
| Question that traps | "Then why is your name on the transfer?" |

## 2. The rules

- **Short.** Most lines under 12 words; an average near 5 or 6. A long line is a speech, and a speech needs a reason (a confession, a final verdict) and a cut-away in the middle. Keep any speech under about 25 words, and never more than three lines in a row from one speaker without a reply or a reaction.
- **One idea per line.** Two ideas become two lines, or one is cut.
- **Reply fast.** The answer comes immediately or the silence is the answer. Write the pause as an action line ("She lets it hang.") so it is a choice, not a gap.
- **Interrupt.** People in a fight cut each other off. Write the cut with an em dash at the end of the broken line, and start the interrupting line at once.
- **Answer the line, not the question.** The comeback hits the insult's weakest word.
- **Say the name.** Names land hard in micro drama ("Mr. Blake." "Chloe."), and they help a viewer who joined late. Use them at the turns.
- **The listener reacts.** After every jab there is a reaction: a gasp, a laugh, a glance at the others. Write it as a short action line.
- **No pleasantries.** "Hello", "How are you", "Thank you so much for coming" are cut unless the politeness itself is the insult.
- **No speeches about feelings.** People in micro dramas show feelings through what they demand and refuse. Save "I love you" for when it costs something.
- **Spoken, not written.** Contractions, fragments, real rhythm. Read it aloud: if it does not sound like a person under pressure, it is not done.

## 3. Status lines: the insult and the comeback

The engine of most episodes is a status exchange in public: the antagonist lowers the lead, the lead raises herself.

- **The insult is specific.** Not "you're poor" but "You smell like bleach. Stand further away." Specific cruelty earns a bigger payoff.
- **The comeback is short, calm and true.** The strongest comebacks are understatements delivered by the person who holds the secret. "You'll want to remember my name, Mr. Blake. You'll be signing it a lot."
- **The witness line.** One onlooker voices what the room thinks ("Isn't that the cleaner?"), which sets up the reversal.
- **The verdict line.** After the reveal, one line from the person with power that ends the exchange ("Security. Escort Miss Price out.").

## 4. Inner voice (V.O.)

- Use it for the stakes the face cannot show, the secret the viewer shares, and the plan.
- Keep it to one short sentence at a time, one to three times an episode.
- It should say something the room does not know. Never narrate what the picture already shows ("I walked into the room.").
- It works best over a reaction, an insert or a wide shot, so the speaker's lips are not the subject.
- Format: `MIA (V.O.)` as the cue.

## 5. Deliveries and parentheticals

Ziztu's clips speak the lines, so the delivery note is direction for the voice:

- Use a parenthetical only when the delivery is not obvious from the line: `(whispering)`, `(through her teeth)`, `(laughing, cruel)`, `(too calm)`, `(breaking)`.
- One per line at most, a few words, an adverb or a manner, never an action that belongs in an action line.
- Range inside a scene is good: a whisper, then a snap, then a shout, then a crack in the voice.
- Write stress with italics (`*never*`) sparingly, for the one word the line turns on.
- Give each recurring character a fixed voice line in the bible (age, pitch, pace, timbre), so their voice stays the same in every clip.

## 6. Exposition without explaining

The viewer needs facts (who owns what, who is whose daughter, what the will says) without anyone explaining them:

- **Put it in a fight.** "You married my father for his money." "And you're still angry he left it to me."
- **Put it in an object.** The will, the ultrasound, the badge, the contract, shown with a caption.
- **Put it in the inner voice**, once, in one sentence.
- **Let the wrong person say it.** The villain bragging, the gossip, the drunk uncle.
- **Never "As you know"**, and never two characters telling each other what they both know.

## 7. Catchphrases and callbacks

- Every episode has one quotable line of 10 words or fewer (a status flip, a threat, a refusal) that works as a caption, a clip and a comment.
- Give each recurring character a distinct rhythm (clipped, formal, sarcastic, warm) so a line could only be theirs.
- Write the antagonist's lines with a crack the lead will use later: the boast, the lie, the detail that does not add up.

- A short signature line for the lead or the protector, repeated at the big payoffs ("Clean it yourself."), becomes the series' meme and its trailer line.
- Callback the first insult at the final payoff: the line the villain used in episode 1 is turned on them in the finale.
- Keep catchphrases rare: three or four uses in a 30 episode season.

## 8. Localization and register

- Write for the market. A US audience expects US names, US places and US idiom; avoid translated phrasing ("Young Master", "this seat", "you are courting death") unless the series is deliberately set in that world (palace, cultivation, a Chinese-language release).
- Titles of address set status fast: "Mr. Blake" from the cleaner, "Damien" from the ex, "Sir" from security. Use the shift ("Mia" to "Miss Calloway") as a payoff.
- Swearing: mild and rare in app dramas; the threat is in the calm, not the word.
- For other languages, keep the rules (short, fast, every line a move) and use that language's natural rhythm and forms of address; do not translate English lines literally. In Chinese and Japanese, keep most lines under about 20 characters. In any script without capital letters, write each character cue with a leading `@` (`@林晓`) so it imports as a character.

## 9. Weak to strong: rewrites

| Weak | Strong | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "I can't believe you would do something like this to me after everything we've been through together." | "Five years. And you couldn't wait five more minutes." | Specific, short, and it accuses |
| "Who are you and what are you doing in my office?" | "Who let the cleaner in?" | An insult instead of a question |
| "I want to let you know that I am actually the owner of this company." | "You're sitting in my chair." | The reveal as a move, not an announcement |
| "Please, I'm begging you, don't do this, my mother is very sick and she needs the surgery." | "Name your price." | The lead keeps her dignity; the stakes are already known |
| "I'm so angry at you right now." | "Get out." | Feeling shown as a demand |
| "As you know, the will says that you must be married by Friday." | "Friday. Married. Or the shares go to her." | Fragments; the clock as a threat |
| "That is not true, I have never stolen anything in my life." | "Check the camera. Then apologize." | A trap instead of a denial |
