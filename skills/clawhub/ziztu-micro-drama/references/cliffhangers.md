# Cliffhangers, and how the next episode opens

The cliffhanger is the product. A micro drama viewer pays per episode or swipes per episode, so the last five to ten seconds decide whether the series earns another minute of their life. This file is the library of cliffhanger types, the rules that make one land, and the way the next episode must open.

## 1. What a cliffhanger must do

- **Stop at the peak.** The episode ends on the frame where the outcome is about to be decided: the hand is raised, the door is opening, the result is being read, the name is half said. Cut there. One more second of reaction and the tension leaks out. If the viewer feels satisfied, you cut too late. In a 60 to 90 second episode the cut lands at about 90 to 95 percent of the runtime.
- **Bring new information.** A cliffhanger that repeats the last one louder (another slap, another insult) is a stall. Each one adds a fact, a person, a choice or a danger the viewer did not have.
- **Ask one specific question.** "Will she slap him?" "Who is at the door?" "Is the baby his?" A viewer who cannot say the question in five words will not tap for the answer.
- **Raise the stakes of the main story.** The best cliffhangers turn the season's central question (will she get revenge, will he learn who she is), not a side plot.
- **Be visual or audible in one beat.** A face, an object, a line. Captions can carry text the viewer must read (`ON SCREEN: "Paternity: 99.98%"`).
- **Be paid off next episode.** The question is answered or sharply reframed within the next episode's first ten seconds. A cliffhanger that is dropped teaches the audience that cliffhangers lie.

## 2. The library

Each type lists what it is, when it works best, a sample final beat, and how the next episode opens. Use the type names in the `[[CLIFFHANGER - type: ...]]` note so the checker can track variety. The sample beats are illustrations, not lines to reuse.

1. **reveal**: a line that changes who someone is. Best at act breaks. Beat: the lawyer to the room: "Miss Calloway is the majority owner." Next opens on the room's reaction and the first person who refuses to believe it.
2. **arrival**: a person enters who changes the power in the room (the ex, the real father, the mafia boss, the thought-dead wife). Beat: the doors open, polished shoes, every guest turns. Next opens on the face and the first line.
3. **evidence**: a document, result, photo or recording is about to be read or shown. Beat: she unfolds the DNA report; her face drains. Next opens on the caption with the result, then who it destroys.
4. **threat**: the antagonist makes a move with a cost: an ultimatum, a weapon, a hostage, a leak. Beat: "Sign it, or your mother's surgery is cancelled tonight." Next opens on her answer, which should be a surprise.
5. **danger**: physical peril with a clock: the fall, the fire, the car, the water. Beat: the balcony rail gives way under her hand. Next opens mid-fall, then the catch.
6. **decision**: the lead must choose now between two costly options. Beat: two phones ring at once, her son's school and the man she loves. Next opens on the choice, then its price.
7. **betrayal**: someone trusted is revealed to be working against the lead. Beat: the best friend steps out of the villain's car, laughing. Next opens on the lead seeing it, or not seeing it (dramatic irony).
8. **exposure**: the lead's secret is about to be discovered. Beat: Damien picks up the badge with her real name on it. Next opens on him reading it, and a quick misdirection that keeps the secret one more episode.
9. **interruption**: a kiss, a confession, a proposal or a signature is cut off by someone or something. Beat: lips an inch apart; the door handle turns. Next opens on who walked in.
10. **withheld-answer**: a direct question is asked and the episode ends before the answer. Beat: "Do you love her?" Close on him, mouth opening. Next opens with an answer that is honest and worse than expected.
11. **power-entrance**: the moment the hidden power shows: the bodyguards arrive, the staff bow, the helicopter lands, the villain recognizes the logo. Beat: twenty security guards line the lobby and bow to the cleaner. Next opens on the villain's face falling.
12. **strike**: the slap, the shove, the thrown drink, frozen mid-motion. Beat: her hand is raised; his eyes widen. Next opens on the impact (as a cut and a sound) and the silence after.
13. **countdown**: a visible clock: hours to the wedding, days to the will deadline, minutes to the surgery. Beat: `ON SCREEN: "Friday, 11:52 PM"` as the clerk's office lights go off. Next opens on the scramble, with the clock still running.
14. **contradiction-clue**: a detail that cannot be true if the official story is true (the poor husband's watch, the maid who speaks perfect French, the necklace that matches the photo). Beat: the "broke" husband pays with a black card. Next opens on her noticing, or deliberately not.
15. **mistaken-identity**: the wrong person is in the wrong place (the wrong car, the wrong room, the wrong bride). Beat: the veil lifts: it is not his bride. Next opens on whose face it is.
16. **return**: someone believed dead, gone or forgotten comes back. Beat: across the gala, a woman with the dead wife's face raises her glass. Next opens on the husband's reaction and her first cold line.
17. **pregnancy**: a test, an ultrasound, a child with his eyes. Beat: two lines on the test, and footsteps outside the bathroom. Next opens on who sees it.
18. **overheard**: the wrong person hears the truth (a call, a whisper, a recording on the wrong speaker). Beat: the villain says "she'll never know"; behind the door, she knows. Next opens on what she does with it.
19. **call**: a phone call or message from an unexpected sender. Beat: the screen glows on his face; `ON SCREEN: "Unknown: I know what you did in Lisbon."` Next opens on the call.
20. **power-flash**: the underdog shows a small part of a hidden skill or rank and stops. Beat: the "useless" son-in-law catches the falling vase one-handed without looking. Next opens on someone who saw it.
21. **question-line**: a line that plants a question the viewer cannot drop. Beat: the old woman: "Do you know who your real father is?" Next opens on the lead's answer and the old woman's reveal.
22. **offer**: a deal with a price, often the season's engine. Beat: "Marry me by Friday, Mr. Blake. Or clean your own office." Next opens on his answer, and on why he cannot say no.
23. **villain-wins**: the antagonist wins this round and the lead is at the bottom. Beat: the security guard drags her out as the rival smiles from the stairs. Next opens with the first sign of the comeback. Use sparingly: two in a row reads as misery.
24. **reframe**: the final image changes the meaning of the whole episode (the helper was the villain, the victim planned it, the dream was a memory). Beat: the "kind" nurse locks the door and takes off her badge. Next opens by showing the reframe from the other side.
25. **vanish**: someone or something disappears (the child, the bride, the evidence). Beat: the crib is empty and the window is open. Next opens on the search, with one clue.
26. **transformation**: a power, a curse or a true form awakens (fantasy, werewolf, anime). Beat: her eyes turn gold in the mirror of the knife. Next opens on what she is now, and who saw.
27. **cut-off**: the reveal line is cut mid-sentence. Beat: "The father of your baby is..." Black. Next opens on the full sentence and the face it lands on. Strong, but use it at most twice a season or it reads as a trick.
28. **impossible-fact**: a fact that cannot be true. Beat: "My sister died twenty years ago." Close on the photo taken yesterday. Next opens on the first rational explanation, and why it fails.
29. **trap**: a lock clicks, the lights cut, the exit is gone. Beat: the elevator stops between floors; the villain's voice on the speaker. Next opens inside the trap, with the clock running.

## 3. Which types each genre leans on

| Genre | Leans on | Use with care |
|---|---|---|
| Billionaire or CEO romance | offer, interruption, power-entrance, contradiction-clue | danger |
| Hidden identity, true heiress | exposure, power-entrance, evidence, contradiction-clue | return |
| Revenge (betrayed wife or daughter) | evidence, overheard, villain-wins, strike, reveal | interruption |
| Rebirth or second chance | reframe, question-line, threat (the old fate approaching) | decision |
| Divorce regret | arrival, withheld-answer, return, pregnancy | villain-wins |
| Secret baby | pregnancy, evidence, exposure, arrival | danger |
| Werewolf, vampire, dragon romance | transformation, arrival, threat, reveal | call |
| Mafia romance | threat, danger, power-entrance, decision | interruption |
| Family and in-laws | strike, overheard, evidence, power-entrance | transformation |
| Male power fantasy (hidden master) | power-flash, power-entrance, arrival | withheld-answer |
| Thriller and mystery | reframe, call, vanish, evidence | strike |
| Horror | reframe, vanish, arrival (something, not someone) | offer |
| Comedy and parody | mistaken-identity, reveal, decision (elimination) | villain-wins |
| AI anime and fantasy action | transformation, power-flash, danger, arrival | evidence |

## 4. Variety and escalation across a season

- **Never the same type twice in a row**, and no type ending more than about a third of a season's episodes. Audiences learn a pattern within five episodes and stop believing it.
- **Escalate the stakes, not the volume.** Each block's cliffhangers should cost more than the last block's: an insult, then a job, then a home, then a child, then a life.
- **Place the strongest cliffhangers on purpose:** episode 1 (the series question), episode 3 (proof the series delivers), the paywall episode, and the end of every block.
- **The paywall cliffhanger asks; it does not answer.** Put the biggest open question on the last free episode and the payoff one or two episodes after it. Spending the biggest reveal before the wall leaves nothing to pay for.
- **Alternate a hot ending and a cold one.** A strike or danger beat (adrenaline) followed next episode by a reveal or question-line (curiosity) keeps the rhythm from flattening.
- **Use dramatic irony at the cut.** The strongest endings often show the viewer something the lead does not know yet (the villain listening behind the door).

## 5. Opening the next episode

The first ten seconds of episode N+1 belong to the cliffhanger of episode N.

1. **Re-enter at the peak.** Open on the last second of the previous episode (the raised hand, the opening door), or one second before it, so a viewer who just tapped is instantly back inside. Do not recap with narration.
2. **Pay or reframe within ten seconds.** The slap lands, the door reveals the face, the result is read. A reframe is allowed only when it is bigger than the original question (it is not her husband at the door; it is his mother, with the baby).
3. **Show the consequence.** Someone reacts, and the power in the room shifts.
4. **Open the new question** before 20 seconds. The episode now has its own engine.

Fair play: no "it was a dream", no "it was nothing", no cliffhanger whose answer happens off screen. A fake-out spends the audience's trust; spend it once a season at most, and only if the reframe is a bigger hook.

## 6. Cliffhangers that make people quit

- Bait timed to the paywall: the slap is about to land, cut; she is about to reveal who she is, cut; and the next episode resolves it in a second with no cost. Viewers say so in reviews.
- A situation one sentence would solve, stretched over episodes because a character refuses to say the obvious thing.
- The same ending type episode after episode ("someone walks in" five times).
- Danger that is obviously fake, or resolved by luck.
- A cliffhanger unrelated to the main story, especially at the paywall.
- Cutting after the peak (the reaction shot, then the walk away, then the cut).
- An answer in the next episode with no consequence: the slap lands and nobody's position changes.
- A question the next episode never answers.
- Misery stacking: the lead loses three cliffhangers in a row with no win between them.
