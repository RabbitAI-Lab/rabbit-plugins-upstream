# Episode anatomy: the second-by-second clock

Every episode has one job: move the spring one notch and open one new question, in about a minute and a half, for a viewer who has a thumb on the screen. A COMPRESS episode piles pressure on the lead (a humiliation, a lie, a trap) and still lets her land one small blow; a RELEASE episode pays the pressure off in public (the reversal, the face-slap, the reveal). This file gives the clock for 45, 60, 90 and 120 second episodes, the openings that work, the payoff types, the word budgets and a worked beat sheet.

## 1. Numbers measured on hit AI micro dramas

Measured on ten of the most-watched AI micro dramas and six top app titles (DramaWave, ReelShort, DramaBox), plus the guidance of working writers:

| Measure | Target |
|---|---|
| Conflict on screen | By second 3: a face in conflict, no establishing shot |
| Situation clear | By second 10: the conflict, who the two people are to each other, what the lead wants now |
| Premise clear (episode 1) | By second 45 |
| The reversal | Exactly one real reversal per episode, at 30 to 50 percent of the runtime (two in a minute and neither lands) |
| Micro-hooks | A line, look, object or reaction that lands every 15 to 20 seconds |
| The cut | At about 90 to 95 percent of the runtime, on the peak |
| Shot length | Median about 2 seconds; almost half of shots under 2 seconds |
| Spoken words | About 80 to 140 words a minute in dialogue-led dramas (150 to 210 when narrated) |
| Line length | About 5 to 6 words on average; most lines under 12 |
| Music | Under 40 to 80 percent of the runtime, with a hit on every reveal |

## 2. The clocks

Times are approximate. The order matters more than the second.

**45 seconds** (a teaser, a social part, an ad cut)
- 0 to 3: the hook, the conflict already happening
- 3 to 10: who is wronged, by whom, and what the lead wants, in one exchange
- 10 to 20: the reversal (the lead answers back, or the secret shows)
- 20 to 38: the release beat, or the pressure doubled
- 38 to 45: the cliffhanger, cut on the peak

**60 seconds**
- 0 to 3: the hook
- 3 to 10: the situation clear: conflict, relationship, the lead's goal
- 10 to 20: compression: the antagonist escalates, one micro-hook
- 20 to 30: the reversal
- 30 to 50: the release (the payoff and the room's reaction) or deeper compression
- 50 to 57: the cliffhanger beat
- 57 to 60: cut on the peak

**90 seconds** (the default)
- 0 to 3: the hook (episode 2 and later: the previous cliffhanger, paid)
- 3 to 10: the situation clear; the inner voice gives the stakes
- 10 to 30: compression: the antagonist's move, a micro-hook every 15 to 20 seconds
- 30 to 45: the reversal (a reveal, an arrival, evidence, a status flip)
- 45 to 78: the release (the payoff and its consequence in the room) or the pressure doubled
- 78 to 87: the cliffhanger beat
- 87 to 90: cut on the peak, then black

**120 seconds**
Two mini-arcs. The first reverses near 35 to 50 seconds and ends near 55 to 65 on a mid-episode hook strong enough to be a cliffhanger on its own; the second escalates to the payoff near 95 to 105 seconds and the cliffhanger in the last ten.

Write the cliffhanger first, then the hook, then fill the middle so it earns both.

## 3. Opening moves for episode 1 and for cold opens

Six cold open types cover almost every hit:

1. **The injustice, now**: the humiliation is happening in the first frame (the bill thrown at her feet, the bride dumped at the altar, the accusation in front of everyone).
2. **The flash-forward**: two or three seconds of the payoff to come (the villain kneeling, the lead in the CEO's chair), then "Three days earlier".
3. **Primal danger**: a fall, a fire, a chase, a body.
4. **In the middle, with the inner voice**: action already under way, the lead's voice-over giving the stakes ("If he finds out who I am, I lose everything.").
5. **The premise montage**: a fast narrated setup of no more than 45 seconds (common in anime and fantasy).
6. **Death and rebirth**: the lead dies betrayed, and wakes up years earlier with every memory.

The documented first moves of hit series, to combine with the types above:

- A money ultimatum: a sick relative and an exact sum force the bargain.
- The substitute bride, pushed into a marriage in someone's place.
- Public humiliation at a party, a dinner or an anniversary.
- The cheating discovered on screen, not reported.
- The wedding interrupted, called off, or the groom missing.
- A life-or-death choice that shows the villain's priorities (he saves his lover, not his wife).
- The hidden identity planted for the viewer in the first frame.
- An impossible status mismatch as the premise itself (a teenager who is the family's great-grandmother).
- The outcast marked as defective (the wolfless girl, the unwanted daughter).
- The lead already trapped (a wheelchair, a cell, a frozen decade).
- A rescue by an overwhelming power (the helicopter, the convoy).
- A contract with a clock (a paid fake girlfriend, a five-year marriage).
- Forbidden proximity (her best friend's father, his brother's fiancée).

Never open on a skyline, a character waking up, a drive to work, or anyone explaining the backstory.

## 4. Opening episode 2 and later

1. Re-enter at the previous cliffhanger's peak (or one second before it). No narrated recap.
2. Pay it or reframe it within 10 seconds, with a visible consequence.
3. Open this episode's new question before second 20.
4. On a free social series, restate the premise once in the first five seconds for people who arrive at part 7 first: a line, an inner-voice sentence or a caption ("She cleaned his office for five years. Today she owns it.").

## 5. Payoff beats

Every episode delivers at least one. Rotate them:

- **The comeback line**: the lead answers the insult in public with one sentence that flips the room.
- **The refusal**: she says no to the person who always got a yes.
- **The competence reveal**: the "maid" fixes the deal, plays the piano, speaks the language, diagnoses the patient.
- **The power entrance**: the bodyguards, the staff, the title, the card, the car.
- **The exposure**: the villain's lie is shown to the people it was told to.
- **The rescue**: the protector arrives in time, in public.
- **The recognition**: someone powerful sees who she really is.
- **The romantic win**: a look held too long, a jacket over her shoulders, a "she's with me".
- **The reversal of the bet**: the person who laughed has to pay what they wagered.

The bigger the wound, the bigger the payoff it supports. A small insult earns a comeback line; a stolen child earns a public ruin.

## 6. Micro-hooks inside a scene

Between the big beats, keep a small pull every 15 to 20 seconds:

- a line that lands (a threat, a secret, a sharp refusal);
- an object that appears (a ring on the wrong hand, a document on the desk, a photo);
- a look between two people the third one misses;
- a reaction from the witnesses (a gasp, a phone raised to film);
- an inner-voice line that tells the viewer something the room does not know;
- a sound from off screen (the elevator arriving, heels in the corridor).

## 7. Word and line budgets

At about 2.5 spoken words a second, with action beats and reactions between lines:

| Episode | Dialogue words | Lines (about 5 to 6 words each) | Printed pages |
|---|---|---|---|
| 45 s | 60 to 100 | 12 to 18 | about 0.75 |
| 60 s | 80 to 140 | 16 to 25 | about 1 |
| 90 s | 120 to 200 | 25 to 35 | about 1.5 |
| 120 s | 160 to 270 | 32 to 45 | about 2 |

In all, a 60 to 90 second episode is about 350 to 500 words of script (dialogue plus action), and a 120 second one up to about 700.

Ziztu's shot planner gives each scene about a minute of screen time per printed page, so the page count is also the runtime it will plan. `scripts/episode_check.py` measures both.

When an episode runs long, cut lines, not pace: delete the line that explains, keep the line that wounds.

## 8. Scenes and places per episode

- One to three scenes; one is common and strong.
- One to three places; a single room with a door that matters is a complete episode.
- Two or three speaking characters per scene; witnesses react, and one of them may speak once.
- The heading of a recurring place is written exactly as in the bible.

## 9. Episode 1 checklist

- [ ] The first frame is the conflict.
- [ ] The lead's wound and the lead's secret or power are both visible to the viewer by 45 seconds.
- [ ] The antagonist does something specific and cruel, in public.
- [ ] The lead answers back at least once.
- [ ] The engine of the season (the clock, the contract or the hidden truth) is on screen.
- [ ] One payoff beat, even a small one.
- [ ] The cliffhanger asks the series question, not a side question.

## 10. A worked 90 second beat sheet

Series: a cleaner who secretly inherits the majority of the company. Episode 1.

| Time | Beat | On screen |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 3 | Hook | A hundred-dollar bill lands on the wet marble at Mia's knees. Damien: "Clean it. Then get out." |
| 3 to 12 | Stakes | Mia (V.O.): "Five years. He never once learned my name." Assistant Chloe films on her phone, smiling. |
| 12 to 30 | Clash | Chloe kicks the bucket over; Damien tells security to take her badge. |
| 30 to 45 | Reversal | Her phone buzzes. `ON SCREEN: "Whitlock: The will was read. It's yours. All 51%."` Only the viewer sees it. Mia stands and tears the bill in half. |
| 45 to 60 | Release begins | "Do you know who I am, Mr. Blake?" He laughs. The assistants laugh with him. |
| 60 to 78 | Payoff | She drops the badge into Chloe's coffee. "Keep it. I won't need it." The room goes silent. |
| 78 to 87 | Cliffhanger | The office doors open. Mr. Whitlock, out of breath: "Miss Calloway. The board is waiting for its majority owner." |
| 87 to 90 | Cut | Close on Damien's face as it goes white. Black. |

Cliffhanger type: arrival plus reveal. Episode 2 opens on the room, one second before Whitlock's line, and the first person who refuses to believe it.
