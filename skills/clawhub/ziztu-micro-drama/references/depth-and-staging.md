# Depth and staging: scripts that don't come back flat

AI drama frames have a recognizable cheap look. Two people stand side by side in the middle of the frame, square to the lens, in front of a wall. Everything is equally sharp and equally lit, and nothing is near the camera or far from it. Viewers can't name the problem, but they feel it, and it reads as "AI slop" before anyone has spoken. Real dramas are layered: something close and soft at the edge, the people sharp in the middle distance, the place falling away behind, paler and hazier, with air between the layers.

Most of that is decided in the script. A planner and a video model stage what the words place, so a scene written as "they argue in the office" comes back flat, while "Damien argues by the desk; behind him, through the glass wall, Chloe watches from the corridor" comes back with depth and a second story beat. This file shows how to write that without overwriting.

## 1. The depth stack

Every shot has three planes, at different distances:

| Plane | How far from the camera | What goes there | How it looks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foreground | Within arm's reach | One simple thing at the edge of the frame: a shoulder, a glass, a door frame, a lamp, leaves | Soft, darker, clear of faces |
| Subject | 2 to 4 metres | The people and the action | Sharp, brightest, the strongest colour |
| Background | 6 metres to the horizon | The place, a light source, and when you can, a story element | Softer, paler and lower in contrast; hazier outdoors |

Two more things make it read:

- **Space between the planes.** Open floor, a table's length or a stretch of road. Without a gap, the layers merge into one surface.
- **Air.** Light haze, dust in window light, steam, rain or city haze behind the people. It turns the distance paler and outlines the subject.

Different distances matter more than different objects. Three objects side by side are still flat.

## 2. Stage in depth: the background does story work

The background is free screen space that most scripts waste. Micro dramas run on dramatic irony (the viewer knows something someone on screen doesn't), and the background is the cheapest place to put it:

- **The witness**: Chloe filming on her red phone, a step behind Damien, while he humiliates Mia. Two beats in one frame, and the payback now has proof.
- **The arrival**: the rival steps into the far door while the couple argues. The viewer sees her before they do.
- **The clock or the object**: the ring box on the next table, the guard walking up the corridor, the car pulling up outside the window.
- **The reaction**: thirty wedding guests far behind, small and soft, all turned toward the slap.

Write it so the person or thing is in the background from the start of the shot, then let a line, a look or a cut bring it forward. A video model handles "she was there all along, small and soft at the far door" far better than "a woman walks in from far away and comes to the table".

## 3. Places as depth kits

Each recurring place in the bible gets a depth kit: one fixed line per plane. It keeps the place the same from episode to episode and gives every shot in it the same layers for free.

| Place | Foreground piece | Playing area | Background, light and air |
|---|---|---|---|
| INT. HALCYON TOWER - CEO OFFICE | The edge of the walnut desk, a glass of water | Pale oak floor between the desk and the glass wall | The sunlit skyline through floor-to-ceiling glass, hazy and pale, with the city far below |
| EXT. ROOFTOP TERRACE - DUSK | Candles and wine glasses on the next table | The couple's table at the terrace's centre | String lights receding to the far door, the skyline in dusk haze |

Paste the kit's words unchanged whenever the place appears. A changed adjective is a changed room.

## 4. Writing depth into action lines

Ziztu plans screen time from the length of the page, so depth has to come cheap: a clause, not a paragraph.

- **Place people near and far, not side by side.** "Mia stands at the window; Damien stays by the door across the room" stages two planes. "Mia and Damien stand in the office" stages one.
- **Use relations a camera can find**: behind her, across the room, at the far end of the corridor, through the glass wall, in the doorway, over his shoulder, at the next table.
- **Give the far person an action**: "watches", "waits", "films", "stands still". Someone who is only there, with nothing to do, tends to vanish or wander forward.
- **Name everyone, in every plane.** An unnamed figure in the background disappears or doubles. A crowd is a number, a role and a direction: "a dozen staff far behind her, turned toward the desk".
- **One depth clause per beat that matters.** Not every action line needs one. The hook, the reveal and the cliffhanger do.
- **Light that separates**: a lamp behind someone, window light from the side, neon outside. Name the source, as the bright-beats-dark rule already asks.

## 5. The hook frame and the cliffhanger frame

These two frames decide whether people stay and whether they tap "next", so stage both in depth on purpose.

- **The hook**: the conflict in the subject plane, plus one near or far element that raises the stakes. Example: the bill lands at Mia's knees; a step behind Damien, Chloe films it on her red phone.
- **The cliffhanger**: very often a background reveal. The lead sharp in the middle distance, the reveal already standing far behind, then the cut. Write which plane holds the reveal: "At the terrace's far door stands Clara in a red dress, holding the same ring box. Damien turns."

The flat test: read the hook beat and the cliffhanger beat and name the foreground, the subject and the background in each. If you can't name at least the subject and one other plane, restage it.

## 6. Briefing Ziztu and checking frames

- In the Director brief, add one short look line: "Layered depth in every shot: soft foreground, sharp subject, hazy background." At 11 words, it leaves room for the rest of the brief inside 120. The place depth kits in the bible carry the detail.
- When the first frames come back, check them for depth along with faces and costumes:
  - Can you name three planes?
  - Is the subject the sharpest and brightest thing?
  - Is the background paler than the people?
  - Does anything cover a face?
  
  A flat frame is the cheap moment to fix it. Ask the Director to restage that shot with a foreground piece and a background element from the place's depth kit, before paying for motion.
- For motion, a slow sideways camera move past the foreground (a truck or a dolly) shows the depth: near things slide faster than far ones. A zoom flattens it.

## 7. What breaks layered shots in AI video

| Problem | What you see | Write instead |
|---|---|---|
| A foreground object near a hand or face | The glass fuses into the hand, or a face is covered | Put it at the edge: "a candle at the near corner of the table" |
| A speaking face far away | The face drifts and the lips don't match | Speakers stay in the middle distance; far people don't speak |
| "A crowd behind them" | Duplicate faces, strangers walking forward, people staring at the lens | A number, a role and a direction: "thirty guests far behind, turned toward the stage" |
| Thick fog or smoke around the lead | A washed-out face | Light haze behind them |
| "Nobody else is in the room" | Negatives plant people | "The room is empty except for Mia" |
| A reveal person who walks in from far away | They vanish, change, or arrive at the wrong place | Have them already standing in the background, then cut closer |
