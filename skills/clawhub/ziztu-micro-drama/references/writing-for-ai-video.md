# Writing scripts that AI video can shoot

A micro drama script for AI video is read twice: once by the viewer's imagination and once by a shot planner and a video model that take every word literally. Ziztu's planner turns each scene into shots (it adds no events, characters or lines, and gives each scene about a minute of screen time per printed page), and its video model voices each shot's lines inside the clip. These rules keep what you wrote and what comes back the same thing. They come from real productions where breaking them cost a paid retake.

## 1. Cast economy

- **Two or three speaking characters per scene.** A scene with five people talking becomes a blur of reaction shots and drifting faces. If a scene needs a crowd, the crowd reacts and one person speaks for it.
- **Few recurring leads.** Introduce at most four or five named characters in the first five episodes: the lead, the love interest or protector, the main antagonist, one ally, one wildcard. Six to ten speaking characters is enough for a whole season, and four in one episode is the most a viewer tracks. Add characters when an episode needs a new escalation, not before.
- **One name per person, everywhere.** The character cue (MIA), the action lines (Mia) and the bible use the same name. No nicknames in cues, no two names that look alike (Mia and Maya, Jon and John), no two characters with the same first letter in the same scene if you can help it. Ziztu builds the cast from the cue names: two spellings make two people.
- **Name everyone in the frame.** When a character is present but silent, name them in the action line ("Damien watches from the doorway"). A person in the frame who is not named tends to vanish from the shot; a person named in a shot they are not in tends to appear, sometimes twice.
- **Crowds are a number and a role:** "a dozen office workers stop typing", "thirty wedding guests turn". Keep crowd beats short and let one or two named people carry the moment.

## 2. Places and time

- **Recurring sets.** Plan three to six places for a season (the office, the family mansion's living room, her small apartment, the hospital corridor) and reuse them. Each new place is a new world to keep consistent.
- **One or two places per episode**, a third only as a short insert of a few seconds. A 60 to 90 second episode with five locations reads as a trailer, not a scene.
- **The same heading text every time:** `INT. BLAKE TOWER - CEO OFFICE - DAY`, never "Damien's office" in one episode and "the executive suite" in the next.
- **Say DAY or NIGHT** in every heading. Use `CONTINUOUS` when the next scene picks up the same moment.
- **Bright beats dark.** Viewers on phones read faces in clear, motivated light: a sunlit office, a bright hospital, warm lamps. Save true darkness for a single horror or thriller beat, and even then give the face a light source.
- **Each place is a depth kit.** Describe every recurring place in three planes, once, in the bible: a foreground piece near the camera (the desk edge, a glass, a door frame), the playing area, and the background with its light and air (the skyline through the glass, hazy and pale). Reuse those words whenever the place appears. The place stays the same, and every shot in it comes back layered rather than flat (`depth-and-staging.md`).

## 3. Wardrobe, props and the Character Lock

- **A signature colour per lead** (her red coat, his charcoal suit, the villain's white dress) so a viewer knows who is who at a glance in a two-second shot.
- **Costume changes are story events.** Write them in the action ("Mia comes down the stairs in the emerald gown") and list each look in the bible. Otherwise the model keeps the first costume forever, or changes it at random.
- **Props carry plots, so describe their shape and colour** the first time and every time they matter: "a silver crescent-moon locket", "a cream envelope with a red wax seal". An undescribed "necklace" comes back as whatever the model likes, and a different one next shot.
- **The Character Lock.** For every recurring character the bible holds one fixed block: age, build, skin, face and eyes, hair, the signature outfit top to bottom with colours, accessories, and a voice line (age, pitch, pace, timbre). Keep the look line under 45 words. It is pasted unchanged into every episode, because a changed adjective is a changed face.
- **Defeat the "AI face".** Audiences have learned to spot, and reject, the same smooth big-eyed face in every AI drama. Give every lead at least five specific features a template face would not have (freckles, a scar through one eyebrow, a gap in the front teeth, a crooked nose, grey at the temples, a tattoo, a nose ring, a worn watch) and avoid words like flawless, perfect and stunning. No two characters share a face description, a hair colour and a signature colour.

## 4. Action lines the camera can film

- **One action paragraph is roughly one shot or one beat.** Two to four short sentences. A long paragraph gets merged into one long shot.
- **One subject and one physical verb per sentence:** "She grabs his wrist." "He drops the glass." Physical verbs (grabs, slams, kneels, tears, slaps, throws, freezes, steps back) become performances; abstract ones ("she processes the betrayal") become nothing.
- **Write the target state, not the change:** "Her face goes cold" rather than "her smile fades" (a model reading "smile" draws a smile). Never describe what is absent ("no one else is there", "there is no ring"): a negative plants the very thing. Describe what is there instead ("the room is empty except for Mia").
- **Cause before effect, in order.** The knock, then the door opens, then who stands there. A planner shoots in the order you write.
- **Stage in depth, not side by side.** "Mia stands at the window; Damien stays by the door across the room" gives the camera two planes; "Mia and Damien stand in the office" gives it one. Use relations a camera can find (behind her, across the room, in the doorway, at the far end, through the glass, at the next table), and give the far person an action (watches, films, waits). A background witness or arrival adds a second beat at no cost in dialogue.
- **Hide a mystery face in the frame itself:** "a man in a black coat, his back to us", "only his hand on the door frame", "a hood shadows her eyes". A face that is described gets drawn.
- **Open on the action, not the architecture.** The first action line of an episode is the hook itself. "A hundred-dollar bill lands on the wet marble at Mia's knees" is a hook; "The Blake Tower skyline at dawn" is a swipe.
- **Mark only the camera beats that matter.** The planner covers the scene well on its own (a master, reverse angles, reactions, inserts). Use a shot line in capitals only where the story needs a specific view: `INSERT - THE DNA REPORT`, `CLOSE ON MIA'S HAND`, `WIDE SHOT`. Usually that is the hook, the reveal and the cliffhanger frame.

## 5. What AI video stages well, and what to write instead

| Stages well | Write it this way |
|---|---|
| A slap, a shove, a thrown drink | The raise and the swing, then the aftermath; the contact is a cut and a sound |
| An entrance that changes the room | The door opens, the shoes, then the face; everyone turns |
| Kneeling, standing up, walking away, freezing | Plain physical verbs, one per sentence |
| Tears, a trembling hand, a clenched jaw | As inserts and close reactions |
| A kiss or a hug | The almost-kiss, interrupted; a hug seen over one person's shoulder |
| A transformation or magic (werewolf, powers) | Start mid-effect or cut on a flash: "her eyes are already gold" |
| A reveal: the rival, the witness, the arrival | Already standing in the background from the start, small and with an action; the line or a look brings attention to them, then cut closer |

| Avoid | Because | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Long fights with contact | Limbs merge, faces drift | One strike, a cut, the result |
| Many people moving at once | Duplicates and strangers appear | Two people move; the crowd reacts |
| Readable text on paper or screens | Models garble letters | Show the paper from an angle and put the words on screen as a caption |
| Car chases and fast vehicles | Hard to keep consistent | Inside the car: faces, the mirror, a horn |
| Mirrors and reflections | Extra or wrong people | Drop the mirror or write the reflection as its own shot |
| Small children in big scenes | Faces drift, policy filters | One child, close, calm action |
| Identical twins or doubles in one shot | The model merges them | Separate shots, different signature colours |
| Someone walking in from far away to the table | They vanish, change face, or arrive in the wrong place | Start them where they end up, or cut from the far figure to a closer shot |
| A crowd "behind them" with no number | Duplicate faces, strangers walking forward, people staring at the lens | A number, a role and a direction: "a dozen staff far behind, turned toward the desk" |

## 6. Text, phones and screens

Micro dramas run on text: the DNA result, the contract clause, the bank balance, the message from an unknown number. AI video cannot be trusted to write any of it legibly. So:

- The picture shows the object (the report in her trembling hands, the phone face down buzzing, the screen glowing on his face), and the words go on screen as a caption. In the script, write it as an action line: `ON SCREEN: "DNA match: 99.98%"`.
- For a phone, write which way it faces: "the back of the phone faces us" or "we see only the glow on her face", then the caption or the voice of the message.
- Never ask for a logo, a brand or a real app interface. Invent names: Halcyon Bank, the Ardent Hotel.

## 7. Voices, inner voice and timing

- **Speech runs about 2.5 words a second.** A line takes its words divided by 2.5, plus about a third of a second. A shot holds at most about 15 seconds; an exchange longer than that is two shots. Never solve a long scene by having people talk faster: cut lines.
- **Short lines.** Most lines are under 12 words; an average near 5 or 6 words keeps the rhythm of a hit drama. A line of 20 words is a speech; give it a reason or split it with a reaction.
- **Delivery in parentheticals, briefly:** `(whispering)`, `(through her teeth)`, `(laughing, cruel)`. They tell the voice how to land the line. One per line at most, and only where the delivery is not obvious.
- **Inner voice carries the stakes.** Write it as `MIA (V.O.)`. It plays best over a reaction, an insert or a wide, where the speaker's mouth is not the subject, because lips tend to move on a close-up even when the line is a thought.
- **Silence is a beat.** Before the biggest reveal, give one line of action with no dialogue ("Nobody breathes."), then the line.

## 8. Safety and rights

- Original characters only. No real people, celebrities or private individuals, and no real brands as characters' employers. If the user wants a real person's likeness, that needs their own uploaded photos and consent, and it is outside this skill.
- Adults are adults: write their age as an adult ("26") and keep romantic and suggestive scenes between adults. Do not combine "young" with revealing clothing or bedroom scenes.
- Violence is implied, not graphic: the swing and the aftermath, never the wound up close. Policy filters on image and video models stop graphic or sexual content and the job fails.
- Fan fiction of existing franchises can raise rights questions when the user wants to publish or sell. Offer an original twist with the same emotional engine.

## 9. Fountain details that matter for import

- Scene headings start with `INT.` or `EXT.` (or a leading `.` to force one) and sit on their own line with a blank line before.
- A character cue is a line in capitals followed directly by the dialogue; extensions go in brackets: `MIA (V.O.)`, `DAMIEN (O.S.)`. Parentheticals sit on their own line under the cue.
- **Scripts in a language without capital letters** (Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Hindi, Arabic, Hebrew, Thai and others) must start every character cue with `@`: `@林晓`, `@林晓 (V.O.)`. Fountain finds cues by their capitals, so without the `@` Ziztu imports the name and the line as action and the character is never cast. Scene headings keep their `INT.` and `EXT.` prefixes in Latin letters.
- Any other line written entirely in capitals must have a blank line after it, or it is read as a character cue. `ON SCREEN: "..."` in sentence case avoids the problem.
- Notes in `[[double brackets]]` do not print. Inside a scene, Ziztu's planner reads them as writer notes, so keep them short and useful (`[[HOOK: open on the swing]]`). Notes before the first scene stay out of the shot plan entirely.
- Sections (`# Episode 3`) and synopses (`= ...`) do not print and do not become shots: use them to organize a season file.
- Write transitions sparingly; `CUT TO BLACK.` on its own line is the episode's last beat.
