# Pronunciation — Stress, Rhythm, and the Sounds That Actually Matter

Intelligibility is decided by **stress and rhythm** far more than by individual sounds. A speaker who gets every consonant right and the stress wrong is harder to understand than the reverse, because English listeners parse speech by locating stressed syllables. Work in that order: word stress → sentence rhythm → the two or three sounds your first language predicts → everything else, optionally, never.

## Why Stress Comes First

English is a **stress-timed** language: stressed syllables arrive at roughly even intervals and everything between them is compressed. Syllable-timed languages — Spanish, French, Italian, Japanese, Cantonese, Turkish — give each syllable roughly equal time. A syllable-timed speaker producing English gives the listener no rhythmic landmarks, and listeners lose the word boundaries even when every phoneme is correct.

The practical consequence: *"I would have gone to the shop"* is spoken as roughly **I-də-v-GONE-tə-thə-SHOP** — two stresses, everything else compressed to schwa. Producing all seven words at full value is what listeners describe as "hard to follow", even though it is more "correct".

## Word Stress Rules

English word stress is not free, but the rules are suffix-based rather than positional.

| Suffix | Stress lands | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| -ion, -ian, -ial, -ious, -ic, -ical | On the syllable **before** the suffix | infor-MA-tion, mu-SI-cian, es-SEN-tial, eco-NO-mic |
| -ity, -ify, -ate (verb), -graphy, -logy, -cracy, -ary | **Two** syllables before the suffix | a-BI-lity, pho-TO-graphy, tech-NO-logy, de-MO-cracy |
| -ee, -eer, -ese, -ette, -esque | On the **suffix itself** | emplo-YEE, engi-NEER, Japa-NESE, ciga-RETTE |
| -ment, -ness, -ful, -less, -er, -ly, -able | **No change** to the root | goVERNment, HAPPiness, comFORTable |
| Two-syllable nouns | Usually **first** syllable | RE-cord, PRE-sent, IN-crease, PRO-duce |
| Two-syllable verbs | Usually **second** syllable | re-CORD, pre-SENT, in-CREASE, pro-DUCE |

That last pair is the noun/verb stress shift and it applies to roughly a hundred common words: *conflict, contract, contrast, convert, decrease, desert, export, import, insult, object, permit, present, produce, progress, project, protest, rebel, refund, reject, subject, suspect, transport, update, upset*. Get it wrong and the listener parses the wrong part of speech, which is a comprehension failure rather than an accent.

**Compound nouns take stress on the first element**: a GREENhouse (for plants) vs a green HOUSE (a house that is green); a BLACKboard vs a black BOARD; the WHITE House vs a white house. This is the one stress rule that changes meaning outright.

## The Schwa

/ə/ is the most frequent vowel sound in spoken English and it exists in almost no learner's first language as a target. It is the sound of *every unstressed vowel*, whatever the spelling: the a in *about*, the e in *taken*, the o in *police*, the u in *support*, the io in *nation*.

The rule: **spelling does not predict the vowel in an unstressed syllable — it is almost always schwa.** *Photograph* is PHO-tə-graph; *photography* is phə-TO-grə-phy; the same letters carry different sounds because the stress moved. A learner who pronounces every written vowel at full value sounds slow and effortful, and is the primary marker of Spanish, Italian, Japanese and Korean accents in English.

Fastest single improvement available: pick any three-syllable word, find the stress, and reduce **both** other vowels to schwa.

## Sentence Rhythm and Weak Forms

About forty English function words have a strong form (stressed, cited alone) and a weak form (unstressed, in a sentence). Using the strong form everywhere is what makes speech sound recited.

| Word | Strong | Weak, in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| and | /ænd/ | /ən/ — *fish 'n' chips* |
| to | /tuː/ | /tə/ — *going tə work* |
| of | /ɒv/ | /əv/ or /ə/ — *a cup ə coffee* |
| for | /fɔː/ | /fə/ — *fə you* |
| can | /kæn/ | /kən/ — *I kən do it* |
| have | /hæv/ | /əv/ — *should əv gone* (which is why people write *"should of"*) |
| are | /ɑː/ | /ə/ — *they ə here* |
| was | /wɒz/ | /wəz/ |
| you | /juː/ | /jə/ — *did jə see* |
| the | /ðiː/ | /ðə/ before consonants, /ði/ before vowels |
| a | /eɪ/ | /ə/ |

Content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, question words, negatives) get the stress; function words (articles, prepositions, auxiliaries, pronouns, conjunctions) get reduced. Exception: any word carries stress when it is being contrasted — *"I said FROM Tuesday, not TO Tuesday."*

## Connected Speech

Words are not spoken separately, and the joins are systematic rather than sloppy.

| Process | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Linking | Final consonant attaches to a following vowel | *an apple* → a-NA-pple; *turn it off* → tur-ni-toff |
| Intrusive /r/ (UK) | /r/ appears between two vowels | *law and order* → law-r-and-order |
| Intrusive /w/ and /j/ | Glide between vowels | *go on* → go-w-on; *I am* → I-y-am |
| Elision | /t/ and /d/ drop between consonants | *next day* → nex' day; *friendship* → fren'ship |
| Assimilation | A sound shifts toward its neighbour | *ten bikes* → tem bikes; *good boy* → gub boy |
| Contraction of *have* | → /əv/ | *would have* → would'əv |

Understanding these matters more than producing them: most listening failures in fast English are not vocabulary gaps but a failure to segment *"What do you want to do?"* out of **wədjəwannədoo**.

## Intonation

| Pattern | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Falling | Statement, finished, certain | *I finished the report.* ↘ |
| Rising | Yes/no question, or genuine uncertainty | *Are you coming?* ↗ |
| Falling on a *wh-* question | Neutral question | *Where's the file?* ↘ |
| Rising on a *wh-* question | Surprise, or asking for a repeat | *Where?* ↗ |
| Fall-rise | Reservation, an unspoken *but* | *The design is fine…* ↘↗ (it is not fine) |
| Rise-fall | Strong feeling, impressed or sarcastic | *Oh, reall-y.* |
| Flat, no movement | Bored, hostile, or synthetic | Avoid |

The fall-rise is the one non-native speakers miss most often, and it carries a lot of anglophone meaning: it is how *"I could do that"* becomes *"but I'd rather not"* without a single word of disagreement. In the UK it is doing much of the work of understatement (`business.md`).

**A rise where a fall belongs turns a statement into a question**, which reads as uncertainty and undermines the content. Speakers who habitually end statements on a rise (uptalk) are heard as less confident in US and UK professional settings — fairly or not.

## Minimal Pairs by First Language

Drill only the pairs your first language predicts. Two or three, done to automaticity, beat twenty done once.

| First language | Hard contrasts | Minimal pairs to drill |
|---|---|---|
| Spanish / Portuguese | /iː/–/ɪ/, /b/–/v/, /s/–/z/, /dʒ/–/j/, initial s-clusters | sheep/ship, beat/bit, berry/very, peace/peas, *school* not *eschool* |
| French | /i:/–/ɪ/, /h/ (silent in French), /θ/–/s/, word stress | hit/it, heat/eat, think/sink, *hôtel* stress |
| Italian | Final consonants (adds a vowel), /h/, /ɪ/ | ship/sheep, *and* not *anda*, hill/ill |
| German | /w/–/v/, final devoicing, /θ/–/s/ | wine/vine, bed/bet, thing/sing |
| Russian / Polish | /iː/–/ɪ/, /w/–/v/, final devoicing, /h/–/x/, articles absent from rhythm | sheep/ship, west/vest, dog/dock |
| Mandarin / Cantonese | /l/–/n/, /r/, final consonants and clusters, /v/, /θ/ | light/night, rice/lice, *test* with the final /t/ |
| Japanese | /r/–/l/, /f/–/h/, /v/–/b/, consonant clusters (vowel insertion), /æ/–/ʌ/ | right/light, food/hood, *street* not *sutoriito*, cat/cut |
| Korean | /r/–/l/, /f/–/p/, /b/–/v/, /z/–/dʒ/, final consonant release | fan/pan, rice/lice, zoo/Jew |
| Arabic | /p/–/b/, /v/–/f/, /ŋ/, vowel inventory, consonant clusters | pen/Ben, van/fan, *spring* without an inserted vowel |
| Hindi / Urdu | /v/–/w/, retroflex /t/ /d/, /θ/, syllable timing | vine/wine, thin/tin, sentence rhythm above all |
| Turkish | Initial clusters, /w/–/v/, /θ/ | *train* not *tirain*, wet/vet |
| Vietnamese / Thai | Final consonants, /l/–/n/ finally, consonant clusters | *cat* with the /t/ audible, ball/bar |

/θ/ and /ð/ (*think*, *this*) are the most-taught and least-important: substituting /t/, /d/, /s/ or /f/ almost never causes a misunderstanding, because no common English word pair is distinguished by that contrast alone in context. Drill it last, or not at all.

## Spelling Traps

English spelling predicts pronunciation badly. The words that are most often mispronounced by fluent non-native speakers, because they are read before they are heard:

| Word | Say | Not |
|---|---|---|
| comfortable | KUMF-tə-bəl (3 syllables) | com-for-ta-ble |
| vegetable | VEJ-tə-bəl (3) | ve-ge-ta-ble |
| Wednesday | WENZ-day (2) | Wed-nes-day |
| clothes | /kləʊðz/, close to *close* | clo-thes |
| business | BIZ-nis (2) | bu-si-ness |
| colonel | KER-nəl | co-lo-nel |
| receipt, debt, subtle | re-SEET, det, SUH-təl | silent b/p pronounced |
| iron | UK EYE-ən, US EYE-ərn | i-ron |
| suite | sweet | suit |
| determine, genuine, examine | -in, not -ine | rhymes with *mine* |
| epitome, hyperbole, recipe | 4 / 4 / 3 syllables, final -ee | silent final e |
| develop, prefer, occur | də-VE-ləp, prə-FER, ə-KUR | stress on the first syllable |
| photograph / photography / photographic | PHO-tə-graf / fə-TO-grə-fi / fo-tə-GRA-fic | one fixed stress |
| almond, salmon, palm | silent l | l pronounced |
| queue | kyoo (one syllable) | |
| choir | KWY-er | |
| -ough words | through /uː/, though /əʊ/, thought /ɔː/, tough /ʌf/, cough /ɒf/, plough /aʊ/, thorough /ə/ | Seven sounds, one spelling — memorize, do not derive |

## Practising Without a Teacher

1. **Shadowing**, the highest-yield drill: take a 60-90 second clip of a speaker whose English you want. Pass 1, listen only. Pass 2, read the transcript while listening. Pass 3, speak along with the audio. Pass 4, speak half a second behind with the transcript. Pass 5, no transcript. Fifteen minutes daily beats two hours weekly, because the target is motor habit, not knowledge.
2. **Record yourself** reading 100 words, then read along with a native recording of the same text, then re-record. The gap you hear is the work list; nobody hears their own stress errors without the comparison.
3. **Drill the pair, not the sound**: minimal pairs force the contrast your ear is not yet making. Perception precedes production — if you cannot hear the difference, you cannot make it.
4. **Mark stress in writing**: before a presentation, mark the stressed syllable of every long word in the script. It transfers to speech faster than any drill.
5. **Fix one thing at a time.** Two weeks per target. Attempting five at once produces careful, slow speech and no habit.

**Write every pronunciation target the moment it appears.** A word or name the user mispronounces, or wants pronounced a particular way, goes as a row in `## Vocabulary` of `~/Clawic/data/english/memory.md` with the target in the `Say it` column (respelling, not IPA, unless the user reads IPA) and the date. The two or three sounds being drilled, and the shadowing cadence if one is agreed, go in `## Progress` and the `## Due` table; each practice session is a dated row in `sessions/<year>.md` (`memory-template.md`).
