# Business English — Email, Bad News, and Saying What You Mean

Professional English has five jobs and one shape each. Getting the shape right matters more than any word choice: an ask buried in paragraph three does not get done, and bad news delivered in paragraph three reads as an ambush.

## The Five Email Jobs

| Job | Shape | Length | Fatal error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ask | One line of context → the ask, explicit, with a date → what you already did → an easy out | 4-6 lines | No date, or the ask below the fold |
| Inform | The fact in sentence one → what it changes for the reader → what, if anything, they do | 3-5 lines | Chronology before conclusion |
| Decline | The no in sentence one or two → one reason → one alternative, or none | 3-4 lines | Three paragraphs of throat-clearing before the no |
| Chase | Reference the thread → restate the ask in one line → a new date → the escape hatch | 3 lines | "Just following up" as the entire content |
| Apologize | The apology, once → the fix, with a date → what prevents a repeat | 3-4 lines | A second and third apology; passive voice |

**Ask, worked example.** Bad: *"I hope this finds you well. I wanted to reach out because we've been working on the Q3 report and it would be great if you could take a look at some point when you have a chance."* — no date, no scope, three hedges. Good: *"Could you review the Q3 report by Thursday? It's 4 pages; I only need the revenue section checked. Draft attached — happy to walk through it if that's faster."* One date, one scope, one escape hatch.

**Decline, worked example.** Bad: *"Thank you so much for thinking of us. We really appreciate the opportunity and after careful consideration and much internal discussion, unfortunately we regret to inform you that at this time we will not be able to..."* Good: *"We're going to pass on this one — the timeline doesn't work with the December launch. If it moves to Q2, I'd like another look."*

The decline rule: **the no goes in the first two sentences**, and there is exactly one reason. Two reasons read as excuses; a reason plus an apology reads as negotiable when it is not.

**Bad news** is a decline with the stakes higher, and the shape is the same. State it, then explain. Explaining first is universally read as evasion, and the reader skims to the end anyway to find out what happened — so the explanation they were meant to read is the part they skip.

## Subject Lines

- **Under 50 characters** — mobile clients truncate around there, and the first 30 carry it.
- **Front-load the noun**, not the verb: *"Q3 report — review needed by Thu"*, not *"Following up on a request regarding the Q3 report"*.
- **Say if action is needed**, in the subject: *Decision needed:* · *Action:* · *FYI:* · *Reminder:*. A reader who can triage from the subject replies faster.
- **Change the subject when the topic changes**, even mid-thread. A three-week thread named *"Quick question"* is unsearchable.
- **Never leave it blank**, and never write *"Hi"* or *"Question"*.

## The Chase

Cadence, from the original send: **+3 business days → +7 → +14, then close the loop and stop.** Three chases is the ceiling; a fourth costs more relationship than the answer is worth.

| Attempt | Line |
|---|---|
| 1 (+3 days) | *"Bumping this — did the Thursday date work?"* |
| 2 (+7 days) | *"Following up on the Q3 review. Still need it by the 14th; let me know if that's not realistic and I'll re-plan."* |
| 3 (+14 days) | *"Last nudge on this. If I don't hear back by Friday I'll assume it's not a priority and take it off the list — no hard feelings either way."* |

The third one works because it names a consequence and offers a graceful exit. *"Just checking in"* names nothing and gets ignored, which is why it needs sending four times.

**"As per my previous email"** is read as hostile in US and UK business English. If you want the hostility, keep it deliberately; if you do not, write *"Following up on Tuesday's note"*.

## British Understatement

UK professional English encodes negatives as positives, and the literal reading is almost always wrong. This is the highest-value table in this file for anyone working across the UK, and it is not a joke — the mismatch causes real project failures.

| What is said | What is meant | What a non-UK reader hears |
|---|---|---|
| "With the greatest respect…" | You are wrong | He is listening to me |
| "That's a very brave proposal" | You will be blamed for this | He thinks I'm bold |
| "Quite good" | Slightly disappointing | Quite good |
| "That's not bad" | That's good | That's mediocre |
| "I'd suggest…" / "You might want to consider…" | Do this | It's optional |
| "I was a bit disappointed" | I am annoyed | It hardly mattered |
| "Very interesting" | I disagree | He's impressed |
| "I'll bear it in mind" | I've forgotten it already | He'll act on it |
| "I'm sure it's my fault" | It's your fault | It was his fault |
| "Could we consider some other options?" | I don't like your idea | He hasn't decided yet |
| "Perhaps you'd like to think about…" | This is an order | It's a suggestion |
| "Correct me if I'm wrong…" | I am not wrong | He's unsure |
| "Not entirely helpful" | Useless | Partly helpful |

The reverse direction has a cost too: US enthusiasm markers (*"Awesome!"*, *"Love it!"*, *"This is huge"*) read to UK ears as either insincere or as unearned approval, and Dutch or German directness reads to both as rude when it is simply unhedged. Working rule: keep your own directness level, add one warmth marker for US readers, and read UK positives one notch down (`register.md`).

## Feedback and Disagreement

**The three-part shape**: observation (what, specifically, with an example) → effect (what it caused) → request (what to do instead, one thing). *"The estimates in section 2 don't have a source. It stalled the review because nobody could check them. Can you add where each figure came from?"*

Avoid the praise-criticism-praise sandwich: readers learn the pattern within two uses and hear the praise as the wrapper it is, which devalues genuine praise. Give praise separately and specifically.

**Disagreeing without escalating**, in ascending strength:

| Strength | Phrasing |
|---|---|
| Soft probe | "Help me understand the reasoning on X." |
| Alternative offered | "Another way to look at it: …" |
| Clear disagreement | "I see it differently — here's why." |
| Position stated | "I don't think that works, and I'd argue against it because…" |
| Escalation flagged | "We disagree on this. I'd like to take it to <person> rather than keep going in circles." |

Start one level below the strength you feel; escalate explicitly rather than by tone. The failure mode is staying at level 1 for weeks and then jumping to level 5, which reads as an ambush.

**Softeners that work** (they hedge the *frame*, not the content): "I might be missing context, but…" · "Pushing back a little:" · "One concern:" · "Not a blocker, but…". **Softeners that do not**: "just", "sorry to be a pain", "this is probably stupid" — they discount the point before it is made (`register.md`).

## Apologies

A professional apology has three parts and no fourth: **the apology, the fix, the prevention.**

*"Sorry — I sent the wrong file. The correct one is attached. I've renamed the drafts so it can't happen again."*

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| "I'm sorry" (first person, active) | "Apologies for any inconvenience caused" (passive, formulaic) |
| Name the actual thing | "Sorry for the confusion" when there was no confusion, there was an error |
| Once | Three times — repetition transfers your discomfort to the reader |
| Then move to the fix | Then explain at length why it happened |
| "Sorry I'm late" | "Sorry if you felt I was late" — the *if* apology negates itself |

**UK vs US "sorry":** in UK English *sorry* is also a sympathy marker (*"Sorry, is this seat taken?"*, *"Sorry to hear that"*) and carries no admission of fault. In US professional English it leans more toward fault, and in legal contexts it can be read as an admission. When writing to a US audience about anything with liability, use *"I regret"* or state the fact without the apology, and take advice.

## Negotiation Language

- **Anchor with a specific**: *"We're at 40k for this scope"* beats *"our rates are quite high"*.
- **Conditional trades**, never unilateral concessions: *"If you can move the deadline to March, we can hold the price."* The *if* is what makes it a trade rather than a giveaway.
- **Ranges signal flexibility you may not want to give.** *"Somewhere between 35 and 45"* is heard as 35.
- **Silence after a number is a technique, not rudeness.** Non-native speakers commonly fill it and negotiate against themselves.
- **"I'll need to check"** is always available and always credible; it costs nothing and buys a day.
- **Closing language**: *"So to confirm: X by Y, at Z. Anything I've missed?"* — restating the terms in one sentence is what stops a dispute later.

## Openers and Sign-offs

| Rung | Opener | Sign-off |
|---|---|---|
| 2 Casual | "Hi Sam," / "Morning —" / straight in | "Thanks", "Cheers" [UK/AU], "Talk soon" |
| 3 Neutral | "Hi Sam," | "Best", "Thanks", "All the best" |
| 4 Professional | "Hi Dr Okafor," / "Dear Dr Okafor," | "Kind regards", "Best regards" |
| 5 Formal | "Dear Ms Okafor," / "Dear Sir or Madam," | "Yours sincerely" (named) / "Yours faithfully" (unnamed, UK) |

Notes: *"Cheers"* is warm in the UK and Australia and reads as odd from an American. *"Regards"* alone is neutral-to-cold in the UK and neutral in the US. *"Best"* is safe everywhere. *"Warm regards"* and *"Warmly"* read as either genuinely warm or slightly much, depending on the industry. Dropping the sign-off entirely is normal in an ongoing thread and is the fastest way to signal a rung drop.

Never open with *"I hope this email finds you well"* (`ai-tells.md`). If you want warmth, make it specific and true: *"Hope the launch went well last week."*
