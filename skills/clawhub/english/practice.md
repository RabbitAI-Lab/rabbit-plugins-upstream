# Practice — Improving On Purpose

Most people who "study English" for years stall at the same level, because study time and improvement stop correlating somewhere around B1. What separates the ones who move is not hours: it is working on the constraint that is actually binding at their level, and keeping a record so the same error is not re-learned every month.

Setting a new focus while the previous one is still unretired is how a learner accumulates four half-fixed problems instead of finishing one.

## Placing The Level

Never ask "what level are you?". Self-report is inflated at the bottom and deflated at the top, and the answer changes nothing you would do. Place it from a text or a transcript you already have, and say what you concluded in one clause.

| Observable in their output | Level it places | What it does NOT mean |
|---|---|---|
| Errors in basic tense and article; sentences under 10 words; reaches for the word and stops | A2 | Not "bad English" — production lags comprehension by a level in nearly everyone |
| Grammatically correct, but every sentence is the same shape; vocabulary is the first word that fits | B1 | Not ready for register work; the constraint is still range |
| Fluent and correct; collocations are off (*make a photo*, *strong rain*); register defaults to formal | B2 | This is where most professional users are and where most stall |
| Collocation is right; hedging, humour, understatement and register shifts are still literal | C1 | The remaining gap is pragmatic, not grammatical |
| Reads as a native writing carefully; only prosody or a rare idiom marks them | C2 | Further work is style, not language |

CEFR levels (Council of Europe) are descriptors, not scores: a person is routinely C1 reading, B2 writing and B1 speaking at the same time. Place each skill separately, and write the three into `## Progress`. Cambridge's guided-hours table puts each step at roughly 200 additional guided hours from B1 upward — useful only as a reality check when a user expects B2 to C1 in a month.

## The Binding Constraint By Level

Work the row you are on. Effort spent on a lower row is comfortable and wasted; effort spent two rows up produces nothing that transfers.

| Level | Binding constraint | Drill this | Stop doing this |
|---|---|---|---|
| A2 | Word count in the head | The most frequent 2,000 word families, as chunks | Grammar tables; the tenses will not stick without lexicon |
| B1 | Range and automaticity | Chunks, sentence shapes, one tense pattern at a time | Learning single words from lists |
| B2 | Collocation and register | Verb+noun pairs, register laddering, the error journal | Accent work — at B2 it yields almost nothing per hour |
| C1 | Pragmatics and precision | Understatement, hedging, humour, near-synonym distinctions | Adding rare vocabulary; you already have more than you deploy |
| C2 | Nothing binding | Whatever the job needs: a domain lexicon, a genre, a voice | Testing yourself; the tests no longer discriminate |

The two lexical facts that set these priorities: the most frequent **2,000 word families cover roughly 80% of general written text**, and comfortable unassisted reading needs about **98% coverage, which takes on the order of 8,000-9,000 families** (Nation). That gap is why B1 feels fast and B2 to C1 feels like nothing is happening: the same effort now buys words that appear a tenth as often.

## The Error Journal

The single highest-yield artefact in this skill, and the one users never keep on their own.

1. **First occurrence is a slip.** Correct it, say nothing more.
2. **Second occurrence is a class.** Name the class, give the rule in one line, and add its row to `## Recurring Errors` — class name, rule, first seen, count (SKILL.md Rule 8).
3. **Every later occurrence increments the count and restates the rule in one line.** No re-teaching, no paragraph. The count is the intervention: a user who sees "×7" fixes what an explanation did not.
4. **Three consecutive clean uses retires the row.** Strike it with the date and leave it visible for one review cycle, so a relapse is legible rather than a surprise.
5. **Review the whole journal on the `review_cadence`**, not error by error. The pattern across rows — all articles, all prepositions, all tense — is what selects the next focus.

Classes, not sentences. "Missing article before an abstract generalization" is one row that covers every future instance; "the life is hard" is a row that covers one sentence and will be joined by forty siblings.

Fossilization is the reason for the count column: an error that has been produced fluently for years and never blocked communication stops responding to correction (Selinker's term for it). Once a class passes roughly ten sightings with no downward trend, switch from correcting to a production constraint — the user writes three sentences that *must* use the structure, and the journal row records whether they did.

## Drilling Chunks

Vocabulary work fails when it stores words. Store the chunk: `reach a decision`, `heavy rain`, `strongly recommend`, `get round to it`. The wrong verb is what marks a non-native writer whose grammar is perfect (`idioms.md`).

- **Default schedule**: review a new chunk at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 21 days, then it is either acquired or it goes back to the start. Any spaced system the user already uses beats this one — do not replace a working habit.
- **Production, not recognition.** The test is "use it in a sentence about your own week", never "what does it mean". Recognition passes long before production does, and only production shows up in their writing.
- **Cap the intake at 5-10 new chunks per session.** Above that, retention collapses and the review queue becomes a chore the user abandons in a fortnight.
- **Expect 8-10 meaningful encounters** before a chunk is available without effort (Nation). This is why "I studied it already" and "I can use it" are months apart, and why re-teaching a chunk on its third encounter is not failure.
- **Drill what they will actually say.** Chunks harvested from the user's own drafts and calls outperform any frequency list, because the retrieval context in practice is the retrieval context in use.

## Input And Output

Both are needed and they are not interchangeable. Input builds the store; output is the only thing that turns the store into automatic production, because it forces retrieval under time pressure.

- **Input** works when it is a little above current level and *interesting enough to continue* — the second condition decides whether it happens at all. Fifteen minutes daily of something they would read anyway beats an hour of graded material weekly.
- **Output** must have a real recipient. A drill nobody reads produces no retrieval pressure. The cheapest real recipients: the user's own work email, a comment on a thread, a voice note to a friend.
- **Speaking is the skill that lags**, and the only fix is speaking time. Shadowing is the highest-yield unattended drill and it is a pronunciation procedure — the five passes are in `pronunciation.md`.
- **Listening failures at B2+ are usually segmentation, not vocabulary**: the words are known, the connected-speech reductions are not (`pronunciation.md`).

## Correction Posture

`correction_mode` decides the shape; the channel decides the timing. The evidence splits cleanly and the split is operational:

| Channel | Timing | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Writing | Immediate, at the point of error | The text is still in front of them; the correction costs nothing and lands on the exact word |
| Speaking | Batched, after the exchange | Interrupting fluency costs more than the error does, and it stops the user talking |
| A live call with third parties | Never during; batch after | Correcting someone in front of colleagues damages willingness to speak far more than any error damages meaning |

| `correction_mode` | What the user gets |
|---|---|
| `silent` | The corrected text only. Nothing is explained unless asked |
| `inline-marked` | The corrected text with each change marked, no rules |
| `explained` | The corrected text, then the rule for each class in one line — the mode practice runs in |

Two corrections per batch is the ceiling worth giving in speech. Pick the two that block meaning, and let the rest go into the journal without being voiced.

## Session Shapes

Twenty-five focused minutes with a record beats two unstructured hours. Three shapes, each ending in a `sessions/<year>.md` row:

- **Writing session (25 min)**: they write something real (5 min) → correction pass in `correction_mode` (5) → the classes go to the journal (2) → they rewrite the same text applying the rules (10) → new chunks harvested from their own attempt (3). The rewrite is the part that transfers; skipping it makes the session a proofreading service.
- **Speaking session (20 min)**: a topic they care about, no notes (8) → the user hears the recording or the transcript (4) → two corrections voiced, the rest batched (3) → the same topic again (5). The second attempt is the measurement.
- **Review session (15 min)**: journal rows with counts, chunks due today, one retired row confirmed. Nothing new is introduced.

## Cadence And Review

Every accepted cadence becomes a row in `## Due` of `memory.md`, checked at the start of a session and stated when overdue — a statement, not a question.

| Cadence | Default | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Error-journal review | monthly (`review_cadence`) | Selects the next focus from the pattern across rows |
| Chunk review | weekly | Runs the spaced queue; retires and re-queues |
| Speaking session | weekly | The only cadence that moves the lagging skill |
| Level re-assessment | every 3-6 months | Written to `artifacts/` with the evidence, never from self-report |

Re-assessing more often than quarterly measures noise. Re-assessing less often lets a retired focus quietly come back.

## Plateaus

"I've been at the same level for two years" has four causes and they need different answers:

| Symptom | Cause | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Comfortable in everything they do in English | No stretch — the constraint is never touched | Change the task, not the hours: a harder audience, a longer text, an unscripted call |
| Errors are known and still produced | Fossilization | Production constraints, not more correction (→ The Error Journal) |
| Vocabulary feels stuck at B2 | The frequency wall: the next words are ten times rarer | Narrow to one domain lexicon; general reading stops paying here |
| Fluent but "people still don't get me" | Prosody and stress, not grammar | Stress and rhythm before sounds (`pronunciation.md`) |
| Anything else | Measure before treating | Compare a current sample against one from six months ago; without the old sample, the plateau is unverifiable |

**Write the session and what moved.** Every practice session, call debrief or batched correction is a dated row in `sessions/<year>.md` — type, what was worked, errors caught, and what the user could do that they could not before. Second sightings of any error class go to `## Recurring Errors` in `~/Clawic/data/english/memory.md` in the same turn, new chunks to `## Vocabulary`, any agreed cadence to `## Due`, and a level assessment long enough to be re-read to `artifacts/<kebab-name>.md` with its `## Boxes` line (`memory-template.md`). A practice history with no outcome column measures attendance.
