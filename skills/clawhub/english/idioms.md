# Idioms, Phrasal Verbs, Collocations, and Slang

Collocation is where flawless grammar gets caught. *Do a mistake*, *make a photo*, *strong rain* break no rule and are instantly non-native, because English pairs are memorized, not derived. Idioms and slang carry a second risk that collocations do not: they date, and a dated one is louder than a formal one.

## Collocations — The Real Fluency Layer

Learn the chunk, never the single word. *Decision* is useless; *make a decision*, *take a decision* (UK), *reach a decision*, *reverse a decision* is the usable unit.

**The five verbs that carry most collocation errors:**

| Verb | Takes | Not |
|---|---|---|
| make | a decision, a mistake, an effort, progress, money, a suggestion, a phone call, sense, an appointment, a difference | make a photo, make a party, make sport, make homework |
| do | homework, business, research, the dishes, a favour, damage, harm, your best, an exercise | do a mistake, do a decision, do a party |
| take | a photo, a break, a look, a seat, notes, medicine, a shower, responsibility, place | take a decision (US), take an exam grade |
| have | breakfast, a shower, a look, a good time, a problem, an idea, an argument, a baby | have 30 years (age), have reason (be right) |
| get | tired, married, lost, better, a job, in touch, ready, permission | get born, get a decision |

**Adjective-noun pairs that must be learned as pairs:** heavy rain / traffic / smoker (not *strong*) · strong coffee / accent / opinion / possibility (not *heavy*) · high probability / risk / standards / expectations (not *big*) · deep sleep / regret / trouble (not *big*) · wide range / gap / audience (not *large*) · close friend / call / relationship · fast food but quick meal · big mistake but serious error.

**Intensifier + adjective pairs**: utterly ridiculous · bitterly disappointed · deeply concerned · highly likely · widely accepted · fully aware · painfully obvious · sorely tempted · richly deserved. Each is fixed: *deeply likely* and *highly disappointed* are wrong for no derivable reason.

Where to check a pair you are unsure of: search the exact chunk in quotes and compare frequencies, or consult a learner's collocation dictionary. Do not reason it out — there is no rule to reason from.

## Phrasal Verbs

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a particle whose meaning is not the sum of its parts. English has thousands; roughly 150 cover everyday use, and they are the register marker that separates conversational English from translated English (*put off* vs *postpone*, *find out* vs *ascertain*).

**Separability**, the rule most learners never get taught:

| Type | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Separable, transitive | Object goes either side of the particle — but a **pronoun must go in the middle** | *turn off the light* / *turn the light off* / *turn it off* — never *turn off it* |
| Inseparable, transitive | Object always after the particle | *look after the kids* / *look after them* — never *look the kids after* |
| Intransitive | No object at all | *break down*, *grow up*, *show up* |
| Three-part | Verb + particle + preposition, never separated | *put up with*, *look forward to*, *get on with*, *come up with*, *run out of* |

Ones with a meaning nowhere near their parts, ranked by how often they trip people:

| Phrasal verb | Means | Trap |
|---|---|---|
| put off | postpone; also disgust | Two meanings, opposite tone |
| put up with | tolerate | Three-part, never split |
| take off | remove; leave the ground; become successful | Three unrelated senses |
| turn down | reject; reduce volume | |
| come across | find by chance; give an impression | *He comes across as arrogant* |
| get away with | avoid punishment for | |
| run into | meet by chance; encounter a problem | |
| look into | investigate | Not *look inside* |
| bring up | raise a topic; raise a child | |
| carry out | perform (a task, a study) | The formal-register phrasal verb |
| fall through | fail to happen | *The deal fell through* |
| go over | review | vs *go through* = experience or search |
| sort out | resolve (UK, very common) | Reads distinctly British |
| figure out | work out (US) | UK prefers *work out* |
| follow up | check on progress | Noun form is *a follow-up* (hyphenated) |
| roll out | launch progressively | |
| back up | support; make a copy | Noun *a backup*, verb *back up* (`mechanics.md`) |

**Register note:** phrasal verbs are rungs 1-3 and the Latinate single verb is rungs 4-5. *We'll look into it* (neutral) / *We will investigate* (professional). Neither is better; mixing them inside one paragraph is the error.

## Idioms Worth Knowing

Grouped by what they do, with a register tag. Understanding all of them matters; producing them is optional.

**Agreement and reaction**: fair enough · makes sense · that tracks · I'm with you on that · no argument here · that's a fair point · you're not wrong (grudging agreement).

**Disagreement, softened**: I see it differently · I'm not sure that follows · that's one way to look at it (often means *no*) · I take your point, but · with respect (UK: precedes a flat contradiction).

**Uncertainty and hedging**: your guess is as good as mine · it's up in the air · hard to say · we'll see · touch and go (precarious).

**Difficulty and effort**: a long shot · an uphill battle · easier said than done · bite the bullet · take the plunge · a steep learning curve · in at the deep end.

**Failure and risk**: back to the drawing board · fell through · a shot in the dark · put all your eggs in one basket · cut your losses · the wheels came off.

**Time and urgency**: down to the wire · at the eleventh hour · in the pipeline · on the back burner · sooner rather than later · kick the can down the road.

**Closing and resolution**: call it a day · wrap it up · that ship has sailed · square one · touch base (corporate, disliked) · circle back (corporate, disliked) · loop you in.

**Money and cost**: ballpark figure · break even · cost an arm and a leg · a drop in the ocean (UK) / a drop in the bucket (US) · foot the bill · cutting corners.

**People and behaviour**: on the same page · pull your weight · throw someone under the bus · play devil's advocate · a safe pair of hands (UK) · has your back.

Two to avoid in professional writing regardless of register: sporting metaphors that do not travel (*a home run*, *a sticky wicket*, *out of left field* — opaque to anyone who does not know the sport) and military metaphors in ordinary work contexts (*in the trenches*, *bite the bullet* is borderline).

## Slang and Its Shelf Life

Slang has a half-life of roughly 3-5 years before it marks the speaker's age rather than their fluency, and using it a year late is a stronger tell than not using it at all.

| Layer | Examples | Shelf life | Safe to produce? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Permanent informal | cool, fine, mate (UK/AU), buddy (US), no worries, sure thing, hang on | Decades | Yes, at rungs 1-2 |
| Stable current | legit, solid, sketchy, low-key, take (as a noun: *hot take*), vibe, ghost (verb) | 5-15 years | Yes, with an audience you know |
| Volatile | anything currently peaking in short-form video | 1-3 years | Understand it, do not produce it |
| Already dated | on fleek, YOLO, epic fail, bae, adulting, lit | Expired | No — these are the loudest possible tell |

Regional slang does not transfer. *Mate* and *cheers* are neutral in the UK and Australia and read as affected from an American; *y'all* is regional US; *heaps* and *arvo* are Australian; *grand* meaning *fine* is Irish (`varieties.md`).

Text-speak has its own register: *lol*, *lmao*, *haha*, *ok/okay/k* differ in warmth (*k* alone reads as annoyance in most anglophone chat cultures), and full stops at the end of a one-line chat message read as curt to under-35 readers in the US and UK. Both are real conventions, not errors.

## Register Tags

When you offer an expression, offer it tagged. An untagged idiom is how a learner ends up saying *"I'm knackered"* to a client.

| Tag | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| [1] | Friends only | knackered, gutted, mate, dude |
| [2] | Casual work | heads up, no worries, quick one, sort it out |
| [3] | Neutral professional | follow up, look into, in the pipeline, ballpark |
| [4] | Professional/client | address the issue, at your convenience, subsequently |
| [5] | Formal | notwithstanding, herewith, pursuant to |
| [US] / [UK] / [AU] | Region-locked | sort out [UK], figure out [US], reckon [UK/AU] |
| [dated] | Marks the speaker's age | groovy, on fleek |
| [corporate] | Understood, disliked | circle back, socialize, synergies |

## Using Idiom Safely

1. **Never use an idiom you have not seen in the wild twice.** Half-remembered idioms come out mangled (*a doggy-dog world*, *for all intensive purposes*) and a mangled idiom is worse than a plain sentence.
2. **One idiom per paragraph, maximum.** Dense idiom reads as trying too hard, in native writing too.
3. **Never in text that will be translated or read under time pressure** — idiom is the first thing machine translation destroys (`translate`).
4. **Never in a negative message.** An idiom attached to bad news reads as flippant (`business.md`).
5. **When unsure, the plain word always wins.** *We'll discuss it later* is never wrong; *let's take that offline* can be.

**Write every chunk the user acquires or rejects.** A collocation, phrasal verb or idiom they asked about, got wrong, or explicitly liked goes as a row in `## Vocabulary` of `~/Clawic/data/english/memory.md` — the whole chunk, never the single word, with its register tag and the date. An expression they approved for reuse in a specific situation ("how I decline a meeting") goes in `## Phrasebook` instead, keyed by situation. Past ~15 rows either section becomes its own file (`vocabulary.md`, `phrasebook.md`) with the same headings and its `## Boxes` line, in the same turn (`memory-template.md`).
