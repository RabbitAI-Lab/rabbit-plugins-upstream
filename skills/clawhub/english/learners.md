# Learners — What Your First Language Makes You Get Wrong

Second-language errors are systematic, not random: each first language produces a predictable set, because it is the *absence* of a feature (articles, aspect, do-support) or the *presence* of a conflicting one (word order, false friends) that leaks through. Diagnose from the error class, not the sentence — one rule retires dozens of future mistakes (SKILL.md Rule 8).

## Interference by First Language

Set `first_language` and read the row. Unset means diagnose from the errors themselves — the pattern usually names the language within five sentences.

| First language | What leaks | The three that matter most |

| German | Verb-final in subordinate clauses, *become* for *bekommen*, capitalized nouns, since/for | "I become a beer" → *get* · "since two years" → *for two years* · "handy" → *mobile/cell phone* |

| Arabic | Copula dropped in the present, definite article over-applied, /p/–/b/, consonant clusters | "He teacher" → *He is a teacher* · "The life is difficult" → *Life is* · "I am living here since 2020" → *have lived* |
| Turkish / Hungarian / Finnish | Articles, cases mapped onto prepositions, no grammatical gender | he/she confusion in speech (no gendered pronoun in L1) · "I am living in here" · preposition guessing |
| Hindi / Urdu / Bengali | Progressive with statives, Indian English standard forms, tag questions | "I am knowing him" → *I know him* · invariant "isn't it?" · *revert* used for *reply* (standard in Indian English, opaque elsewhere) |
| Vietnamese / Thai | Final consonants dropped, no plural or tense marking, classifier transfer | Same core set as Mandarin plus final-consonant clarity in speech (`pronunciation.md`) |

Every row's fix is the same shape: name the class, give the one-line rule, add it to the journal on the second sighting.

## Articles — The Five-Step Procedure

Articles are the single largest error class for speakers of article-less languages, and the only one with a reliable algorithm. Run the steps in order; stop at the first that applies. Do not fix articles by ear.

1. **Proper noun?** → no article. Exceptions that take *the*: rivers and seas (*the Thames*), mountain ranges (*the Alps*), plural or descriptive country names (*the Netherlands*, *the United Kingdom*), institutions with *of* (*the University of Tokyo* — but *Tokyo University*), newspapers (*the Guardian*), ships and hotels.
2. **Singular and countable?** → it must have a determiner. English has no bare singular count nouns: *dog*, *idea*, *report* alone is always wrong. Steps 3-4 pick which determiner.
3. **Can the reader identify which one?** → *the*. It is identifiable when it was mentioned before, is unique in the shared world (*the sun*, *the CEO*, *the file we discussed*), is defined by what follows (*the report I sent*), or is obvious from context (*close the door*).
4. **Not identifiable, singular?** → *a / an*, chosen by **sound**, not letter: *an hour*, *an MBA*, *an FAQ*, *a university*, *a one-off*, *a European*.
5. **Plural or uncountable, speaking generally?** → **no article**. *Life is hard*. *Dogs are loyal*. *Water freezes at 0 °C*. *I like music*. This is the step that Slavic and East Asian speakers over-apply *the* to, and the step Romance speakers also over-apply *the* to — from opposite directions.

Generic reference has three forms and they are not interchangeable: *Dogs are loyal* (default, safest), *A dog is loyal* (any single instance, slightly formal), *The dog is loyal as a species* (scientific or formal register only). When in doubt, use the plural with no article.

## Uncountable Nouns

These take no plural -s and no *a/an*, and they are the fastest-recognized non-native error because native speakers never make them.

advice · baggage · behaviour · equipment · evidence · feedback · furniture · homework · information · knowledge · luggage · money · music · news · progress · research · software · staff · traffic · training · travel · work

Countable partner when you need a unit: *a piece of advice*, *an item of equipment*, *a piece of furniture*, *a research study* or *a piece of research*, *a job* (for one unit of work), *a suitcase* (for one bag).

Two special cases: **news** takes a singular verb (*the news is good*), and **staff / team / police** take a plural verb in UK English and a singular in US English (`varieties.md`). **Data** is contested: plural in scientific writing (*the data are*), singular in general and business writing (*the data is*) — pick by audience and record it in `artifacts/style-sheet.md`.

Some nouns are countable in one sense and uncountable in another: *experience* (uncountable = accumulated; countable = an event), *time*, *paper*, *room*, *business*, *chicken*, *hair*.

## Prepositions

Prepositions do not translate. Two-thirds of them are learned as fixed chunks; the remaining third follows a rule.

**Time.** *at* a clock time (*at 3pm*, *at night*, *at the weekend* UK) · *on* a day or date (*on Monday*, *on 26 July*) · *in* a month, season, year, century, or part of day (*in July*, *in 2026*, *in the morning*). Zero preposition before *next*, *last*, *this*, *every*: *see you next Tuesday*, never *on next Tuesday*.

**Place.** *at* a point or an address or an event (*at the station*, *at 12 Oak St*, *at the conference*) · *on* a surface or a line (*on the table*, *on the bus*, *on the second floor*) · *in* an enclosed space or an area (*in the room*, *in London*, *in the car*). *In* the car but *on* the bus, train and plane — because you can stand up in the latter.

**Verb + preposition, memorized not derived.** These are where interference is most visible:

## Tense and Aspect

Four contrasts produce most tense errors.

| Contrast | Rule | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Present simple vs continuous | Simple for habits and states; continuous for right now and temporary | Stative verbs (*know, believe, own, want, need, understand, seem, hear*) take no continuous: *I am knowing* → *I know* |
| Past simple vs present perfect | Past simple for a finished time (*yesterday*, *in 2019*, *last week*); present perfect for unfinished time or present relevance | If the sentence names a finished past time, it cannot be present perfect: *I have seen him yesterday* → *I saw him yesterday* |
| *for* vs *since* | *for* + duration (*for two years*); *since* + starting point (*since 2024*) | Both take present perfect for an ongoing state: *I have lived here for two years*, never *I live here since* |
| Past perfect | Only for an event before another past event already in view | If there is only one past event, past simple is correct — past perfect for emphasis alone is a common over-correction |

**Conditionals**, the three shapes plus the mixed one: *If it rains, I stay in* (real, present) · *If it rained, I would stay in* (hypothetical, present or future) · *If it had rained, I would have stayed in* (counterfactual, past) · *If I had studied, I would be a doctor now* (mixed: past cause, present result). Never *would* in the *if* clause of the first two — the single most common conditional error in every L1.

## Word Order and Question Formation

**Canonical order** is Subject-Verb-Object, and English enforces it far more strictly than inflected languages: nothing goes between the verb and its direct object. *I like very much this book* → *I like this book very much*.

**Adverb placement**: frequency adverbs go before the main verb but after *be* (*I always work late*; *I am always late*). Manner and time adverbs go at the end, manner before place before time (*She spoke quietly in the meeting yesterday*).

**Adjective order**, when several stack: opinion → size → age → shape → colour → origin → material → purpose. *A lovely small old round brown Italian leather riding boot*. Native speakers cannot state the rule and always follow it, which is why a wrong order sounds wrong without being explainable.

**Do-support** is what most L1s lack. Questions and negatives in the present and past simple need *do/does/did*, and the main verb goes back to its base form: *Do you like it?* · *He doesn't work here* · *Did she go?* (never *Did she went?*). No do-support with *be*, modals, or the perfect: *Are you ready?*, *Can you help?*, *Have you seen it?*

**Indirect questions revert to statement order**: *Can you tell me where the station is?* — never *where is the station*. Same for reported speech: *He asked what time it was*.

## The Fossilized Errors

Errors that survive to C1 because they never block comprehension and nobody corrects them. These are the highest-value items in an error journal, because they are the ones the user cannot hear.

| Fossil | Fix |
|---|---|
| "I have doubt" / "I have a doubt" | *I have a question* — "doubt" in English means disbelief, not a query |
| "Please do the needful" | Indian English standard; opaque elsewhere → *Please handle this* |
| "Kindly revert" | → *Please reply* ("revert" = go back to a previous state, outside Indian English) |
| "I will inform you" as a closing | → *I'll let you know* |
| "As per our discussion" | → *Following our conversation* / *As we discussed* |
| "Please find attached" | Correct but rung 4; at rung 2-3 → *Attached* / *Here's the file* |
| "Anyways" | Non-standard in writing; → *Anyway* |
| "The same" as a pronoun ("please review the same") | → *it*, *them* |
| "Nowadays" every paragraph | → *now*, *these days*, or nothing |
| "It depends of" | → *depends on* |
| "In my opinion I think" | Pick one |
| "More better", "most easiest" | One comparative marker only |
| "Every informations", "many advices" | Uncountable (→ Uncountable Nouns) |
| "Since 2 years" | → *for 2 years* |
| "I am agree", "I am disagree" | → *I agree*, *I disagree* — they are verbs, not adjectives |
