# Conversation — Spoken English, Calls, and Meetings

Spoken English is a different register, not the written one performed aloud: it sits roughly one rung below written English for the same relationship, tolerates fragments and false starts, and depends on turn-taking signals that never appear in text. A speaker with excellent written English who cannot get a word into a meeting is failing at those signals, not at English.

## Greetings and Openers

**"How are you?" is a greeting, not a question.** The expected answer is two words and a return: *"Good, you?"* · *"Not bad, thanks — you?"* · *"Yeah, fine, how about you?"*. Answering it in detail is the single most recognizable non-native move in English small talk. Save the real answer for *"How have you been?"*, which genuinely asks.

| Situation | US | UK | AU |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neutral greeting | "Hey" / "Hi, how's it going?" | "Hi" / "Hiya" / "Alright?" | "Hey" / "How ya going?" |
| To a group | "Hey everyone" / "Morning all" | "Morning" / "Morning all" | "Morning" / "G'day" |
| Meeting someone new | "Nice to meet you" | "Nice to meet you" / "Pleased to meet you" | "Good to meet you" |
| Meeting again | "Good to see you" | "Good to see you again" | Same |
| Answering the phone at work | "This is Sam" | "Hello, Sam speaking" | "Sam speaking" |

*"Alright?"* in the UK is a greeting meaning *hello*, not a question about your wellbeing; the answer is *"Alright?"* back or *"Yeah, you?"*. *"You alright?"* with concern in the voice is the real question — intonation is the only difference.

## Small Talk

Small talk is a protocol, not a conversation: two to four exchanges of low-stakes information that establish you are a person before business starts. Skipping it reads as cold in the US, the UK and Australia; extending it past four exchanges reads as stalling.

**Safe topics**: weather (UK: always safe, genuinely used), travel to the meeting, the weekend (before/after), sport if you know they follow it, food, the building or the coffee, current non-political news, how their team is doing.

**Unsafe topics** across all anglophone business cultures: salary, age, weight, religion, party politics, health specifics, marital or reproductive status, immigration status, how much someone paid for something.

**The three-move pattern:** answer → add one detail → return the question. *"How was your weekend?" — "Good, thanks. Took the kids to the coast, first sun in weeks. How about you?"* The middle move is the one non-native speakers skip, and its absence is what makes a conversation stall.

Small talk before a meeting runs 30-90 seconds on a call and up to 3 minutes in person. The transition out is explicit and formulaic: *"Right — shall we get started?"* · *"Anyway, let's dive in."* · *"OK, so what did you want to cover?"*

## Backchanneling

Native listeners emit a signal every 3-8 seconds to show the channel is open: *mm*, *mhm*, *yeah*, *right*, *sure*, *OK*, *I see*, *got it*, *totally*, *wow*. On a phone call with no video, silence longer than about 5 seconds is interpreted as *the line dropped* or *you disagree* — and this is the single highest-value spoken habit for speakers of languages where silent listening is polite (Japanese *aizuchi* is frequent but different in placement; East Asian and Nordic listeners often under-signal for anglophone expectations).

Warning: *"yeah"* and *"mhm"* mean *I am listening*, not *I agree*. Anglophone speakers know this; if you need to withhold agreement explicitly, say so at the end — *"Got it. I'm not sure I agree, but I follow you."*

## Discourse Markers

The small words that manage a conversation. Using them is most of what makes speech sound native; they carry almost no dictionary meaning and enormous functional meaning.

| Marker | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| So | Start a topic, or sum up | "So, about the deadline…" / "So that's the plan." |
| Right | Close a topic, or move on | "Right. Next thing." |
| Anyway | Return after a digression | "Anyway, where were we?" |
| Well | Precede a dispreferred answer (a *no*) | "Well… it's not that simple." |
| Actually | Correct something, softly | "Actually, it's Thursday." |
| I mean | Repair or expand what you just said | "It's cheap. I mean, cheaper than the alternative." |
| Look / Listen | Mild confrontation; raises the stakes | "Look, we've been over this." |
| You know | Invite agreement, fill a gap | Overused, but native; two or three per minute is normal |
| Basically | Signal a simplification is coming | "Basically, it doesn't scale." |
| To be fair | Concede a point | "To be fair, they weren't told." |
| That said | Reverse direction | "That said, we could try it." |
| Sort of / kind of | Approximate | "It's sort of working." |

Fillers (*um*, *uh*, *like*) are not errors: native speech contains them at a rate of roughly two to three per hundred words, and their total absence is one of the things that makes recorded speech sound synthetic. Excess is the problem — *like* every five words marks a register and an age.

## Turn-Taking in Meetings

Anglophone meetings run on short gaps between turns (typically well under a second) and mild overlap is normal in US meetings, less welcome in UK ones. Waiting for a clear silence means never speaking. The technique is to claim the turn with a formula, then say the thing.

| Move | Phrase |
|---|---|
| Get in | "Can I jump in there?" · "Quick thought —" · "Sorry, just on that point—" |
| Get in when someone is running long | "Can I stop you there for a second?" |
| Come back to something skipped | "Going back to what Ana said about X…" |
| Ask for the floor formally | "Can I add something before we move on?" |
| Park a tangent | "Let's take that offline" · "Worth its own conversation — shall we park it?" |
| Check understanding | "Just so I'm clear: you're saying X?" · "Let me play that back —" |
| Disagree | "I see it differently" · "I'd push back on that" (`business.md`) |
| Protect your turn from an interrupter | "Let me just finish this thought" |
| Bring someone in | "Priya, you've done this before — what do you think?" |
| Close a topic | "So we're agreed: X by Friday. Anything else on this?" |

**Starting the sentence before you know how it ends is normal** in spoken English and is how native speakers claim turns. Waiting until the sentence is fully formed is a written-language habit that costs the turn.

## Calls and Video

- **Open by naming yourself**, even to people who have your number: *"Hi, it's Sam."* Caller ID is not a greeting.
- **State the purpose in the first 15 seconds**: *"Calling about the invoice — should take two minutes."* A call with no stated length raises the listener's guard.
- **Say the other person's name once** early. It anchors attention and is expected in US business calls.
- **Signal the end before ending**: *"Anyway, I should let you go"* · *"I'll let you get back to it"* · *"That's all from me — anything else?"* Hanging up after the last content sentence reads as abrupt everywhere.
- **On video with lag**, hand the turn over explicitly (*"…that's my view — over to you, Ana"*): overlap recovery is much slower on a call than in a room.
- **Ask for a repeat without apologising three times**: *"Sorry, you cut out — say that again?"* · *"Could you say that once more, slower?"* One apology, one request.
- **Never pretend to have understood.** The cost compounds: acting on a misunderstanding is worse than one awkward second.

## Buying Time and Repairing

Native speakers stall constantly. Using the stalls is more fluent than a long silence or a translated sentence.

**Stalls**: "That's a good question…" · "Let me think about that for a second." · "How do I put this…" · "Give me a moment." · "So, a couple of things —"

**Word searches**: "What's the word… " · "It's like a — you know when you…" · Describe the function instead of naming the thing (*"the thing that holds the door open"*). Describing around a missing word is a native technique, not a failure.

**Repairs**: "Sorry, let me start again." · "That came out wrong — what I mean is…" · "Scratch that."

**Checking you were understood**: "Does that make sense?" (neutral) · "Am I making sense?" (self-directed, safe) — never *"Do you understand?"*, which questions the listener's competence in both US and UK English.

## Exiting a Conversation

Ending is a two-step formula everywhere in anglophone culture: **signal, then close.** Skipping the signal is what makes an exit feel rude.

| Signal | Close |
|---|---|
| "Anyway, I should let you go." | "Good to see you — talk soon." |
| "I've got another call in a minute." | "Let's pick this up Thursday." |
| "Right, I'd better get back to it." | "Catch you later." |
| At an event: "I'm going to grab a drink." | "Great to meet you — enjoy the rest of it." |
| On a call: "That's everything from my side." | "Thanks, speak soon." |

*"We should get lunch sometime"* is a closing formula, not a plan, in US and UK English. If a plan is meant, propose a date in the same breath.

## Humour

- **UK**: self-deprecation, understatement and deadpan sarcasm delivered with no marker at all. Taking a deadpan line literally is the standard failure; the tell is an exaggerated claim delivered flatly.
- **US**: irony is usually flagged by tone or a follow-up (*"I'm kidding"*), self-deprecation is milder, and enthusiasm is genuine rather than ironic.
- **AU**: heavy sarcasm and mock insults between people who like each other; *"you're an idiot"* from an Australian colleague is often affection.
- **Safe everywhere**: humour about the situation (the weather, the technology, the meeting overrunning). **Unsafe everywhere in professional contexts**: humour about a person present, about protected characteristics, or about anyone's competence.
- **Never explain a joke** in English; if it lands flat, move on with *"Anyway—"*. Explaining it is more awkward than the miss.
