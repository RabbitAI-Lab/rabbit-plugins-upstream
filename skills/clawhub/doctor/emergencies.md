# Emergencies — The First Ten Minutes

Scope: recognition plus the few actions that change outcome before help arrives. Step-by-step rehearsal drills for bleeding, choking, burns and CPR are `first-aid`; what follows is what a person needs while the phone is still on speaker.

**Before any of this**, if `## Boxes` in `~/Clawic/data/doctor/memory.md` indexes `artifacts/emergency-summary.md`, that file is the one to read out to responders: conditions, current medicines, allergies, and who to call. Read `~/Clawic/data/health/profile.md` for anticoagulants and adrenaline autoinjectors before advising anything.

## Order Of Operations, Always

1. Danger to the rescuer first — traffic, electricity, gas, water. A second casualty helps nobody.
2. Response: speak and squeeze the shoulders.
3. Airway and breathing: look and listen for 10 seconds. Occasional gasping (agonal breathing) after collapse is **not** breathing — start compressions.
4. Call emergency services on speaker, or send a specific named person: "you, in the blue jacket, call now and come back and tell me."
5. Only then treat the visible problem.

## Cardiac Arrest

- Unresponsive and not breathing normally → compressions immediately. Depth 5-6 cm on an adult, rate 100-120 per minute, allow full recoil, minimise pauses.
- Trained and willing: 30 compressions to 2 breaths. Untrained, unwilling, or alone with no barrier device: **compressions only, without stopping** — it is not a lesser option for an adult collapse.
- Send someone for the nearest AED at the same moment as the call. Attach it as soon as it arrives and follow its voice; it will not shock a rhythm that should not be shocked.
- Survival falls by roughly 10 percentage points per minute without CPR, and by about 3-4 with bystander CPR in progress. That gap is the entire reason to start imperfectly rather than wait.
- Children and infants: 5 rescue breaths first, then 15:2, compressions to about one third of chest depth.

## Anaphylaxis

- Recognition: sudden onset after an exposure, with **airway** (tongue or throat swelling, hoarseness, stridor), **breathing** (wheeze, distress), or **circulation** (pale, clammy, faint, collapse). Skin signs may be absent — their absence does not exclude it.
- **Intramuscular adrenaline into the outer mid-thigh, immediately, through clothing if necessary.** Autoinjectors deliver 0.3 mg for adults; the Resuscitation Council UK ampoule dose is 0.5 mg of 1:1000. Paediatric autoinjectors are 0.15 mg. Repeat after 5 minutes if there is no improvement.
- Lie the person flat with legs raised unless they are struggling to breathe, in which case let them sit. **Standing up suddenly after adrenaline has killed people** — keep them down until responders take over.
- Antihistamines and steroids treat neither airway nor circulation. They are adjuncts and must never delay adrenaline.
- Everyone who has had adrenaline goes to hospital, even if they feel fine: biphasic reactions occur hours later.

## Choking

- Coughing effectively → encourage the cough, do nothing else, stay with them.
- Cannot speak, cough or breathe → alternate 5 sharp back blows between the shoulder blades with 5 abdominal thrusts (European Resuscitation Council sequence; AHA teaches abdominal thrusts, with back blows as an accepted addition). Infants under 1: 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts, never abdominal.
- Unconscious → lower to the floor, call, start CPR; compressions generate the pressure that clears many obstructions.
- Anyone who received abdominal thrusts is assessed afterwards for internal injury.

## Severe Bleeding

- Direct firm pressure on the wound with anything absorbent, for 10 uninterrupted minutes. Lifting the dressing to look restarts the clock and is the most common failure.
- Bleeding through the first dressing → add on top, never remove.
- Limb bleeding that will not stop with pressure → a commercial tourniquet, or an improvised wide band, applied high and tight above the wound; write the time on the person and never loosen it.
- Embedded objects stay in place; pressure goes around, not on.
- Anyone on anticoagulants bleeds far past what the injury suggests — escalate earlier and say the drug name on the call.

## Stroke

- FAST: **F**ace droop, **A**rm drift, **S**peech trouble, **T**ime to call. BE-FAST adds sudden **B**alance loss and **E**ye/visual loss, which FAST alone misses.
- The single most useful fact for the hospital is **the last time the person was seen normal** — thrombolysis is decided on that clock, not on when the symptoms were noticed. If they woke with it, the clock starts when they went to bed.
- Nothing by mouth: swallowing is often impaired and aspiration is a real complication. No aspirin — haemorrhagic stroke must be excluded by scan first.
- Symptoms that resolve completely within an hour were still a TIA, and a TIA is an emergency assessment the same day: the stroke risk is highest in the first 48 hours.

## Seizure

- Time it. Protect the head, remove hard objects, do not restrain, and **never put anything in the mouth**.
- After it stops, recovery position and stay until they are oriented; drowsiness and confusion for 15-30 minutes is normal.
- Call emergency services if: it lasts longer than 5 minutes, a second seizure follows without recovery, it is the person's first, they are pregnant or diabetic, it happened in water, they are injured, or they do not regain consciousness.

## Hypoglycaemia

- Suspect in anyone on insulin or sulfonylureas who is sweaty, shaky, confused, aggressive or drowsy. Treat before confirming if a meter is not to hand — the risk of treating a normal glucose is nil.
- Conscious and able to swallow: 15-20 g of fast carbohydrate (a small glass of juice, 4-5 glucose tablets), re-check in 15 minutes, repeat if still low, then a longer-acting carbohydrate.
- Unconscious or fitting: nothing by mouth. Recovery position, emergency services, glucagon if prescribed and someone is trained.
- Alcohol, missed meals and unaccustomed exercise are the usual precipitants; a hypo that needed outside help gets reported to the prescriber, because the regimen needs changing.

## Poisoning And Overdose

- Call poison control or emergency services **before** doing anything, including inducing vomiting or giving milk. Corrosives and hydrocarbons cause worse injury on the way back up.
- Have ready: what, how much, when, the person's weight, and the packaging.
- Paracetamol overdose is the case where the person feels fine and the liver is failing — treatment is time-critical and effective early, so a feeling of wellness is not reassurance.
- Opioid overdose (slow or absent breathing, pinpoint pupils, unrousable): naloxone if available, and rescue breaths matter more than compressions if a pulse is present. Naloxone wears off before many opioids do — the person still needs transport.

## Burns And Scalds

- Cool under cool running water for **20 minutes**, and it is still worth doing up to 3 hours after the injury. Ice, butter, toothpaste and creams all make it worse.
- Remove clothing and jewellery from the area unless it is stuck to the skin.
- Cover with cling film laid flat (not wrapped tight) or a clean non-fluffy cloth.
- Hospital assessment for: any burn bigger than the person's palm (≈1% of body surface), any full-thickness or white/leathery/painless area, any circumferential burn, and any burn to face, hands, feet, genitals or across a joint; also chemical, electrical, and any burn in a child or older adult.
- Keep the person warm overall while cooling the burn — hypothermia during prolonged cooling is a real risk in children.

## Heat And Cold

- Heat stroke: temperature above ~40 °C **with altered mental state** — confusion, staggering, collapse. This is an emergency; heat exhaustion (sweating, nausea, headache, normal mentation) is not, but becomes one if mentation changes. Cool aggressively: shade, remove clothing, cold water immersion or wet skin plus airflow.
- Hypothermia: handle gently — rough movement can trigger arrhythmia. Remove wet clothes, insulate including the head, warm sweet drinks only if fully alert. Nobody is dead until warm and dead: continue CPR longer than usual.

## Where This Goes

**After any emergency, write it in the same turn** (`memory-template.md`): the episode row in `~/Clawic/data/doctor/episodes/<year>.md` with what happened, what was given and the outcome; any new allergy or reaction into `~/Clawic/data/health/profile.md` under `## Allergies` with the reaction described, because "allergy" with no reaction recorded is the label that later blocks the right treatment.
