# Research Playbook

Use the entries relevant to the current stage. Match depth to the user's request and field standards. Produce work the researcher can add to existing project materials.

## A short decision record

For complex tasks, record the current decision, checked evidence, main uncertainty, next action, and the condition for completion or a change of plan. In multi-turn work, retain rejected paths and their reasons when useful. Use records available in the current environment.

## Discovery and design

- **Literature and contribution**. Check the closest work's population or materials, operations, comparison, outcomes, and scope. Distinguish rarity in the field, an untested comparison in a specific study, and a search that found no match. For formal evidence synthesis, specify the question, sources, search dates, eligibility, screening, and synthesis methods. Consult reporting guidance appropriate to the study.
- **Questions and hypotheses**. Define the unit, phenomenon, and boundary conditions. Explore different mechanisms, levels of analysis, timescales, or boundaries when needed. Transfer relations through analogy where the mapping holds. For a priority explanation, state a prediction and an outcome that would weaken it.
- **Design and inference**. For experiments, identify the manipulation, comparison, outcomes, timing, and major confounds. For observational work, state the target relationship, selection process, confounding, and assumptions. Align qualitative questions with sampling or materials, analytic approach, and discrepant cases. For theory and computation, specify assumptions, benchmarks, and reproducible checks. For measurement or technology development, specify use, evaluation criteria, failure cases, and comparators.
- **Piloting**. Test feasibility and likely failure points. When feasibility is the objective, prioritize predefined criteria for recruitment, manipulation, measurement, and data quality. Plan follow-up tests for exploratory findings and account for the imprecision of small pilot estimates.

## Execution, analysis, and interpretation

- **Execution**. Obtain data from authorized materials and check applicable review, consent, permissions, and confidentiality requirements. Record versions of materials, protocols, environments, and data. Document when and why deviations occurred and what they affect. Preserve traceable inputs and processing steps where possible.
- **Analysis**. Check units, ranges, missingness, sample flow, exclusions, dependence, and clustering. Distinguish planned and exploratory changes. Use estimates, intervals, and alternative explanations to handle uncertainty. Distinguish an inconclusive difference from evidence that a difference is small enough for the stated purpose. Check sensitivity to defensible processing or model choices when relevant.
- **Anomalies**. Compare the observation with the original prediction and check whether measures, implementation, sequence, tasks, or selection changed. Separate identifiable artifacts and theoretical boundaries. Describe what the study can establish when mechanisms may coexist. Test post hoc explanations on new material or samples where feasible.

## Writing, review, and follow-up

- **Claims and evidence**. Map major claims to traceable results, sources, or arguments, and identify whether they support description, association, causation, or mechanism. Clarify what readers can verify and any sharing constraints. Use reporting guidance appropriate to the study.
- **Peer review**. State the concern, concrete action, location of the revision, and supporting evidence. Explain what the current evidence can answer when a comment raises a separate question or requires a new study.
- **Reproduction, replication, and extension**. To reproduce an analysis, retain inputs, versions, environment, steps, and outputs. For replication with new data, specify what is held constant and what differs. For extension, state the changed condition, competing predictions, and interpretations.
