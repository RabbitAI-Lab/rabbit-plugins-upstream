# Scaling — Past the Hours-for-Money Ceiling

Scope: the four levers past a capacity ceiling, their break-even maths, and what each one costs. Running an agency once it has staff is `agency`; pricing a packaged product is `pricing`.

## The Ceiling

`max revenue = billable_hours_per_year × rate`. With 1,200 hours the ceiling is fully determined by the rate, and every growth conversation is really about which of four things changes.

| Lever | What changes | Difficulty | Reversible |
|---|---|---|---|
| Raise the rate | The multiplier | Lowest | Yes |
| Fewer, larger engagements | Sales cost per unit of revenue | Low | Yes |
| Productize | What is sold | Medium | Mostly |
| Subcontract | Who does the work | High | Painfully |

Pull them in that order. Practices that jump to subcontracting because the calendar is full usually had an unpriced rate problem, and hiring converts an easy problem into a hard one.

## Lever 1 — Raise the Rate

The only lever with no operational cost. Revenue holds while the win rate stays above `old ÷ k` (SKILL.md Rule 6), so a 20% rise survives 60% → 50%.

- Free capacity is the real prize: the same income in fewer hours funds selling, productizing, or a life.
- Ceiling: eventually the buyer segment cannot pay more. That is a positioning problem, and the answer is a different buyer, not a bigger number (`positioning.md`).
- Detail, sequencing and the diagnostic live in `rates.md`.

## Lever 2 — Fewer, Larger Engagements

Sales cost is roughly fixed per engagement, so unit size is a margin lever.

- **The maths**: if selling an engagement costs ~8 unbilled hours regardless of size, three 5,000 projects cost 24 hours of selling and one 15,000 project costs 8. At an 85/hour opportunity cost, that is 1,360 recovered.
- **Retainers** compound the effect: sold once, delivered monthly, with a notice period that makes revenue forecastable. A retainer at 3+ months beats the same revenue in one-offs on nearly every dimension except concentration.
- **The cost is concentration** (SKILL.md Rule 5). Larger units mean fewer clients; recheck the cap before celebrating.
- **The other cost is procurement**: bigger engagements attract legal review, vendor onboarding and longer payment terms (`getting-paid.md`).

## Lever 3 — Productize

Convert a bespoke service into a defined scope, fixed price, repeatable delivery.

**Readiness test**, all four required:

1. The same deliverable has been sold **~5 times** — the number where the shape stops changing.
2. Delivery time variance under ~20% across those instances; higher variance means the scope is still bespoke.
3. The outcome is describable without discovery ("a two-week accessibility audit with a prioritized remediation plan").
4. There are enough buyers to sell it repeatedly (`positioning.md`, niche sizing).

**The economics**: margin comes from delivery getting faster while the price does not fall. An audit that took 40 hours the first time and takes 24 by the fifth is a 40% margin improvement at a constant price — that is the entire thesis.

**What it costs**: discovery is replaced by marketing, because a productized offer must be found rather than scoped. That is a different skill set and a different weekly rhythm. Practices that productize and keep selling the way they always did just have a shorter proposal.

**Escape hatch**: keep one bespoke tier above the product for clients who need it, priced at the full rate. The product is the entry point, not the whole book.

## Lever 4 — Subcontract

Buy hours, sell them at a margin, keep the client relationship.

```
margin      = (client rate − sub rate) ÷ client rate
your profit = (client rate − sub rate) × sub hours − management hours × your rate
```

Worked: client 620/day, sub 380/day, 20 days → gross 4,800. Management, review and rework at 4 days of your time (at 620) = 2,480. **Net 2,320**, a real margin of 19% on the 12,400 billed — not the 39% the headline suggests. Management time is what makes or breaks this lever, and it is always underestimated at first.

- **A 30-40% gross margin is the usual floor** for the risk to be worth carrying, because you now own their errors, their availability and the client's expectations.
- **Review time is not optional.** Delivering a subcontractor's work unreviewed is how a practice loses a client and a reputation in one delivery.
- **You are liable** for the whole deliverable regardless of who produced it (`contracts.md`, `insurance.md`).
- **Cashflow inverts**: subcontractors expect payment on their terms while the client pays on theirs. Never sign a pay-when-paid arrangement with a subcontractor's terms shorter than the client's (`getting-paid.md`).

## Choosing the Lever

| Situation | Lever |
|---|---|
| Fully booked, win rate above 70% | Raise the rate — the market is telling you the number is low |
| Fully booked, win rate around 30-40%, many small projects | Fewer, larger engagements |
| The same request keeps arriving with the same shape | Productize |
| Demand exceeds capacity at a rate the market will not exceed | Subcontract |
| Income adequate, time scarce | Raise the rate and sell fewer hours; do not add people |
| Bored rather than constrained | This is a positioning question, not a scaling one (`positioning.md`) |

## The First Subcontractor

1. **Start with overflow, not core work.** A discrete, specifiable piece with a clear acceptance criterion — the same criterion you would give a client (`contracts.md`).
2. **Contract them** with: scope and acceptance, rate and payment terms, IP assigned to you (so you can assign it onward — a chain with a broken link cannot deliver what your client contract promises), confidentiality, and a non-solicit of your client.
3. **Verify their status**: they must be genuinely independent, invoicing their own business. A subcontractor who works only for you, on your schedule, with your tools, is your employee under every test in `classification.md`.
4. **Price with the margin floor** and quote the client at your normal rate — never at cost-plus-a-little.
5. **Review everything** for the first three engagements, and write what review found — defects and the rework hours they cost — into the `Rework` column of their `## Bench` row in `~/Clawic/data/freelance/memory.md`. Rework hours priced at your rate are what turn the headline margin into the real one.
6. **Own the client relationship**: all client contact through you unless explicitly agreed, and the sub is introduced honestly as part of your team (never hidden — discovery of a hidden subcontractor ends engagements).
7. **Pay them on time, always.** The best subcontractors choose who they work for on exactly this, and a reliable bench is worth more than a few days of float.

Record them in the shared `~/Clawic/data/contacts/contacts.md` (role: subcontractor) and their rate, margin and rework in `## Bench`.

## White-Label and Partnerships

- **White-label for an agency**: they sell, you deliver invisibly, at 50-75% of what they bill. Good channel, poor identity — no case study, no client contact, no referrals (`pipeline.md`).
- **Partnership with an adjacent trade** (designer ↔ developer ↔ copywriter ↔ marketer): each brings the other into engagements at full rate, or one contracts and subcontracts the other. Agree in advance who holds the client, who carries liability, and how referral fees work — commonly 5-10% of first-engagement value.
- **Never both-side a referral fee**, and never take a margin on a partner's work without telling the client the work is subcontracted where the contract requires it.
- **Collectives** — several freelancers sharing a brand and pipeline while invoicing separately — give agency reach without agency overhead. They fail on unclear client ownership and on unequal selling; write both down before the first shared engagement.

## Becoming an Agency

The step where the job changes profession. Signals it is genuinely the right move:

- Demand exceeds two people's capacity **consistently for two or more quarters**, not in one good season.
- Subcontracting has been run profitably for a while, with the margin measured, not assumed.
- The buyer actually wants a team (continuity, cover, scale) rather than the individual they hired.
- You would rather sell and manage than deliver — the honest question, and the one most people answer wrongly.

Costs to name plainly: payroll is a fixed cost against variable revenue; utilization must run 70-80% to work; you stop being the practitioner; and the practice becomes hard to shrink in a downturn. Running it from there is `agency`.

## Products and Other Income

Non-hours revenue, ordered by how well it fits an existing practice.

| Form | Realistic contribution | Fit |
|---|---|---|
| Templates, components, assets from work already done | Small but near-zero marginal cost | High — it is a by-product |
| A course or workshop in the niche | Meaningful for a practice with an audience | Medium — needs the audience first |
| Group coaching or office hours | Leverages expertise, caps at your calendar | Medium |
| Paid community or newsletter | Slow, compounding, needs consistency | Medium |
| Affiliate and referral income | Small, and only for tools you actually use | Low, and it costs credibility if faked |
| SaaS or a real product | Can exceed the practice entirely; usually takes years and kills the practice's selling time in the meantime | Low compatibility — treat as a separate business (`startup`) |

Rule of thumb: a product built **from work already delivered** is a by-product; a product built from scratch is a different company competing with your practice for the same hours. Say which one is being proposed.
