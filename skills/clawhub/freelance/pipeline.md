# Pipeline — Finding Work Before You Need It

## Coverage Ratio

`coverage = value of live opportunities ÷ revenue target for the period`.

- **3-4× is healthy** for a practice winning roughly a third of what it quotes. Derive your own: `coverage needed = 1 ÷ win_rate`, taken from `## Win/Loss`. A 50% win rate needs 2×; a 20% win rate needs 5×.
- Count an opportunity only when it has a named person, a plausible budget, and a next step with a date. Everything else is a hope with a company name attached.
- **Lag is the reason to hold coverage**: from first contact to cash is typically 4-10 weeks for direct clients, longer with procurement. Selling starts when the calendar is full, not when it empties — the queue you build today pays in two months.
- Coverage under 2× triggers the dry-spell protocol immediately, not after the current project ends.

## Channel Mix

Run **two active channels plus one being tested**. One channel is a single point of failure; four are none of them worked properly.

| Channel | Lead time | Cost | Rate ceiling | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Referrals and repeat clients | Shortest — often pre-sold | Zero cash, needs delivery quality and asking | Highest; price is rarely the objection | Every established practice; the majority of solo revenue in most surveys |
| Former colleagues and employers | Short | Zero | High | The first six months, and after any career move |
| Cold outreach | 4-12 weeks to first deal | Time only | High if targeted | Specialists with a nameable buyer (→ Cold Outreach) |
| Marketplaces | Fast first job, slow to good rates | Take rate + lead costs | Capped by global supply | Building initial proof, or a defined bootstrap period (`platforms.md`) |
| Agencies / white-label | Medium | 25-50% margin taken | Their rate minus their cut | Filling gaps; never the only channel (→ Agencies) |
| Inbound content | 3-12 months | Sustained time | Highest, plus inbound qualifies itself | Niched practices with something specific to say (`positioning.md`) |
| Communities and events | Medium | Time, some cash | High | Trades where trust is the barrier |
| Job boards and recruiters | Fast | Zero | Medium; often contract-of-employment shaped | Long contracts — check `classification.md` first |
| Partners in adjacent trades | Medium, then recurring | Reciprocity | High | Designers ↔ developers ↔ copywriters ↔ marketers |

Record which channel produced each opportunity in `## Pipeline`. After ~20 opportunities the source table answers the only question that matters: which hour of selling is worth the most.

## The Warm Fifty

The fastest pipeline in existence and the one most people skip because it is uncomfortable.

1. List fifty people who already know your work: colleagues, ex-managers, clients, suppliers, people from your trade's community.
2. Message them individually with one specific sentence about what you now sell and who it is for. Not "I'm freelancing now, let me know if anything comes up" — that produces nothing, because it asks the reader to do the matching.
3. Ask a question with a low cost of answering: "do you know anyone dealing with X" is answerable; "do you have work for me" is not.
4. Expect roughly 1 in 10 to produce a conversation and 1 in 30 an engagement in the first quarter — enough to matter at fifty, useless at five.
5. Re-contact quarterly with something useful, never with the same ask.

## Referrals as a System

Referrals are treated as luck by most practices and as a process by profitable ones.

- **Ask at delivery**, when the outcome is fresh, not at invoice time and never during a problem. The script is specific: "who else has this problem" beats "keep me in mind".
- **Make the referral easy to forward**: two sentences the referrer can paste, naming the problem you solve, not your job title.
- **Close the loop.** Tell the referrer what happened, whatever it was. Referrers who never hear the outcome stop referring.
- **Pay for them if you agree to**: 5-10% of first-engagement value is the common band for a commercial referral partner. Agree it in writing before the introduction, and never take a fee from both sides.
- **Referrals from a departing client are the most valuable asset of an ending engagement** — ask before the last day, not after (`clients` for the ending conversation).

## Cold Outreach That Is Not Spam

- **Target by trigger, not by list.** A funding round, a job posting for the role you would fill, a migration announcement, a product launch, a public complaint about the problem you solve. Triggers give a reason for the timing, which is what separates a message from spam.
- **Fifteen researched messages beat five hundred sent.** Response rates on a specific, trigger-based message run in the low single digits to low teens; on a generic blast they round to zero and get the domain flagged.
- **Structure**: their situation (one line, proving research) → the specific outcome you produced for someone like them (one line, with a number) → a question that is cheap to answer. No attachments, no rate card, no calendar link in the first message.
- **Follow up twice, then stop**, spaced about a week apart, each adding something (a relevant example, a short observation about their setup). Never "just bumping this".
- **Comply**: honour opt-outs, identify yourself, respect local rules on unsolicited B2B contact (in the EU, GDPR legitimate interest plus ePrivacy; in several countries, an explicit unsubscribe path is mandatory even B2B).

## Inbound

The slowest channel to start and the only one that eventually qualifies leads for you.

- **One artifact that answers the buyer's actual question** beats a blog. A teardown, a benchmark, a template, a public case study with numbers.
- **Publish where the buyer already reads**, not where your peers do. A post read by 200 buyers outperforms one read by 5,000 practitioners of your own trade.
- **Case studies are the highest-converting inbound asset** a freelancer owns: problem, what was done, measured outcome, what the client said. Store each at `artifacts/case-study-<client>.md` with its portfolio rights recorded (`contracts.md`).
- Measure by conversations produced, never by impressions. If a quarter of publishing produced no conversation, the audience or the topic is wrong, not the volume.

## Agencies and Subcontracting In

Working through another agency or freelancer as the hidden supplier.

- **Expect to be paid 50-75% of what they bill.** That is the price of them carrying sales, client management and payment risk — and it is a fair trade when it is a channel, not a dependency.
- **Their payment terms often chain to their client's** ("pay when paid"). Push for fixed terms; a pay-when-paid clause makes their collections problem your cashflow problem (`getting-paid.md`).
- **Non-solicit clauses are normal and usually reasonable** at 12 months. Read the scope: a clause covering "any client of the agency" rather than the ones you worked with is over-broad.
- **You are invisible on purpose.** No portfolio rights, no case study, no client contact unless agreed in writing. Price the invisibility in, and never let this channel exceed the concentration cap (SKILL.md Rule 5).

## Qualifying in 10 Minutes

Score before spending an hour on a proposal. Four dimensions, 0-3 each; below 7, decline or send a template reply.

| Dimension | 3 points | 0 points |
|---|---|---|
| Problem fit | Squarely the thing you sell, with proof to show | Adjacent, would need learning on their budget |
| Budget | Range stated and above your minimum engagement | "What would something like this cost?" with no range after being asked |
| Decision path | You are talking to the person who signs, or one step away | Unknown committee, procurement not mentioned |
| Timing | Start date within 8 weeks, and it is funded | "Sometime next year", exploratory |

Any red flag from the SKILL.md table overrides the score. Log the decline in `## Win/Loss` with the reason — declines are data about who is finding you, which is a positioning signal (`positioning.md`).

## The Dry Spell Protocol

Triggered when coverage drops below 2×, in this order. The order is the content: most practices start at step 6 and destroy their rate.

1. **Re-contact every client from the last 24 months** with something specific to their situation. Cheapest revenue in existence; repeat work needs no sales cycle.
2. **Run the Warm Fifty again** with the current, sharper offer.
3. **Ask three past clients for one referral each**, naming the kind of work wanted.
4. **Fifteen trigger-based cold messages**, researched, over one week.
5. **Reactivate the marketplace or agency channel** for a bounded period, at a rate you are willing to be anchored to (`platforms.md`).
6. **Only now consider price**: a time-boxed scope reduction with a written expiry, never a rate cut. A rate cut is permanent; a "launch scope for August" is not.
7. **Use the gap deliberately**: a case study, a productized offer, the tax admin, the rate card — the work that never happens when the calendar is full (`scaling.md`).

Keep the sequence in `artifacts/runbook-dry-spell.md` with the numbers of the last spell, because the fourth month of a good year is when it is forgotten.

## The Weekly Hour

Non-negotiable, even fully booked: one hour a week reviewing the pipeline (`## Due` carries the cadence), plus two selling hours a day during any month with less than 8 weeks of booked work. The month-4 cliff of a new practice, and the annual cliff of an established one, both have the same cause: selling stopped when delivery got busy.
