# Working With Lawyers

Legal spend is one of the few budgets where the buyer usually cannot judge the product. The controls are structural: scope, fee structure, budget, and knowing which work should never leave the building.

**Before engaging or briefing**, read `## Legal Context` in `~/Clawic/data/lawyer/memory.md` for the existing relationships and `## Matters` for what is already open and what it has cost. Counsel details live in the shared `~/Clawic/data/contacts/contacts.md`; read it before adding anyone.

## The UPL Line

Practising law without a licence is a regulated offence in most jurisdictions, and the line matters for what this skill and any non-lawyer can do.

| Generally safe | Requires a licensed lawyer |
|---|---|
| Explaining what a clause says and what it exposes you to | Advising a third party on their legal position for a fee |
| Drafting your own company's contracts and policies | Drafting for others as a service, in jurisdictions that reserve it |
| Preparing your own filings and correspondence | Representing another person before a court or tribunal |
| Comparing options and pricing risk | Signing off on a legal opinion others will rely on |
| Preparing a brief for counsel | Certifying compliance where certification is reserved |

Self-representation is a right in most systems for individuals, and companies are usually required to be represented in higher courts. Nothing in this skill is legal advice for a third party, and Red Flags routes anything close to the line.

## When To Engage

Cost-justified, not fear-justified. Engage when: the exposure exceeds what a mistake can absorb; a deadline is statutory and irreversible; the matter is adversarial and filed; a regulator is involved; the question is jurisdiction-specific and unfamiliar; the document will be relied on by a third party; or the work is a Red Flags row.

Do not engage for: reviewing a two-page mutual NDA on standard terms, drafting an internal policy, explaining a clause, or producing a first draft that counsel will rewrite anyway. Send counsel the second draft, not the blank page — reviewing costs a fraction of drafting.

The economics: for a routine commercial contract, the cost of counsel review scales with pages and rounds. A useful rule is to spend on legal review roughly in proportion to annual contract value and irreversibility — the same $3,000 of review is obviously right on a three-year $500,000 commitment and obviously wrong on a $2,000 annual tool.

## Finding The Right Lawyer

- **Match the specialism exactly.** Commercial contracts, employment, IP prosecution, IP litigation, data protection, corporate/M&A, immigration, tax and disputes are different professions that share a title. A generalist handling a specialist matter is the most expensive option available.
- **Match the size.** A large firm brings depth, conflict checks and brand comfort at a rate that makes routine work uneconomic; a boutique or solo practitioner brings partner attention at a fraction. Route routine volume to the smaller firm and bet-the-company matters to the larger one.
- Sources that work: referrals from companies at the same stage in the same sector, the counsel who ran a comparable deal for a peer, and bar association or law society directories for verification. Verify the licence and the jurisdiction, always.
- **Conflicts**: run the check before disclosing anything substantive. A firm that acts for the counterparty cannot act for the user, and disclosure to a conflicted firm can create problems of its own.
- Interview questions worth asking: who will actually do the work, what has this exact matter cost you before, what is your estimate and what would make it wrong, and what will you need from us.

## Fee Structures

| Structure | Fits | Watch |
|---|---|---|
| Hourly | Unpredictable scope, disputes, negotiation | The meter runs on emails and internal conferences; requires a budget and reporting |
| Fixed fee per matter | Well-defined deliverables — formation, a template set, a trademark filing | Define exactly what is included and what triggers a variation |
| Capped fee | Work that is mostly predictable | Caps are often "estimates" in practice; get it in writing as a cap |
| Retainer (access) | Ongoing questions, a monthly allowance of hours | Unused hours usually do not roll over; check utilisation quarterly |
| Contingency / conditional fee | Claimant-side disputes with real damages | Percentage rises with stage; check what happens to disbursements if the case is lost |
| Success fee / uplift | Where permitted | Regulated and jurisdictional; caps often apply |

Rates vary by market, firm size and seniority far more than by quality. The lever that matters more than the rate is **who does the work**: the same task at partner rate versus associate rate differs by a multiple, and much routine work should not be at partner rate at all.

Disbursements are separate from fees: filing fees, court fees, counsel/barrister fees, experts, searches, courier. Ask for them to be estimated too — they are frequently the surprise.

## The Engagement Letter

Read it; it is a contract, and it is drafted by the other side. Check: the scope of the retainer (what is included and expressly excluded), who is the client (the company, not the founder personally — this matters enormously if their interests ever diverge), the fee structure and rates by person, the estimate and what happens when it is exceeded, billing frequency, interest on late payment, the right to a lien over files, termination rights, conflict-waiver provisions, and limitation of the firm's own liability.

Conflict waivers requesting consent to act against the user in unrelated matters are common in large-firm engagement letters and are negotiable.

## Briefing

A good brief halves the bill. Send, in one document:

1. **The question**, stated as a decision to be made, not a topic. "Can we terminate this contract in March without paying the remaining term?" beats "please review this contract".
2. **The facts**, chronologically, with the dates (`disputes.md` chronology).
3. **The documents**, complete and in order, with the signed versions identified.
4. **The commercial objective** and what the user would accept.
5. **The constraints**: budget, deadline, and what has already been said to the other side.
6. **What has already been done** — this skill's analysis, the positions taken, the questions already answered.

State the budget explicitly and ask for an estimate before work starts. "Please let me know if this will exceed £X" turns an open engagement into a controlled one.

## Budgets And Control

- Agree a budget per matter, phased where the matter has stages (advice, correspondence, proceedings, disclosure, trial), and ask for notification at 75% of each phase.
- Ask for monthly narrative bills whatever the billing cycle. Discovering a large bill at quarter end removes every opportunity to have redirected the work.
- Name a single point of contact on the user's side. Three people emailing the firm produces three attendances, all billed.
- Batch questions. Six emails across a week cost more than one email with six questions.
- Ask for the deliverable format up front: a two-line answer, a one-page note, or a formal opinion are different products at very different prices, and firms default to the most defensive one.
- Do the fact-gathering, document collation and first drafts in-house. Paying a firm to organise documents is the least valuable legal spend there is.

## Reviewing The Bill

Read every narrative line. Common items worth querying, politely and routinely:

| Line | Question |
|---|---|
| "Attending to file", "reviewing correspondence" with no detail | What was the work and what was the output |
| Multiple fee earners at the same meeting or call | Was the second attendance necessary |
| Research on a point the firm was engaged for its expertise in | Is the client funding the firm's training |
| Time booked after the deliverable was sent | What was it for |
| Partner rate on document assembly or bundling | Should this have been delegated |
| Block-billed entries covering a whole day | Ask for itemisation; many jurisdictions require it on request |

Querying bills is normal professional practice and does not damage the relationship; consistently not querying them changes how the matter is staffed.

## Privilege

Attorney-client privilege protects communications made for the purpose of giving or obtaining legal advice, between the lawyer and the client, kept confidential. Work-product doctrine separately protects material prepared in anticipation of litigation.

What breaks it:

- **Distribution.** Forwarding to people outside the privileged circle waives it, and in some systems waiver on a topic is broad rather than document-by-document.
- **Content.** Business advice from a lawyer is not privileged just because a lawyer gave it. Copying a lawyer on a commercial email does not make the email privileged (SKILL.md Traps).
- **In-house counsel in the EU.** Communications with in-house lawyers are not privileged in EU competition proceedings (*Akzo Nobel*, 2010), and several member states do not recognise in-house privilege at all. Route sensitive EU matters through external counsel.
- **Non-lawyers.** Analysis produced by this skill, by consultants, or by employees is not privileged and is discoverable. Keep candid assessments of weakness with counsel.

Practical hygiene: label genuinely privileged material, keep the circle small and named, separate legal advice from business discussion in different documents, and route investigations through counsel where privilege is wanted (`employment.md`). Common-interest and joint-defence arrangements can preserve privilege between allied parties but need to be documented before sharing.

## In-House Versus Outside

Hiring the first in-house lawyer becomes economic when external spend is consistently high **and** legal is a bottleneck in the business's own workflow — the second condition matters more than the first, because an in-house lawyer's real value is availability and context, not hourly arbitrage. Below that, a fractional or part-time general counsel and a good playbook cover most needs (`clauses.md`).

The hybrid that works: in-house or playbook handles volume commercial work; specialist firms handle disputes, IP prosecution, employment terminations and anything jurisdictional.

## Firing A Lawyer

Clients may change lawyers at will, subject to paying for work done and to court permission where proceedings are live. The mechanics: request the complete file (rules on file ownership and liens for unpaid fees vary), get a status note and the immediate deadlines, notify the other side and the court if applicable, and confirm the handover of any deadlines in writing. Do it between phases, never on the eve of one.

## Building Institutional Memory

Every matter that closes should leave behind more than an invoice: the advice received, the position adopted, the template that came out of it, and what it cost. Otherwise the same question is bought again in two years at a higher rate.

**After any engagement or briefing**, write in the same turn (`memory-template.md`): the matter row in `## Matters` in `memory.md` — subject, firm, fee structure, budget and spend to date in the billed currency, status and next date — with deadlines into `## Due`; the lawyer, firm, specialism and rate structure into the shared `~/Clawic/data/contacts/contacts.md` (identity key = lowercase email), referenced from `## Matters` by name only; legal spend into the shared `~/Clawic/data/finances/budget.md` with its currency; and the substantive output — the advice summary, the template produced, the position adopted — into `~/Clawic/data/lawyer/artifacts/memo-<topic>.md` with its `## Boxes` line. Store the firm's client-portal reference, never its password (SKILL.md, secrets).
