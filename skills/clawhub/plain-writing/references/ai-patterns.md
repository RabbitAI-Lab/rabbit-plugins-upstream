# AI-shaped writing patterns

Use this reference in all modes. Cut these patterns unless protected content
requires the frame.

## Sentence patterns

- **Binary contrast:** “This is not X. It is Y” and “not just X but Y.” State Y
  or the real distinction directly.
- **Negative listing:** “Not X. Not Y. Z.” State Z.
- **Faux insight:** “What everyone gets wrong” or “Here is what nobody tells
  you.” Delete the setup.
- **Colon reveal:** a dramatic label followed by a lowercase claim, such as
  “The useful part: a separate agent grades it.” Write a normal sentence. Keep
  colons for lists, labels, quotations, and grammatically complete clauses.
- **Rhetorical setup:** “Think about it,” “Plot twist,” or a self-answered
  question. State the point.
- **Dramatic fragmentation:** “X. And Y. And Z.” Join related thoughts into
  complete sentences.
- **Fake synthesis:** “Same problem, different angle,” “Both cases come down
  to the same thing,” or “The tricky part is the cycle.” State only a
  supported relationship. Do not invent one to replace the phrase.
- **Concessive loop:** “X will help, but any Y can restart the loop.” Keep only
  supported claims and name the actual condition or uncertainty.
- **Importance puffery:** language that declares an event historic, essential,
  or proof of commitment without evidence. State the concrete fact.
- **Fake-profound kicker:** a final aphorism, cute metaphor, or mic-drop line.
  Delete it. End on the clearest result, decision, or next action already
  supported by the text.
- **Canned AI diction:** “delve,” “ever-evolving,” “tapestry,” “realm,”
  “paramount,” and “transformative.” Prefer concrete language unless the word
  has a precise, literal purpose.
- **Narrator scaffolding:** “The log sequence shows the loop,” “The evidence
  indicates that,” “There is a direct precedent,” or “including customer
  impact.” Delete the frame or abstract recap when the evidence or conclusion
  works without it, even if the detail appears later. State the trace,
  measurement, example, or effect. Keep necessary attribution, uncertainty, or
  inference.

Keep contrast that corrects a real misconception. Keep colons for lists,
labels, quotations, code, and explanations.

## Whole-piece patterns

- Generic headings such as “Where we are,” “Why this matters,” “What we are
  hearing,” “The bigger picture,” and “What comes next.”
- A heading for every short paragraph.
- Source labels replaced one-for-one with polished Markdown headings.
- Repeated heading, paragraph, and bullet shapes that force false symmetry.
- Bullets that are ordinary prose fragments, or a list that repeats the
  paragraph before it.
- An opening that announces the topic instead of starting with the useful
  fact.
- Report-generator transitions that announce evidence, explain what a nearby
  log already shows, or summarize a concrete example into an abstract category.
- A conclusion that recaps every section, announces “the takeaway,” or ends
  with engagement bait.
- Emoji headings, decorative bold, and bold words scattered through
  sentences.
- Robotic rhythm: repeated sentence openings, matching paragraph lengths,
  stacked punch lines, or every section ending with the same summary cadence.

Restructure when the outline causes these problems. A long document can need
many headings or lists; navigation and parallel scan tasks are valid reasons.
Formatting is evidence of a problem only when it decorates, repeats, or
distorts the content.

## Replacements

Delete the frame instead of replacing one stock phrase with another. Keep
useful roughness, domain language, and varied sentence lengths.
