# Learning Methodology — Comprehensible Input Through Media

## The Core Theory: Comprehensible Input (Krashen)

Stephen Krashen's Input Hypothesis (1982) states that language is acquired when learners are exposed to **input that is one step beyond their current level** — designated **i+1**. "i" is the learner's current competence; "+1" is language that is slightly beyond it but still comprehensible through context.

### Why TV and Movies Are Ideal i+1 Input

- **Visual context**: You see facial expressions, gestures, and situations that provide meaning cues
- **Emotional engagement**: Content you care about activates memory formation
- **Natural repetition**: Characters reuse vocabulary across episodes
- **Authentic language**: Real speech patterns, idioms, and pronunciation — not textbook language
- **Motivation sustainability**: A show you love provides 20+ hours of compelling input

### The Problem: The Gap at i+2 or i+3

When vocabulary is too far beyond your level (i+3), the input becomes noise. You can't infer meaning from context when you don't know 30%+ of the words. This is why learners plateau — they jump to native content too early.

### The Solution: Pre-Study Vocabulary

Language Immersion TV bridges the gap by:
1. **Identifying** the specific vocabulary in the content you want to watch
2. **Ranking** it by frequency (high-frequency words = highest ROI)
3. **Building decks** so you can pre-study before watching
4. **Reducing unknown words** from 30% to ~10%, moving the content from i+3 to i+1

## Frequency and Coverage

### Zipf's Law in Language

Word frequency in any language follows Zipf's Law: the nth most common word appears ~1/n as often as the most common word. This creates a steep long tail.

### Coverage Data (English)

| Vocabulary Size | Text Coverage | What You Can Understand |
|----------------|---------------|------------------------|
| 500 words | ~85% of tokens | Basic dialogue, simple plots |
| 1,000 words | ~89% | Most everyday conversations |
| 2,000 words | ~95% | Most TV shows and movies |
| 5,000 words | ~98% | Films, news, literature |
| 9,000 words | ~98%+ | Near-native comprehension |

### Implication for Study Strategy

- The **first 2,000 words** give you 95% coverage — focus here first
- Words appearing **3+ times** in a series are high-value (you'll encounter them repeatedly)
- Words appearing **once** are low ROI — skip them unless interesting

## Optimal Study Workflow

### Phase 1: Foundation (A1–A2)

Use a structured beginner course (Duolingo, Babbel, or textbook) to reach ~1,000 words. Don't start with media yet.

### Phase 2: Bridge (A2–B1)

1. Choose a **sitcom or animated film** in your target language — simpler vocabulary, slower speech
2. Build a vocabulary deck from the subtitles
3. Pre-study 50–100 words before each episode
4. Watch with **target-language subtitles** (not native-language)
5. Log unknown words for the next session

### Phase 3: Immersion (B1–B2)

1. Move to dramas, documentaries, and more complex content
2. Build series-level frequency lists to identify recurring vocabulary
3. Reduce pre-study to only high-frequency unknown words
4. Begin watching without subtitles for short segments

### Phase 4: Fluency (B2–C1)

1. Watch native content with minimal pre-study
2. Focus on idioms, slang, and register (formal vs. informal)
3. Use the phrase extraction feature to learn multi-word expressions

## Subtitle Best Practices

### Use Native-Language Subtitles

- **Correct**: Spanish subtitles when learning Spanish
- **Incorrect**: English subtitles when learning Spanish (you'll just read English)
- **Also good**: No subtitles, if you're at B2+ and practicing listening

### Quality Sources

- **OpenSubtitles.org** — largest free subtitle database
- **Podnapisi.net** — good quality, multi-language
- **Built-in streaming subtitles** — Netflix, Amazon often have good native-language CC

### Avoid Machine-Translated Subtitles

Auto-translated subtitles contain errors that teach you wrong patterns. Always use subtitles created by native speakers.

## Phrase and Idiom Learning

Single words are necessary but not sufficient for comprehension. Much of natural speech consists of **fixed expressions**:

- **English**: "what's up", "never mind", "figure out", "hang on"
- **Spanish**: "claro que sí", "no pasa nada", "echar de menos"
- **French**: "bien sûr", "ça va", "pas de problème"

Language Immersion TV extracts these by detecting **collocations** — word pairs and triplets that appear together significantly more often than chance.
