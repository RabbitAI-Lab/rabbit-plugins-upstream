Ever tried to explain something and watched their eyes glaze over? 👀

I was that person last week. A friend asked me what I do with AI image generation. I went into this whole thing about diffusion models, latent spaces, UNet architectures... Ten minutes later, she was scrolling Instagram.

I was frustrated. "Why doesn't she get it? It's so simple!"

And that's exactly the problem.

---

🧠 It's called **The Curse of Knowledge**

Once you know something, you literally cannot imagine what it's like not to know it.

Here's a famous experiment that proves it:

In 1990, Stanford researcher Elizabeth Newton asked people to tap out the rhythm of a song on a table. The "tappers" thought listeners would guess the song ~50% of the time.

The real number? **2.5%**.

Tappers heard the full song in their head — melody, lyrics, harmony.
Listeners heard "tap tap tap."

You're the tapper in your conversations. Every. Single. Day.

---

💼 **Where I see this most:**

• Engineers talking to non-technical colleagues
• Doctors talking to patients (⚕️ "it's a subchondral cyst" — WHAT?)
• Creators explaining their process to clients
• Parents explaining anything to teenagers
• Me, talking to my friend about AI

---

🔥 **3 signs you're cursed:**

1️⃣ People ask "what does that mean?" more than twice
2️⃣ You catch yourself saying "it's basically just..." (it's never "just")
3️⃣ The other person nods but you can SEE they've checked out

---

💡 **How I've learned to break it:**

**Use analogies, not jargon.**
Instead of "diffusion models denoise latent representations," try "it's like an artist who starts with a canvas full of static and slowly reveals a picture."

**Start with WHY.**
"Here's why this matters to you" — before ANY explanation, people need to know why they should care.

**Tell a story, don't deliver a lecture.**
Stories create hooks in the brain. Bullet points create boredom.

**Ask them to repeat it back.**
"Can you explain that in your own words?" — if they can't, I need to try again.

---

🎯 **My new rule:**

Before I explain anything, I ask myself three questions:
• What does THIS person already know?
• What's the ONE thing they actually want to know?
• Can I say it in two minutes or less?

If I can't answer all three, I'm not ready to explain.

---

The Curse of Knowledge isn't a flaw. It's proof that you've learned something deeply.

But real expertise isn't about how much you know. It's about how well you can help others understand.

Einstein probably put it best:
"If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough."
