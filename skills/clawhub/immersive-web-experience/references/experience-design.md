# Experience Design Principles

## Start from an experiential thesis

Define the experience in one sentence: **The visitor should feel _X_ while doing _Y_, because the interface behaves like _Z_.** Every scene and effect must reinforce that sentence.

## Design the arc

Use five functions, not mandatory page sections: invitation, orientation, escalation, revelation, and resolution/action. A short site may combine functions; a long site may repeat escalation and revelation.

Control three curves:

- information: what becomes understandable and when;
- intensity: density, speed, scale, contrast, and surprise;
- agency: when the visitor observes, explores, controls, or commits.

Provide calm between high-intensity moments. Constant spectacle flattens the experience.

## Agency and causality

Input should produce an intelligible response. Scroll may advance time, move a camera, reveal layers, or transform an object, but should not switch meanings arbitrarily. Preserve the visitor's sense of location and progress.

Use a signature mechanic once it has been taught, then vary it. One memorable rule is stronger than many unrelated tricks.

## Continuity

Connect scenes with at least one continuous property: object, trajectory, scale, crop, color, light, texture, grid, phrase, or camera direction. Transitions should answer “how did here become there?”

## Content before choreography

Prioritize the claim, evidence, and action of each scene. Motion controls their order and emphasis. If the scene becomes meaningless when motion is removed, provide an equivalent static reading order.

## Evaluation questions

- Can a visitor describe where they are and what changed?
- Is the main action discoverable without guessing?
- Does each scene earn its duration?
- Is the strongest moment reserved for the most important idea?
- Does the ending resolve the visual and narrative premise?
