# Ladder Engineering Reference

## 1. The injury picture

- US data (consumer safety surveys): ~500,000 ladder-related injuries treated
  in ERs per year; ~300 deaths. Falls **under 12 ft** dominate fatal outcomes
  for homeowners — height is a poor predictor of severity.
- Primary causes: wrong size (overreaching from top steps), wrong lean angle,
  overload beyond duty rating, and contact with overhead lines.

## 2. Stepladder size selection

Maximum standing height = ladder size minus ~2.5 ft (top cap + top step are
NOT standing surfaces). Comfortable maximum reach for a ~5'9" person with a
12-inch vertical reach ≈ platform height + 4 ft.

| Size (ft) | Max platform (ft) | Max touch-reach (ft) |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 2.0 | ~6 |
| 6 | 3.5 | ~7.5 |
| 8 | 5.0 | ~9 |
| 10 | 6.5 | ~10.5 |
| 12 | 8.0 | ~12 |
| 14 | 9.5 | ~13.5 |
| 16 | 11.0 | ~15 |
| 20 | 14.0 | ~18 |

Rules baked in:
- Never stand on the top two steps or the top cap.
- Never lean a closed stepladder against a wall (self-supporting design).
- Spreaders fully locked before the first step.

## 3. Extension ladder usable length

Sections overlap (the fly rides inside the base), so nominal ≠ usable:

| Nominal (ft) | Max usable (ft) |
|---|---|
| 16 | 13 |
| 20 | 17 |
| 24 | 21 |
| 28 | 25 |
| 32 | 29 |
| 36 | 33 |
| 40 | 37 |

Required span for wall work = support height. For roof access = roof edge
height + 3 ft of rail above the edge (handhold for step-off/return).

## 4. The 4:1 rule

Base distance from wall = support height ÷ 4, i.e. a 75.5° lean angle.

- Shallower (base too far out): rails slide out at the feet under load.
- Steeper (base too close): the ladder tips backward with you on it.

Field check without a tape: toes against the rails, arms straight ahead —
your palm should just brush the rungs.

## 5. Duty ratings (ANSI ASC M14 / OSHA 1910.23 labels)

| Type | Rating (lb) | Intended use |
|---|---|---|
| III  | 200 | Light household |
| II   | 225 | Medium duty light commercial |
| I    | 250 | Heavy duty industrial |
| IA   | 300 | Extra heavy — pro painter/roofer |
| IAA  | 350 | Special duty |

The rating covers **body + clothes + tools + materials carried**. A 220 lb
person hauling a 40 lb bundle of shingles on a Type III household ladder is
30% over the label.

## 6. Overhead lines

- Minimum approach: **10 ft for lines up to 50 kV**, plus 4 inches per kV
  above 50 kV (OSHA-style clearances; utilities may require more).
- Aluminum ladders: never within clearance of any line, period.
- Residential service drops (120/240 V) kill people every year — treat
  unknown voltage as high; wet wooden ladders and contaminated fiberglass
  also conduct.
- Remember the ladder while CARRYING it upright — a surprising share of
  line contacts happen during repositioning.
- If work intrudes on clearance, the utility re-routes/shields or the job
  waits. There is no "quick" exception.

## 7. Roof access sequence

1. 3 ft of rail above the roof edge, base at height÷4, fly locked.
2. Climb to just below the edge; inspect roof condition and footing.
3. Maintain 3 points of contact while transferring weight to the roof.
4. Grab the rail ABOVE the edge before fully committing to the roof.
5. Returning: hand on the rail above the edge BEFORE stepping back on the
   ladder — the ladder is not anchored against your downward momentum.
6. Tie-off or helper footing the base is standard practice.

## 8. Surface and weather gates

- Feet: both rails on firm, level, dry footing (levelers on slopes, never
  bricks-under-one-leg improvisation).
- Wind gusts, wet rungs, storm within ~10 miles, or steep/wet roof pitch →
  abort. Rent the right lift or hire it out.

## 9. Straight talk about risk

Most fatal homeowner ladder falls happen during "five-minute" jobs with a
borrowed ladder under clear skies. The arithmetic above removes the two
biggest setup unknowns (size and angle); it does not remove gravity. When
the output says marginal, the professional option is the cheap option.
