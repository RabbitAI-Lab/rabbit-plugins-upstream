# The Client Who Will Not Move

Stuckness is almost never a motivation deficit. It is a commitment that is too big, a goal that belongs to someone else, an ambivalence that has not been given room, or a competing commitment doing exactly its job. Diagnose which one before you push, because pushing the wrong one costs the relationship.

## Diagnose First

Run this order. Each step is cheap and rules out the step below it.

| Order | Check | Evidence | If yes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Is the commitment too big? | Fails the 20-minute worst-day test, or was missed twice | Halve it (SKILL.md Rule 5). Do not proceed until two halvings have failed |
| 2 | Is the deadline real? | "This week", "soon", no date | Put a date and a time on it; vague deadlines are not missed, they are never started |
| 3 | Is the observable missing? | Nobody can say whether it happened | Add the third field (Rule 6); unverifiable commitments decay silently |
| 4 | Is it theirs? | They cannot restate it in their own words without it changing shape | The goal test below |
| 5 | Is there ambivalence? | "Yes, but" on every option | Stop proposing, evoke both sides |
| 6 | Is there a competing commitment? | Effortful, consistent behavior *against* the goal | Immunity-to-change map |
| 7 | Is capacity the constraint? | Sleep, illness, caregiving, crisis, executive-function load | Coach the capacity, or pause the goal honestly |
| 8 | None of the above | The engagement itself may be wrong | Re-contract or end (`progress.md`) |

Skipping to step 5 or 6 because they are more interesting is the standard mistake. Most stuckness dies at steps 1-3, and those are the ones the coach can fix in ninety seconds.

## Ambivalence

"Yes, but" is not resistance to you. It is the client holding both sides of a genuine trade-off while only one side is allowed in the conversation.

- **Stop proposing.** Every new option gives the "but" something to attach to, and each round makes the client more fluent in why change is impossible — they hear themselves say it.
- **Ask for the whole case for staying**: "give me the full argument for changing nothing." Take it seriously, write it down, and do not counter any of it. Ambivalence resolves when the unspoken side has been said out loud completely.
- **Then the other side, in their words**: "what worries you about a year of exactly this?" This is evoking change talk (`questions.md`), and it works because they are making the argument, not you.
- **Double-sided reflection with the change side last**, then a scaling question on importance and confidence.
- **The decisional balance is four cells, not two**: benefits of changing, costs of changing, benefits of staying, costs of staying. The two cells people skip are *benefits of staying* and *costs of changing* — which is precisely where the ambivalence lives.
- Signal that it has resolved: the client starts generating options unprompted. Until then, planning is premature and any commitment set will be missed.

## Hidden Competing Commitments

Kegan and Lahey's immunity-to-change map, for the case where the client is working hard *against* their own stated goal. This is not sabotage; it is a second commitment being honored.

Four columns, built in this order:

1. **Improvement goal** — what they say they want, stated positively and in their words.
2. **Doing / not doing** — the observable behaviors that work against it. Behaviors only, no theories. "I rewrite the email four times", not "I'm a perfectionist".
3. **Competing commitment** — the worry underneath: "what is the worst that would happen if I did the opposite of column 2?" The answer, converted to a commitment: "I am also committed to never being seen as unprepared."
4. **Big assumption** — the belief that makes column 3 necessary: "if I am seen as unprepared, I will lose my standing here." Stated so it can be tested.

Then a test, not an insight: design the smallest safe experiment that checks the big assumption ("send one email after a single pass, to the lowest-stakes recipient, and observe what happens"). The map explains; only the experiment changes anything, and skipping to insight is why this tool has a reputation for being profound and inert.

Signature that this is the right tool: the client is *competent* and *effortful* everywhere else, and the failure is oddly consistent.

## Avoidance in Disguise

Each of these feels like work to the client, which is exactly what makes it durable.

| Disguise | Tell | Move |
|---|---|---|
| Research | Reading, comparing tools, "I want to understand it properly first" | "What would you do if you already knew enough?" Then a start time in the next 24 hours |
| Planning | Better plans, new systems, replanning after each miss | Ban the plan. One action, today, from the current plan |
| Preparation | Courses, certifications, "once I have X" | Ask what the qualification protects them from |
| Busyness | Full calendar, no progress on the goal | Ask what the busy protects them from; then cut one thing, permanently, in the session |
| Consulting others | Everyone's opinion, no decision | "Whose opinion would change your mind? What would they need to say?" |
| Perfecting | Version 7 of something unshipped | Define "good enough" as an observable, and ship the current version this week |
| Helping others | Their goal is on hold while they support everyone | Ask what happens to them if the people around them succeed and they have not moved |

The general shape: **the avoidance behavior is always adjacent to the goal and always virtuous.** That is why it survives — nobody, including the client, can call it laziness.

## Limiting Beliefs

A limiting belief arrives dressed as a fact: "I'm not a morning person", "I'm bad at sales", "nobody in my family finished anything".

- **Test it against evidence they supply.** "When was the last morning that worked? What was different?" One counter-example from their own history does more than any encouragement. A counter-example you supply is an argument, and arguments harden beliefs.
- **Separate identity from behavior.** "I'm disorganized" is unfalsifiable; "I did not write the deadline down" is fixable. Keep converting nouns into verbs until there is something to act on.
- **Check the belief's job.** Most limiting beliefs prevent a specific humiliation. "If you were suddenly good at sales, what would you have to do?" — the answer is often the thing being avoided, and the belief is the protection.
- **Do not attack the belief head-on.** Direct contradiction produces defense of the belief. The reliable route is a behavioral experiment small enough that the belief does not need defending.
- Record a belief in `## Patterns` only when it has appeared twice, with the count and the exact wording (`memory-template.md`). Their phrasing is the handle you will use six sessions later.

## Fear of Success

Progress that reverses close to the goal, self-sabotage at the finish, or "something always comes up" at 80%.

- Ask **what gets harder if this works**: more visibility, more responsibility, a relationship that changes, an identity that no longer fits, an excuse that stops being available.
- Ask **who loses**. A promotion can outgrow a peer group; a healthy body can change a partnership; a successful business can end a shared identity of struggle.
- Ask **what they would have to give up**. The complaint itself is often load-bearing — it has organized their conversations for years.
- The move is never reassurance. It is to make the cost explicit and let them choose it knowingly. A cost chosen out loud stops operating in the dark.

## Capacity, Not Character

Before any of the psychological readings, rule out the physical and situational ones. Coaching a capacity problem as if it were a motivation problem is the fastest way to add shame to an already hard situation.

- Sleep debt, illness, pain, medication changes, caregiving, a newborn, grief, a house move, a layoff, a legal process — any of these makes normal commitment sizing wrong, not the client.
- Executive-function constraints (ADHD, post-concussion, long-term burnout) break willpower-based structures specifically. Externalize instead: same-day starts, body doubling, environment design, no multi-week horizons (`niches.md`).
- The move is honest re-sizing, not encouragement: shrink the commitment to what is genuinely available, or pause the goal with a named return date in `## Due`. A paused goal with a date beats a live goal that is missed weekly.
- Persistent depletion with no situational cause is a Red Flags row in SKILL.md, not a coaching problem (`referral.md`).

## The Goal Was Never Theirs

The ownership test, in three moves:

1. **Restate test**: "tell me the goal the way you would tell your closest friend." If the shape changes, the version they were carrying was performed.
2. **Energy test**: watch what happens to their voice when they talk about it. A goal that produces flatness for three sessions is not a goal, whatever the plan says.
3. **Origin test**: "whose idea was this originally?" Inherited goals come from a partner, a parent, a manager, a peer comparison, or an old version of themselves who wanted something different.

Then the permission move: "what happens if you just don't?" Half of inherited goals dissolve on that question, and the relief is diagnostic. What is left after the dissolution is usually the real goal, and it is worth the session it cost (`goal-setting.md`).

## When Stuck Is the Right Answer

Not every stuck client should be unstuck. Sometimes the correct output of a session is a decision not to pursue the goal.

- The goal conflicts with a value they hold more strongly. Coaching them past it is a disservice with a good conversion rate.
- The timing is genuinely wrong and the honest move is a pause with a date, not a smaller commitment.
- The client is grieving, recovering, or in a crisis; the goal can wait and saying so is the intervention.
- Three re-contracts have produced three stalls: the engagement, not the client, is the problem (`progress.md`).

**When a diagnosis lands** — the competing commitment named, the belief with its counter-example, the goal identified as inherited — write it to `## Patterns` in `~/Clawic/data/coach/memory.md` with the date and the count, and any resized commitment to `## Commitments` with its verdict history intact (`memory-template.md`). An immunity map or a decisional balance the client will re-read goes to `artifacts/<kebab-name>.md` with its `## Boxes` line, in the same turn — these are the documents clients return to a year later.
