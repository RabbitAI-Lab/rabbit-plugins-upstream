# Questions — The Toolkit and When Each One Works

A question is a tool with a function, not a phrase to admire. Pick by what the conversation needs next: open the field, focus it, get specific, evoke the client's own argument for change, or force a decision. Using an evocative question when the need is specificity produces a beautiful, useless answer.

## Question Grammar

- **Under ~12 words, one clause** (SKILL.md Rule 3). If it needs a comma, it is two questions or an opinion in disguise.
- **Open with what or how.** "What" gets facts and options; "how" gets process. "Why" gets justification — the client defends the past instead of designing the future. The exception: "why does this matter to you?" points at motivation, not at blame, and is the one safe why.
- **No question that contains its answer.** "Have you considered talking to her?" is advice. The coaching form is "what options do you have?" and then, if genuinely absent, advice labeled as advice.
- **Singular, not stacked.** Two questions in one breath means they answer the easier one.
- **Present or future tense wherever possible.** Past tense is for evidence, not for exploration.
- **No adverbs of judgment.** "Why didn't you just…" and "couldn't you simply…" are criticism with a question mark; the client hears the adverb and nothing else.

## By Function

| Function | Use when | Questions |
|---|---|---|
| Open the field | The topic is narrow or defensive | "What do you actually want here?" · "What are we not talking about?" · "If this were easy, what would you do?" |
| Focus | Too many threads, no traction | "Of everything you have said, what matters most?" · "What is the one thing that makes the rest easier?" |
| Get specific | Answers are abstract | "When did that last happen, exactly?" · "What did you say, in your words?" · "How many times this month?" |
| Evoke their case for change | Ambivalence, "yes, but" | "What worries you about staying as you are?" · "What is the cost of another year of this?" |
| Surface the obstacle | Motion without progress | "What is stopping you?" then, on the first answer, "and what else?" three times |
| Access blocked wisdom | They know but will not say it | "If a friend brought you this, what would you tell them?" · "What do you already know that you are not acting on?" |
| Shift perspective | Stuck inside one frame | "What does your boss think is happening here?" · "How will this look in a year?" |
| Test ownership | The plan may be yours | "Say it back the way you would tell your partner." A changed shape means it was never theirs |
| Force decision | Endless analysis | "What will you do?" · "By when?" · "Will you? Yes or no." |
| Find resources | Low confidence | "When have you done something this hard before?" · "What is already working that we could do more of?" |
| Close | Consolidate | "What are you taking away?" · "What was most useful?" |
| Anything else | You do not know what is needed | "What is the question you were hoping I would not ask?" — high yield, use it once per engagement, not per session |

## Sequences That Work

Single questions rarely move anything; short sequences do. Each of these is three or four moves with a defined exit.

- **The "and what else" ladder.** "What is stopping you?" → "and what else?" → "and what else?" → "which of those is the real one?" The third answer is usually the honest one; the first is the socially acceptable obstacle. Stop at four — beyond that it becomes an interrogation.
- **Specificity ladder.** Abstraction → last instance → what was said → what they did → what they would do differently. Each rung is a single question. The rung where the client slows down is where the material is.
- **The goal behind the goal.** "What will having that give you?" repeated three times, then "and what would you do with *that*?" Ends in a value, which is what actually sustains effort (`goal-setting.md`).
- **Cost accounting.** "What has this already cost you?" → "what will it cost in a year?" → "what is it costing the people around you?" Use once, and only when the client has been circling for weeks; it is the sharpest tool here and it wears out.
- **Success cost.** "What gets harder if this works?" → "who loses something?" → "what would you have to give up?" This is the sequence for progress that reverses near the finish (`stuck.md`).
- **Miracle question (de Shazer), full form.** "Suppose tonight, while you sleep, the problem is solved — but nobody tells you. What is the first small thing you notice tomorrow morning?" Then: "who else notices?" and "what part of that already happens sometimes?" The last question is what turns it from fantasy into an action.

## Clean Language

Clean-language questions (Grove) add nothing of the coach's: they take the client's exact words and ask about them. Use when the client's metaphor is doing the work — "I'm carrying it", "I'm stuck in the mud", "there's a wall".

- "And what kind of *wall* is that wall?"
- "And is there anything else about that *wall*?"
- "And where is that *wall*?"
- "And what would you like to have happen?"
- "And what needs to happen for *that*?"

The discipline is not to translate. The moment you replace "wall" with "barrier" or "resistance", you are working on your metaphor and the client is now translating for you. Clean language is slow and looks strange in a transcript; it is the most reliable way to work with material the client cannot yet name literally.

## Motivational Interviewing

Miller and Rollnick's method for ambivalence, adapted to coaching. It is the correct tool whenever the client both wants and does not want the change — which is most of the time when a commitment has been missed repeatedly.

- **Four processes, in order**: engage (relationship), focus (agree the target), evoke (their arguments for change), plan (only after change talk outweighs sustain talk). Planning before change talk is the classic error and it produces the "yes, but" loop.
- **OARS as the moment-to-moment behavior**: Open questions, Affirmations of effort, Reflections of what they said, Summaries that gather the change talk together.
- **Change talk vs sustain talk.** Change talk sounds like desire, ability, reason, need ("I want", "I could", "because", "I have to"); sustain talk defends the status quo. Your job is to ask questions that produce more of the first and to reflect it back — not to argue against the second.
- **The righting reflex is the failure mode**: when the coach argues for change, the client is forced into the role of arguing against it, and people believe what they hear themselves say. If you are making the case, you have swapped roles.
- **Double-sided reflection** to hold both: "so working weekends is destroying your evenings, and it is the only way you currently feel safe about the deadline." Change side last — the second half is what they respond to.

## Questions That Backfire

| Question | What it produces | Use instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Why didn't you do it?" | Justification, then defensiveness | "What got in the way?" |
| "Don't you think you should…?" | Compliance or a fight | "What options do you see?" |
| "Have you tried X?" | Yes/no, plus the sense that you were not listening | "What have you tried?" first, always |
| "How does that make you feel?" | A label, rarely useful, often performed | "What happens in you when that comes up?" |
| "What's your biggest fear?" too early | A rehearsed answer; the real one needs trust | Get three sessions of evidence first |
| "Is that a goal or a wish?" | Feels like a test | "How will you know you have it?" |
| Any question with "just" in it | Signals the coach thinks it is easy | Remove the word and ask again |
| Rapid-fire questions with no pause | Interrogation; the client stops thinking and starts answering | One question, 5-7 seconds of silence |

## Reframes

A reframe changes the frame, not the facts, and it is offered as a question — never asserted.

- **Failure → data**: "if that attempt was an experiment, what did it tell you?"
- **Fixed trait → behavior**: "'I'm not a morning person' — what did you do on the mornings that worked?" A limiting belief stated as identity is dissolved by a counter-example the client supplies (never one you supply).
- **Problem → constraint**: "if you had to hit that with half the time, what would you drop?"
- **Should → choose**: "you said you should. Do you want to? What happens if you decide not to?" Half the stuck goals in coaching are inherited obligations that die the moment someone gives explicit permission to drop them.
- **Blame → agency**: "what part of this is inside your control?" — then coach only that part.
- A reframe the client rejects is information, not a failed technique: they are attached to the current frame for a reason worth asking about.

## Asking About Numbers

Coaching goes vague exactly where it matters, and a number is the cheapest cure.

- **Scaling, 0-10**, for confidence, energy, clarity, or importance. Then two follow-ups, always the same pair: "what makes it a 4 and not a 2?" (resources) and "what would 5 look like?" (the next action). Never ask for 10.
- **Confidence below 7 on a commitment predicts a miss.** Ask for the number at the close; if it is under 7, shrink the commitment until it is 7 or above. This one question, asked at the close, prevents more misses than any check-in afterwards.
- **Frequency and recency beat adjectives**: "often" becomes "how many times this month?"; "recently" becomes a date.
- **Time honesty**: "how long will that take?" then "and if it goes badly?" People plan against the good case; the second number is the one that lands in the calendar.

**When a question or a reframe visibly lands** — the client goes quiet, or says "huh" — record the exact wording in `## Patterns` in `~/Clawic/data/coach/memory.md` with the date (`memory-template.md`). What works for this person is the most reusable thing coaching produces, and it is the first thing forgotten.
