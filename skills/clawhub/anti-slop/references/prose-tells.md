# Prose Tells: The Full Reference

How to use this file: don't grep-and-delete. Read a section, then look at the actual draft with that pattern in mind. A single instance of almost anything here is fine — the tell is density (several from one tier clustering in a paragraph) or a structural pattern repeated more than once in a short piece. Fix by making the sentence more specific, not by swapping in a different vague word.

## 1. Vocabulary tiers

Words are tiered by how reliably they signal AI generation when they cluster. None of these are "banned" — they're all real words with legitimate uses. The question is always: is this the specific, plain word, or did I reach for the fancier one out of habit?

### Tier 1 — rare in unforced human writing; check every instance
delve, tapestry, underscore (verb), testament (to), boundaries (metaphorical, as in "pushing boundaries"), realm, multifaceted, multifarious, kaleidoscope (metaphorical), symphony (metaphorical), a myriad of, plethora, cornerstone, bedrock (metaphorical), linchpin, panacea, holistic, synergy, paradigm shift, game-changer, unlock (metaphorical, "unlock potential"), elevate (metaphorical), unleash (metaphorical), navigate (metaphorical, "navigate the complexities"), embark (metaphorical, "embark on a journey"), foster (metaphorical, "foster growth"), cultivate (metaphorical), harness (metaphorical), fabric (metaphorical, "fabric of society"), tapestry (again — it's that common), woven, intricate/intricacies, nuanced/nuance (as a filler noun rather than a real distinction), landscape (metaphorical, "the landscape of X"), ecosystem (metaphorical, outside actual biology/tech-stack contexts), journey (metaphorical, for anything other than literal travel).

### Tier 2 — fine occasionally, suspicious in clusters (the "promotional register")
robust, seamless, seamlessly, vibrant, dynamic, comprehensive, streamline/streamlined, leverage (verb), utilize (just say "use"), facilitate, optimal, optimize, pivotal, crucial, essential, vital, invaluable, indispensable, transformative, revolutionize/revolutionary, groundbreaking, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, innovative, empower/empowering, meticulous/meticulously, profound/profoundly, staggering, unprecedented, ever-evolving, ever-changing, ever-present, thought-provoking, awe-inspiring, breathtaking (outside literal physical description), captivating, compelling (as a filler adjective rather than an argued claim), rich (metaphorical, "rich history/tapestry"), diverse (as filler rather than a specific claim), tailored, bespoke (outside actual custom manufacturing), curated (outside actual curation), curate as a verb for ordinary selection.

### Tier 3 — ordinary words; only worth noting when they cluster
additionally, moreover, furthermore, notably, importantly, ultimately, fundamentally, essentially, effectively (as filler), significantly (without a number attached), various, numerous, several, key (as in "key factor," overused as a default adjective), overall, in general, broadly, largely, arguably (as a hedge rather than a real qualifier), certainly, indeed, undoubtedly, clearly (asserting rather than showing).

### Verbs that inflate a plain sentence
"serves as," "stands as," "represents," "boasts," "features," "offers," "acts as" — used where a plain "is," "are," or "has" would do the same job with less ceremony.
- Inflated: "The museum serves as a testament to the city's industrial past."
- Plain: "The museum documents the city's industrial past."

## 2. Phrase-level tells

Phrases are a stronger signal than individual words — a whole memorized unit is harder to justify as coincidence than one adjective.

### Generic openers
- "In today's fast-paced world..."
- "In the ever-evolving landscape of X..."
- "Since the dawn of time, humans have..."
- "Imagine a world where..."
- "In this article/post, we will explore..." (when the title already said what it's about)
- "Let's dive into..." / "Let's delve into..." / "Let's break this down..."

Fix: open with the first real fact, claim, or example. If the piece is about a specific event, product, or argument, start there.

### Generic closers
- "In conclusion..." (rarely needed — the reader can see it's the last paragraph)
- "Ultimately, the choice is yours."
- "Only time will tell."
- "As we move forward..."
- "The possibilities are endless."
- "It's clear that X will only continue to grow/evolve/matter."

Fix: end on the actual conclusion, a concrete next step, or just stop. Not every piece needs a moral.

### Hedge phrases (throat-clearing before the actual point)
- "It is important to note that..."
- "It is worth mentioning that..."
- "It should be noted that..."
- "It is essential to understand that..."
- "One must consider..."
- "One should remember that..."

Fix: delete the hedge and state the claim directly. "It is important to note that the deadline moved" → "The deadline moved."

### Transition padding
- "That being said..."
- "With that in mind..."
- "Having said that..."
- "At the end of the day..."

These aren't always wrong, but a piece that uses more than one is usually padding rhythm rather than logic. Check whether the sentence works with the transition deleted — it almost always does.

### Fake engagement / infomercial transitions
- "The catch?"
- "The kicker?"
- "Here's the thing:"
- "But here's what's interesting..."
- "Sound familiar?"

These mimic spoken rhetorical pauses but read as a script when stacked. Fine once in a genuinely conversational piece; a tell when every section has one.

## 3. Structural patterns

These matter more than any single word, because they're patterns of *thought*, not vocabulary choices — swapping synonyms doesn't fix them.

### "It's not just X, it's Y" (the single strongest tell)
This construction tries to look balanced and thoughtful by setting up a contrast and then resolving it. It shows up as "It's not just a museum, it's a community hub," "Not only does this save time, it also builds trust," "This isn't a bug, it's a feature."

Fix: state the second half directly, or combine the two facts without the theatrical setup.
- Before: "It's not just a scheduling tool, it's a way to reclaim your time."
- After: "It schedules meetings and blocks out focus time automatically."

### Reflexive rule of three
The rule of three is a real rhetorical device ("Friends, Romans, countrymen"; "blood, sweat, and tears") — it works when the three items are true, distinct, and earned. It becomes a tell when applied as a rhythm reflex to things that don't actually have three parts: "efficient, effective, and reliable," "simple, powerful, and transformative," "save time, reduce costs, and increase ROI." If you notice you've written three adjectives or three benefits reflexively, check whether all three are doing real work — often one is redundant filler added purely for the cadence.

### Symmetrical, over-balanced paragraphs
AI-generated pieces often have suspiciously uniform paragraph lengths and a "both sides, therefore middle ground" shape even on questions that don't have a tidy middle. Real writing has uneven rhythm — some short punchy sentences, some long ones, some one-sentence paragraphs. If every paragraph in a piece is 3-4 sentences of similar length, vary it.

### Hedging that never commits
A string of qualifiers ("it could be argued," "some might say," "in many cases," "it depends") stacked so densely that the piece never actually takes a position. If the user or the content calls for a view, take one — hedge only where there's real uncertainty, and say what the uncertainty specifically is rather than gesturing at it.

### The "X is more than just Y" family
Sibling of the "not just" pattern: "Success is more than just talent," "A home is more than just four walls." Same fix — say the actual point without the setup-and-reveal structure.

## 4. Sycophancy and validation reflexes

Documented in RLHF-trained models: a strong tendency to validate before answering, regardless of whether validation is warranted.

- "Great question!"
- "Absolutely!" / "Absolutely right!"
- "You raise a really interesting point."
- "That's a really insightful observation."
- "I completely understand your frustration." (stated generically, not in response to anything specific the person actually said)
- A compliment on the premise of a question before any engagement with its content.

Fix: if a question is genuinely sharp, that should be visible in the quality of the answer, not announced before it. Skip straight to the substance. This applies double in chat responses — a validating opener before a one-sentence factual answer is one of the most-reported complaints about over-tuned assistants.

## 5. Formatting excess

Bullets, bold, headers, and emoji are tools, not defaults. Research on preference-model training (*From Lists to Emojis: How Format Bias Affects Model Alignment*, 2024) found that human raters and reward models are measurably biased toward these formats independent of content quality — which is part of why chat models reach for them by default. That doesn't make them wrong; it means they should be a deliberate choice about *this* content, not a reflex.

Warning signs:
- A short, conversational answer restructured into a header + three bullets + a "Key Takeaway" box.
- Every bullet point bolded at the start regardless of whether it's naming a category or just continuing a sentence.
- Headers over sections that are one or two sentences long.
- Horizontal rules or emoji used purely as decoration between sections that already have headers.
- Nested bullets used to simulate structure in content that's actually a single flowing argument.

Fix: default to prose. Reach for a list only when the content is genuinely enumerable (steps in strict order, parallel items being compared, a reference table) and a list is easier to scan than a paragraph would be. If you can read the "bullets" aloud as a natural sentence with "and" between them, it probably should have been a sentence.

Em dashes: not a tell on their own — used correctly they're a clean way to set off an aside. A paragraph with three or more starts to read as a rhythmic tic. If a passage has more than one or two, convert some to commas or periods.

## 6. Numbers and specificity tells

AI-generated text sometimes handles numbers oddly: spelling out round numbers where a human would just write the digit ("seven tools" vs. "7 tools"), or reaching for a suspiciously precise-sounding but unverifiable statistic ("73% of users report...") without a source. Two checks:
- If you wrote a specific statistic, do you actually know where it came from? If not, either verify it or replace it with a real, sourced number, a range, or a qualitative claim you can actually stand behind.
- Real specificity (a name, an exact date, a dollar figure, a direct quote) is one of the best anti-slop tools there is — it's much harder to fake than vocabulary, and it's usually what's missing when a passage feels generic.

## 7. Before/after gallery

**Generic → specific**
- Before: "Our platform leverages cutting-edge AI to deliver a seamless, comprehensive solution."
- After: "Our platform uses a fine-tuned model to auto-categorize tickets in under two seconds."

**Hedge-stacked → direct**
- Before: "It is important to note that, in many cases, the results may vary depending on several factors."
- After: "Results vary by device — older phones saw a smaller improvement."

**Not-just-but → plain**
- Before: "This isn't just a pricing change, it's a fundamental shift in how we think about value."
- After: "We're moving from per-seat pricing to usage-based pricing."

**Rule-of-three reflex → earned specifics**
- Before: "The new dashboard is fast, intuitive, and powerful."
- After: "The new dashboard loads in under a second and puts the three metrics people check most on the front screen."

**Sycophantic opener → straight answer**
- Before: "Great question! Daylight saving time is actually a really interesting topic to dig into."
- After: "Daylight saving time shifts clocks forward an hour in spring to align daylight hours with typical waking hours."

**Over-formatted → prose**
- Before: A header, three bold-lead bullets, and a "Key Takeaway" box, all for a one-fact answer.
- After: One or two sentences that state the fact plainly.
