# Policy, politics and contested arguments

Map verified observations, value premises, hypotheses, forecasts, conditions, causal mechanisms, recommended actions and uncertainty **as different kinds of propositions**. Apply the same evidentiary standards across political positions. Do not select a political side, attribute hidden motives, rank candidates or engineer voter manipulation.

## Four editorial inspections

1. **Claim class:** is the sentence observation, interpretation, prediction, ethical commitment, proposed policy, or metaphor? Has the next sentence quietly transformed it into another class?
2. **Inference mechanism:** what would make the conclusion follow? Check correlation-versus-causation, denominator changes, selection bias, missing comparison group, anecdote promoted to population evidence and alternative explanations.
3. **Counterargument:** if a reader has one predictable, strongest objection, does the author address it accurately? Don't invent a spokesperson's argument or require equal rhetorical space for claims unsupported by evidence.
4. **Commitment:** does the opening ask for action stronger than the analysis warrants, and does the closing return to the actual author intention? Don't rewrite the policy goal to a different one in order to create coherence.

## Paired decisions

**Edit — non sequitur without choosing policy**
- Draft: “We don't know whether fares or bus reliability matter more. Therefore, remove all fares tomorrow.”
- If the brief is pilot-first: revise to “That uncertainty is why the city should test reliability before committing to a permanent fare change.”
- If the brief is free-fares-now: propose a pilot compatible with that position or ask which position is actually intended. Never choose secretly.

**Edit — forecast as fact**
- “The plan will save £20m” when the source is a model with assumptions: “The model projects £20m in savings under [verified assumptions].” If assumptions were not supplied, ask; do not invent them.

**Leave — moral claim**
- “No one should have to worry about getting home safely after a late shift.” As an ethical commitment, it does not require a statistical citation. Assess its implications and downstream proposed remedy separately.

**Leave — strong, fairly sourced criticism**
- An argument can be sharply critical and still accurate. Don't flatten force merely to sound “balanced”; intervene in factual overreach or omitted essential context, not intensity alone.

**Edit — data scope mismatch**
- “This survey of 400 voters proves all citizens demand the change.” Replace with a source-faithful population/method description; keep sampling uncertainties visible. Do not calculate representativeness without a method.

## Contextual question

At the exact original passage: “What premise does the next policy recommendation depend on, and has the reader been given evidence or only a value judgment?” Link to a specific proposed edit. Do not quiz the author on which political view to hold.
