# Modal Verbs in Research Writing

> Extracted from *Science Research Writing* (Glasman-Deal) **Unit 4.5 — Language and Writing Skills: Modal Verbs**.
> 6 functional categories. Use to diagnose whether the modal verb matches the writer's intended epistemic stance.

⚠️ **A single modal verb can carry more than one meaning.** Context disambiguates. Common cases:
- "should" = EXPECTED (likely) OR ADVISABLE (recommended)
- "can" = ABLE (capability) OR POSSIBLE (option/occasionally) — risky, often ambiguous
- "must" = OBVIOUS (no other explanation) OR NECESSARY (essential)

---

## 1. ABLE (capability)

| Tense | Form | Example | Paraphrase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present Simple | **can** | The model **can** predict a wide range of data. | The model is able to predict ... |
| Present Simple neg. | **cannot** | This system **cannot** identify other pathogenic bacteria. | This system is not able to identify ... |
| Past Simple | **could** | The algorithm **could** convert unstructured data. | The algorithm was able to convert ... |
| Past Simple (perfect) | **could have** | With a larger sample, the method **could have** identified more infections. | ... would have been able to identify ... |
| Past Simple neg. | **could not** | The robot **could not** react dynamically. | The robot was not able to react ... |
| Past Simple neg. (perfect) | **could not have** | Without this data, we **could not have** detected the contamination. | ... would not have been able to detect ... |

> **Tips:**
> - If unsure between can / be able to, **use be able to** — safer, less likely to be misinterpreted.
> - For future tense: use **will be able to**.
> - "could" means possible as well as able — consider whether using "could" makes the meaning ambiguous.
> - "could" can refer to conditional / hypothetical ability: "If uptake was 100%, this **could** prevent infection."
> - "be capable of" is an alternative to can / could in some contexts.

## 2. POSSIBLE / OPTIONAL

| Tense | Form | Example | Paraphrase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present Simple | **may / might / could** | These interactions **may / could / might** be the same for each species. | It is possible that these interactions are the same ... |
| Present Simple neg. | **may not / might not** (not could not, not cannot) | These interactions **may not / might not** be the same. | It is possible that these interactions are not the same ... |
| Past Simple (perfect) | **may have / might have / could have** (not can have) | This response **may have / might have / could have** caused the reduction. | It is possible that this response caused the reduction ... |
| Past Simple neg. (perfect) | **may not have / might not have** (not could not have, not cannot have) | This response **may not have / might not have** caused the reduction. | It is possible that this response did not cause the reduction ... |

> **Tips:**
> - "may / might / could" in Present Simple can refer to future possibilities OR current/permanent possibilities.
> - "might" is slightly weaker than "may" and less common in research writing.
> - **Don't use may / might / could just to stay safe.** If results support an interpretation, upgrade to "highly likely / probable / almost certain".
> - "can" is **ambiguous and risky** in this category. "Particle formation can occur in the boundary layer" could mean any of: (a) may possibly occur, (b) is able to occur, (c) sometimes occurs. Where ambiguous, replace with one of those explicit forms.
> - "can" is fine for **options/choices**: "An X or a Y **can** be used" = It is possible to use either.
> - "can not" (two words) does NOT mean the same as "cannot" (one word). "can not" = possibly not (like may not). "cannot" = impossible.
> - "could not / cannot have / could not have" mean **impossible** (NOT possible/negative):
>   - "This **cannot** be due to a change in pressure." (impossible)
>   - "This **could not** be due to a change in pressure." (impossible)

## 3. EXPECTED / LIKELY / PROBABLE

| Tense | Form | Example | Paraphrase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present Simple | **should** | The ratio **should** remain constant if expansion is uniform. | The ratio is expected to / is likely to / will probably remain constant ... |
| Present Simple neg. | **should not** | The ratio **should not** change unless expansion changes. | The ratio is not expected to / is not likely to / will probably not change ... |
| Past Simple (perfect) | **should have** | Our data suggest that the decrease **should have** occurred during the first year. | ... was expected to occur during the first year. |
| Past Simple neg. (perfect) | **should not have** | Our data suggest that corrosion **should not have** occurred for at least two years. | ... was not expected to occur for at least two years. |

> **Tips:**
> - "should have" often refers to something that **didn't** happen.
> - "should not have" often refers to something that **did** happen.
> - "was likely / probable" and "was not likely / probable" refer to the **level of certainty** about a past event, not whether it did or didn't happen.
> - "ought to" = "should" but becoming less common in science writing.

---

## 4. OBVIOUS / IMPOSSIBLE

(Use when you mean no other explanation is possible.)

| Tense | Form | Example | Paraphrase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present Simple | **must / have to** | This **must** be the result of direct collision. | It is obvious that this is the result ... |
| Present Simple neg. | **cannot** | This **cannot** be a result of direct collision. | It is impossible that this is the result ... |
| Past Simple (perfect) | **must have** | This effect **must have** been due to the increased rate. | It is obvious that this effect was due to ... |
| Past Simple neg. (perfect) | **cannot have / could not have** | This effect **cannot have / could not have** been due to the increased rate. | It is impossible that this effect was due to ... |

> **Tips:**
> - "must not" means **not allowed**, it does NOT mean impossible.
> - "have to" is mostly used in spoken communication.
> - "Being certain that something is true and knowing that it is true are not the same" — "must" / "must have" often communicate an absence of proof (you cannot empirically verify, so you commit to the obvious explanation).

---

## 5. ADVISABLE / RECOMMENDED

| Tense | Form | Example | Paraphrase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present Simple | **should** | The tubes **should** be centrifuged before the experiment. | It is advisable to centrifuge the tubes ... |
| Present Simple neg. | **should not** | The tubes **should not** be centrifuged before the experiment. | It is not advisable to centrifuge the tubes ... |
| Past Simple (perfect) | **should have** | We later realised the samples **should have** been diluted. | ... it would have been advisable / a good idea to dilute the samples ... |
| Past Simple neg. (perfect) | **should not have** | The samples **should not have** been diluted with water. | ... it was not advisable / not a good idea to dilute the samples ... |

> **Tip:** "should" is ambiguous between this category and category 3 (EXPECTED). Context must disambiguate.

---

## 6. NECESSARY / ESSENTIAL

| Tense | Form | Example | Paraphrase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present Simple | **must / need to / have to** | The tubes **must / need to / have to** be centrifuged. | It is necessary to centrifuge the tubes ... |
| Present Simple neg. | **do not need to / do not have to / need not** | The tubes **do not need to / do not have to / need not** be centrifuged. | It is not necessary to centrifuge the tubes ... |
| Past Simple | **needed to / had to** | We found that the samples **needed to / had to** be diluted. | ... it was necessary to dilute the samples ... |
| Past Simple neg. | **did not need to / need not have / did not have to** | We found that the samples **did not need to** be diluted. | ... it was not necessary to dilute the samples ... |

> **Tips:**
> - "must not" = not allowed, it does NOT mean not necessary.
> - "did not need to" usually = it wasn't necessary, AND we didn't do it.
> - "need not have" usually = it wasn't necessary, BUT we did do it.
> - "did not have to" is less common in formal science research writing.

---

## Diagnostic checklist

- [ ] No modal stacking: "This **may can** cause..." (pick one)
- [ ] "must" used only for OBVIOUS / no-other-explanation; not for possibility
- [ ] "can" not used ambiguously where "may / might / could" or "is able to" is meant
- [ ] "could not" / "cannot" used only for IMPOSSIBLE; "may not" / "might not" for possible-negative
- [ ] "should" disambiguated by context (likely vs advisable)
- [ ] "should have" / "should not have" form agrees with the implied did/didn't-happen
- [ ] No "ought to" (becoming less common in science writing)
